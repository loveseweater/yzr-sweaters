ISF 10+2 for US Knitwear Imports: What Must Be Filed Before the Vessel Loads

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

ISF 10+2 is a US customs filing for ocean cargo that has to reach CBP before the goods are loaded onto the vessel in China — as a rule no later than 24 hours before lading. The importer supplies ten data elements, the carrier supplies two. Penalties run to thousands of dollars per violation and CBP can hold the container with a do-not-load order, so the practical deadline for a buyer is days earlier than the sailing date: your broker needs the final factory documents before the container is stuffed, not after the ship leaves.

We mostly hear about ISF when something has already gone wrong — a broker chasing a packing list the day before cut-off, or a buyer surprised by a penalty that had nothing to do with the sweaters. It is one of those steps that sits entirely on the paperwork side of an order, which is why it gets left until last. If you import knitwear into the US by sea, it is worth knowing which ten fields you are responsible for and where each one comes from, because almost all of them originate at the factory.

What the "10" and the "2" actually are

The rule asks for advance cargo information so CBP can assess a container before it starts moving. Ten elements come from the importer and two from the carrier. In plain terms, the importer's ten are:

  • Seller — the party selling the goods, with name and address. For a direct factory order this is the Dongguan manufacturer or its trading entity, exactly as it appears on the commercial invoice.
  • Buyer — the party buying the goods, again with a full address. Get this consistent with the purchase order; a mismatch between buyer on the PO and buyer on the invoice is a common avoidable query.
  • Importer of record number — your IRS/EIN or CBP-assigned number, or the FTZ applicant ID if that applies. If you are unsure who is the importer of record under your terms, our notes on Incoterms cover why this is not a trivial question.
  • Consignee number(s) — the party who will actually receive the cargo at destination.
  • Manufacturer (or supplier) — the actual factory name and address, from which the manufacturer ID is built. This is where trading-company orders get complicated: the MID should describe the knitting and finishing plant, not the office that answered your email.
  • Ship-to party — the US address the goods are ultimately delivered to, which may be a 3PL or a distribution centre rather than your own office.
  • Country of origin — China for our production, but state it per line if a mixed order includes accessories made elsewhere.
  • HTSUS number — normally to six digits. Sweaters and pullovers sit in 6110, but a mixed knitwear container can also carry cardigans, dresses and small accessories, so the filing may need several codes. Our HS code guide explains why the classification is worth getting right on both sides of the ocean.
  • Container stuffing location — where the cartons were loaded into the container, which for factory-loaded FCL is usually the plant or a nominated warehouse.
  • Consolidator / stuffer — the party that physically stuffed the container, with name and address.

The carrier's two are the vessel stow plan and container status messages. You do not supply those, but you do supply almost everything else — and eight of the ten are things only your supplier can confirm accurately.

Why knitwear orders get caught out

The sweater itself is not the problem. The problem is the shape of a typical knitwear order: many styles, many colours, many sizes, packed into cartons with a breakdown that only exists in a spreadsheet the factory finalises at the end of the run. Three situations we see a lot:

  • The final packing list arrives too late. A factory that is still linking and pressing on Tuesday cannot give you a truthful carton-by-carton breakdown on Monday. If your broker filed from a provisional packing list and the final one differs, the filing needs updating. Ask for the draft packing list at 80% completion, not at stuffing.
  • Mixed styles mean mixed classifications. A single 40HQ of a winter program can contain 6110 pullovers, 6110 cardigans, 6104 knitted dresses and 6117 scarves. Filing one HTS code for the whole container is the kind of shortcut that generates a query later. See the shipping document set for how the invoice, packing list and B/L have to agree.
  • The vessel changes after filing. Rolled containers and blank sailings in peak season mean a booking moved to a different vessel after the ISF was filed. The filing has to be updated to match the actual loading. Our piece on peak-season bookings covers why this is normal rather than exceptional in autumn.

LCL shipments are a little different: the consolidator or NVOCC generally files for the consolidated container, but they still build that filing from the data you give them, so accuracy stays your problem. This is one of the reasons we suggest buyers read our guide to choosing a freight forwarder before deciding who controls the chain.

Who files, and what it costs

The legal responsibility sits with the importer of record — you — even though a licensed customs broker almost always transmits the filing on your behalf. Brokers commonly charge a modest per-filing fee on top of their entry work; the expensive part is never the fee, it is a late or inaccurate filing. CBP can issue a do-not-load instruction that keeps the container off the vessel, and civil penalties for violations run into thousands of dollars per occurrence. Confirm current amounts with your broker rather than relying on a figure in a blog post; the principle is that a documentation error is far more expensive than the hour spent checking it.

Bonding is a separate question. A continuous bond normally covers the ISF obligation; a single-entry importer may need a dedicated ISF bond. Our guide to US customs bonds walks through the single-entry versus continuous decision, and the customs clearance walkthrough picks up the process from arrival onwards.

The data pack we send buyers before loading

Because the filing is built from supplier paperwork, we treat the pre-loading document pack as part of production, not as an afterthought. For an FCL order it contains the commercial invoice in final form, the packing list with carton counts and the style/colour/size breakdown, the manufacturer's legal name and address for the MID, the HS classification support for every style in the shipment, the stuffing location, and — as soon as the carrier issues them — the container and seal numbers. We send the pack several days ahead of cut-off so a broker can query anything odd while there is still time to fix it.

Two habits make this painless. First, forward the whole pack to your broker the same day you receive it, not just the invoice. Second, tell us immediately if the consignee, ship-to address or vessel changes; an amended ISF is cheap, an unnoticed one is not. If you want the stuffing itself watched, our notes on container loading supervision describe what a third party checks at the gate.

What to put in the purchase order

Most ISF disputes are really contract disputes about timing. Four lines are usually enough:

  • A document deadline stated relative to the sailing — for example a draft packing list at 80% production and a final pack at least five working days before cut-off.
  • One named owner of the data on each side: who at the factory signs off the packing list, who at your end transmits it to the broker.
  • A re-file trigger — any change of vessel, consignee, stuffing location or classification gets reported within a set number of hours.
  • Who carries the cost of a filing error attributable to incorrect supplier documents. A supplier confident in its paperwork will agree to this; one that hedges is telling you something useful.

Frequently asked questions

When exactly does the ISF have to be filed?

As a rule, no later than 24 hours before the cargo is loaded aboard the vessel at the foreign port, and in practice earlier because carriers set their own documentation cut-offs. Treat the working deadline as several days before sailing: your broker cannot file accurately until the factory has finalised the packing list and the manufacturer details.

Does my factory file the ISF for me?

No. The importer of record is responsible and a licensed customs broker normally transmits it. What the factory does is supply most of the underlying data — legal seller name, manufacturer address, origin, HTS codes, stuffing location and the final packing list — so a supplier with sloppy paperwork will cause problems even if your broker is excellent.

What happens if the vessel or the packing list changes after filing?

The filing must be updated to reflect the actual shipment. Tell your broker as soon as you know: a rolled container, a blank sailing, a changed consignee or a revised carton breakdown all need an amended ISF. An update sent in good time is routine; an unamended filing discovered at arrival is where penalties and do-not-load holds come from.
