Sweater Product Page Copy: Answering the Questions That Cause Returns

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Quick answer

Most sweater returns are not caused by a defective garment — they are caused by a customer expectation the page never corrected. The four questions shoppers most often get wrong are how heavy it is, how warm it is, whether it will pill, and how it should be washed. Each of those has a factual answer that comes from the factory, and putting them on the page is cheaper than processing the return.

A sweater product page usually leads with adjectives: soft, cosy, luxurious, timeless. Those words cost nothing and communicate little, because every competing page uses them. What a shopper actually wants to know is whether the garment will be thick enough for a cold commute, whether it will look worn after three washes, and whether the armhole will feel tight. Those are answerable questions, and answering them is the most direct way to lower returns.

Which questions cause the most knitwear returns?

Four themes dominate, and each is a communication failure rather than a product failure.

"It is thinner than I expected." A customer who pictured a chunky winter sweater and received a fine-gauge one will return it as "not as described", even though the garment is exactly as specified. Weight is invisible in a photograph, and almost no product page states it.

"It is not warm enough." Related but distinct. Warmth comes from trapped air, which depends on gauge, fibre loft and fabric weight together, and a shopper cannot infer it from a photo. Our notes on how knitwear keeps you warm explain the mechanism.

"It bobbled after two wears." Pilling is expected behaviour in some yarns and a defect in others, and the difference is knowable before the customer buys. A page that says nothing sets the expectation at zero pilling, which no wool knit will meet.

"I washed it and it shrank." Usually a care-instruction problem. If the garment is hand wash only and the page does not say so prominently, the customer will machine wash it and hold the brand responsible. Our notes on shrinkage cover what drives the movement.

What factual detail should the page carry?

The information below is unglamorous and it is exactly what reduces returns, because it converts a subjective impression into a checkable fact.

  • Fibre composition with percentages. Not "merino blend" but the actual split. A shopper who knows they are buying 30 percent wool can calibrate their expectation, and our notes on sweater yarn explain why the blend matters more than the headline fibre.
  • Fabric weight in GSM or grams per garment. The single most useful number for calibrating thickness. Our notes on fabric weight and GSM explain how the figure is measured and what ranges mean in practice.
  • Gauge, for a shopper who understands it, and a plain-language equivalent for one who does not — "fine 12-gauge knit" plus "lightweight, layer-friendly". Our notes on knit gauge cover the terminology.
  • Pilling expectation, stated honestly. "This wool blend may pill lightly with wear; a fabric comb removes it" sets a realistic expectation and pre-empts the complaint.
  • Care instructions up front, not buried at the bottom. Hand wash, dry flat, do not tumble — and why. Our notes on caring for knitwear cover the practical routine.
  • Fit and finished measurements, which our notes on online size guides cover in their own right.
  • Hand feel, described concretely. "Soft against the neck, no prickle" is useful; "luxuriously soft" is not. Our notes on hand feel and micron explain what drives the difference.

How should the copy be structured?

Three blocks, in this order, because shoppers scan rather than read.

First, one sentence of what it is and who it is for. "A fine merino crew neck for layering under a coat — light enough to wear indoors." That single line does more expectation-setting than a paragraph of adjectives.

Second, a short factual block. Composition, weight, gauge, care, fit. Bulleted, scannable, no marketing language. This is the block that answers the return-causing questions.

Third, the descriptive paragraph. How it feels, how it drapes, what it pairs with. By this point the shopper has the facts and the prose is adding colour rather than carrying the load.

Most pages invert this, leading with atmosphere and burying the facts. That order assumes the shopper will read to the end, and most will not.

Why does this matter for a supplier relationship?

Because the facts a product page needs are manufacturing facts, and a brand cannot write them without its supplier.

Fabric weight, gauge, fibre percentages, pilling behaviour, shrinkage and care instructions all originate at the factory and are recorded in the specification. A brand that has to guess will either omit the detail or get it wrong, and a wrong care instruction is both a return risk and, in some markets, a compliance problem — the subject of our notes on care labelling rules.

The practical implication is that the product page should be written from the tech pack and the approved sample, not from the design brief. Our notes on building a tech pack explain where each figure is recorded, and the bill of materials is where the component specification sits.

What should a brand avoid saying?

Unverifiable superlatives. "The softest sweater you will ever own" is a claim a customer can test and disprove, and the resulting disappointment is a return reason.

Environmental claims without evidence. A vague "sustainable" invites scrutiny the product may not survive. Our notes on EU sustainability rules cover the direction of travel on substantiating claims.

Care instructions that contradict the label. If the page says machine washable and the label says hand wash, the customer will follow the page and the brand will own the outcome.

Fit descriptions that assume a body type. "Flattering on everyone" is both unverifiable and exclusionary. Describing the actual fit — where the hem sits, how much ease — is both more useful and more honest.

How do you check the page is working?

  • Read return reasons by style and map them to the page. A cluster of "thinner than expected" on one style is a missing weight figure, not a bad garment.
  • Look at pre-purchase questions. If customers repeatedly ask the same question before buying, that answer belongs on the page. Our notes on reducing consumer returns cover the wider set of causes.
  • Compare return rates between styles with and without a full factual block. The difference is usually visible within a season.
  • Ask the factory whether the stated care matches the tested behaviour. A page is only accurate if the garment actually behaves as described, and the test to confirm it is covered in our notes on lab testing.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most important fact to put on a sweater product page?

Fabric weight, expressed in GSM or grams per garment. Thickness is the expectation shoppers most often get wrong from a photograph, and it is the cause of most "not as described" returns. Composition and gauge support it, but weight alone corrects the most common misreading.

Should I mention pilling on the product page?

Yes, honestly. Every wool knit pills to some degree, and a page that implies otherwise sets an expectation the garment cannot meet. Saying that light pilling is normal and easily removed with a fabric comb prevents a complaint and reads as confidence rather than as a warning.

Where do I get the facts for the product page?

From the tech pack, the approved sample and the factory's test data — not from the design brief. Weight, gauge, composition, pilling behaviour and tested care instructions are all manufacturing facts, so the supplier has to supply them. A brand that writes a page without them is guessing.
