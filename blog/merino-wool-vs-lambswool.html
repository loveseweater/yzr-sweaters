Merino Wool vs Lambswool: What Sweater Buyers Need to Know

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Fibre choice is the single biggest cost decision in a sweater program, and it is the one buyers most often get wrong. Put a merino crewneck and a lambswool cardigan side by side and, to an untrained eye, both are "wool sweaters". To your customer's hand, and to your margin, they are two different products.

First, what each wool actually is

Lambswool is the first clip, sheared from a young lamb. It is not a breed — lambswool can come from many sheep breeds, which is why its character varies so much from one supplier's yarn to the next. As a first clip, the fibre is naturally soft and springy, with a lofty hand.

Merino, by contrast, refers to wool from Merino sheep, a breed developed specifically for fineness. Merino wool is graded in microns — the diameter of the fibre — and most apparel-grade merino sits in the high teens to low twenties. The lower the micron count, the finer and softer the yarn, and the higher the price. This is why two "100% merino" sweaters can feel and cost completely differently.

Hand feel: where merino pulls ahead

Finer fibres bend more easily against skin, which is why fine merino can be worn next to the skin without the prickling of older-style wool knits. Lambswool is soft for a wool, but because it is defined by the age of the sheep rather than the breed, its comfort depends on the source. Some lambswool yarns feel nearly as smooth as mid-grade merino; others read noticeably crisper.

One option worth knowing: certain first-clip merino yarns — often sold under names like Geelong — combine first-clip softness with merino fineness at a friendlier price than top-grade merino. Asking "which lambswool or merino base?" is a more useful brief than just "lambswool or merino?".

Warmth and seasonality

Both fibres are genuinely warm, but they suit different products.

  • Lambswool has more loft. It shines in mid-weight autumn and winter knits: cable sweaters, chunky cardigans, anything where bulk reads as cosiness.
  • Merino gives warmth without weight. It breathes well and wicks moisture, which makes it the standard choice for lighter layering pieces, fine-gauge turtlenecks, and sweaters meant to move between outdoors and heated interiors.

If your customers buy "one good winter sweater" for a cold climate, lambswool is a natural fit. If your line is about lightweight layers or sells across seasons, merino fits better.

Pilling and aftercare

Pilling — those small fibre balls on the fabric surface — happens when short or loose fibres work their way out of the yarn and tangle. It is driven by staple length, yarn twist, knit density and finishing, not by the fibre name alone: fine merino generally pills less than average lambswool, but a tightly knitted, well-finished lambswool can outperform a loosely spun merino.

As a buyer, two things help: first, evaluate pilling on a worn sample, not on a spec sheet; second, ask your manufacturer which anti-pilling finishing options are available for the specific yarn. Blending in a small percentage of nylon is another common durability fix, at a small cost to the "100% wool" story on your label.

Price — and where cashmere fits in

As a rule, fine merino yarns command a premium over standard lambswool, though premium lambswool types narrow the gap considerably. The usual hierarchy buyers work with looks like this:

  • Lambswool — best value in natural wool
  • Merino (mid to fine micron) — the premium standard
  • Merino-cashmere blends — cashmere hand feel at a controlled cost
  • Pure cashmere — the top tier, softer and warmer again, but significantly more expensive and more delicate

Cashmere fibres are finer still, which is why pure cashmere feels so distinctive. For most brands, a merino-cashmere blend delivers much of that perceived luxury while keeping landed cost workable — worth discussing with your supplier before defaulting to either pure option.

Matching the fibre to your product line

  • Lambswool: mid-market autumn/winter crewnecks, cable knits and cardigans where structure and value matter.
  • Merino: next-to-skin basics, fine-gauge layering pieces, travel-friendly and performance-leaning lines.
  • Merino-cashmere blend: elevated women's knitwear and gift-tier pieces.
  • Pure cashmere: top-price-point statements where the fibre itself is the selling point.

How to decide when you are sourcing

Start from your retail price and work backwards — the fibre has to leave room for yarn, making, finishing, freight and your margin.
