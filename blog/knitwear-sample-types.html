Knitwear Sample Types Explained: Fit, Photo, Press and Knitdown

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

Knitwear development uses several distinct sample types, each answering a different question. A knitdown tests the structure, a fit sample tests the shape on a body, a photo sample is the finished look shot for selling, a press sample goes to editors and buyers, and a counter sample is the bulk reference. Ordering the right ones at the right stage costs less than ordering all of them at once.

"Send me a sample" is an ambiguous instruction in knitwear development. A factory can reasonably respond with a knitdown swatch, a full garment in the intended yarn, or a version in whatever yarn was closest to hand — and all three are legitimate answers. The confusion is expensive, because a buyer expecting a wearable fit sample and receiving a swatch has lost a week. Naming the sample you want is the simplest way to shorten a development cycle.

Why are there so many sample types?

Because knitwear development answers a sequence of separate questions, and no single sample can answer all of them at once. Is the stitch structure right? Is the shape right on a body? Does it look right when styled and photographed? Is the bulk the same as what was approved? Each question needs its own reference, and each has an appropriate moment in the calendar.

The practical consequence is that buyers who compress everything into one sample usually discover the structure problem and the fit problem at the same time, which makes it hard to tell which adjustment caused the improvement. Separating them is slower on paper and faster in practice. Our notes on the sampling process cover how these sit in the overall timeline.

What is a knitdown?

A knitdown is a flat swatch or panel knitted from the intended yarn on the intended machine and gauge, with the intended structure. It is the cheapest and fastest sample in the process, and it answers the questions that require no garment at all: does the stitch read as intended, is the yarn the right weight, does the colour work at this gauge, and what does the hand feel like when knitted rather than wound on a cone.

Knitdowns are the right starting point for almost every development. Because they cost a fraction of a garment sample, a buyer can review several yarn or structure options in one go, which is why a supplier who opens development with a set of knitdowns is usually signalling an efficient process. Our guide to knit patterns explains why structure has to be fixed on a swatch before a garment is attempted.

What is a fit sample?

A fit sample is the first full garment in the intended yarn, structure and size, made specifically so the shape can be judged on a body. It is the point at which measurements stop being abstract: a garment can hit every point of measure and still sit wrong at the shoulder or pull across the chest.

What a fit sample should be reviewed for:

  • Shape on a person, in the intended size, worn as the customer will wear it — over a shirt if it layers, over nothing if it does not.
  • Movement: arms raised, seated, reaching. Fit problems in knitwear usually appear in motion rather than standing still.
  • Proportion — sleeve length against body length, neckline depth against shoulder width, hem position. Our guide to silhouettes and fits covers the geometry involved.

Fit samples are usually made in one or two sizes rather than the full range, because the purpose is to confirm the block rather than to check grading. Our sample review checklist covers what to check once one arrives.

What are photo and press samples?

Both are finished garments, and both are about selling rather than developing.

  • A photo sample is the finished piece at its best — correct yarn, correct colour, correct trims, pressed and finished — used for the ecommerce and campaign photography. It matters that this sample is genuinely production-representative, because the photographs become the customer's expectation, and a photograph of a sample that bulk cannot match generates returns. Our notes on sweater photography cover how to get usable images from it.
  • A press sample is sent to editors, stylists or wholesale accounts ahead of the season, usually on a returnable or loan basis. Press samples need to be finished to the same standard and often arrive before bulk production exists, which makes them genuinely early production runs — and therefore expensive relative to their quantity.

Both types cost more per unit than bulk, because they are effectively one-off production with the same setup. Buyers who need four colourways photographed should expect to pay for four photo samples, not one.

What is a counter sample?

A counter sample — sometimes called a sealed sample or bulk reference — is the sample made from bulk yarn, in the bulk construction, that both sides sign off and keep. It is the physical standard that bulk production and any third-party inspection are judged against.

Its defining feature is that it is not aspirational: it is made under production conditions, from the actual yarn lot, so that if it differs from the earlier fit sample, the difference is visible at the counter-sample stage rather than after shipment. Our guide to AQL inspection explains why the inspector needs it, and our notes on reviewing a sample cover how to seal and record it.

How many samples does a buyer actually need?

Fewer than many buyers order, and in a specific order. A practical sequence for a new style:

  • Knitdowns in two or three yarn or structure options — cheap, fast, and they eliminate the wrong choices before any garment is made.
  • One fit sample in the chosen option, in the key size, for shape approval.
  • One counter sample from bulk yarn before production starts.
  • Photo and press samples only when the style is confirmed and the marketing calendar requires them.

Where buyers commonly overspend: ordering a full garment before the structure is settled, ordering photo samples before bulk is confirmed, and ordering press samples in a colourway that later changes. Each is avoidable by sequencing rather than reducing ambition.

Who pays for samples?

Knitdowns and development samples are normally charged, since they consume yarn and machine time; photo and press samples are charged at close to production cost because they are production. The common arrangement is that some or all of the sampling cost is credited against the bulk order if it proceeds — worth confirming in writing before sampling starts rather than after, as our notes on reading a sweater quotation explain in the context of the wider costing.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a knitdown before a fit sample?

Strongly recommended. A knitdown settles the yarn, gauge and structure at a fraction of the cost, so the fit sample can be about shape rather than about whether the fabric was right. Skipping it usually means the first garment sample raises two problems at once, and it becomes unclear which change fixes which.

How many photo samples should I order?

One per colourway you intend to photograph, in the size the images will be taken in, plus at least one spare if the style will be styled in multiple ways. Photo samples are production-quality garments, so ordering them before bulk is confirmed risks paying for samples in a colourway that later changes.

What is the difference between a counter sample and a fit sample?

A fit sample is made to confirm the shape on a body and may use the nearest available yarn or construction. A counter sample is made under bulk conditions from the actual yarn lot, and it becomes the sealed standard that production and inspection are measured against. The fit sample is for development, the counter sample is for delivery.
