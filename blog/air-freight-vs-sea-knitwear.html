Air Freight or Sea Freight for Knitwear? How to Actually Decide

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Sea freight is almost always cheaper per garment, but it is not always the cheaper decision. The real comparison is not freight rate versus freight rate — it is the full landed cost of being six weeks early or six weeks late. For small first orders under roughly 300 pieces, urgent replenishment, and sample batches, air usually wins. For planned seasonal volumes above a few hundred kilograms, sea wins by a wide margin.

Every autumn we watch the same conversation happen: a buyer gets a production date, counts backwards to their launch, and discovers the maths doesn't work on sea freight. Then the question becomes whether to pay for air. This post is the working-out we go through with them — the numbers, the hidden costs, and the situations where each option genuinely wins.

The real cost difference, per garment

Knitwear is a bulky, light cargo: it ships by volume, not weight. A standard folded sweater in a polybag takes up roughly 0.003–0.005 cubic metres. That shapes the whole comparison.

On sea freight, a 20-foot container holds around 25,000–30,000 folded sweaters, and even LCL (less-than-container-load) shipments price out at only a few cents of freight per garment for bulk orders. If you want the full mechanics of containers, loading and documents, our guide to shipping sweaters by sea covers it.

On air freight, you pay by chargeable weight — and because knitwear is voluminous, the airline charges whichever is greater: actual weight or volumetric weight (length × width × height ÷ 6000). A 400 g sweater in packaging can be charged as 600–800 g. At typical rates of USD 4–8 per chargeable kg on the China–Europe and China–US lanes, that is roughly USD 2.5–6 of freight per sweater. On a $6 garment, air freight can equal the product cost itself.

The part of the maths people skip

Freight rate is only one line. The honest comparison includes:

  • Transit time. Sea from South China to Northern Europe or the US West Coast is roughly 25–40 days port-to-port, plus a week or two on each side for customs and trucking. Air is 3–8 days door-to-door. Our Incoterms guide explains how much of that journey is your responsibility under each term.
  • Cost of being late. A winter capsule that lands in mid-January instead of mid-November doesn't just arrive late — much of it lands in markdown. If the discount you'll take exceeds the air freight premium, air was the cheaper option all along.
  • Cost of cash tied up. Sea freight means your payment is sitting in cartons on the water for six weeks. For a growing brand, that cash-flow cost is real even if it never appears on an invoice.
  • Minimum charges. Air shipments under about 45 kg carry punishing minimums; below that, express courier (DHL, FedEx, UPS) is usually the right tool. Sea LCL has its own minimums and destination fees that can surprise first-time importers on small volumes.

When air freight is the right call

Four situations come up repeatedly:

  • First orders and market tests. A 100–300 piece first order exists to learn, not to stock. The extra few dollars per garment buy you six weeks of selling data before you commit to a real volume. Our first order vs reorder article covers how to size that test.
  • Urgent replenishment of a proven seller. When a style sells out in October, sea freight delivers in December — after the season. Air freight on a hot reorder is not a luxury; it's the only version that generates revenue.
  • Sample and photo batches. Pre-production samples, press samples and e-commerce photo samples always go by courier or air. Nobody photographs a collection six weeks late on purpose.
  • High-value lightweight product. Cashmere and fine merino have the best freight economics in knitwear: high value per kilogram. A $90 cashmere sweater absorbs $4 of air freight far more comfortably than a $6 acrylic one.

When sea freight wins outright

Once you cross a few hundred kilograms of planned, seasonal product, sea wins and it isn't close. Bulk winter orders placed in summer, basics programs with predictable replenishment, and anything with a fixed launch date more than three months out should all go by sea — ideally as FCL once you're near a container load, which is also gentler on the garments than repeated LCL handling.

The buyers who get sea freight right are the ones who treat the calendar as the constraint, not the freight rate. Our knitwear buying calendar works backwards from selling season to order date, which is where these decisions should start.

The split-shipment middle ground

The pattern we see working well for growing brands is a deliberate split: air-freight 10–20% of the order to hit the launch date and start collecting sell-through data, sea-freight the balance to arrive before the peak. You pay the air premium on a minority of units and the sea rate on the majority — and you're never sitting on a launched page with no stock.

Frequently asked questions

How much does air freight add per sweater?

Typically USD 2.5–6 per sweater on China–Europe and China–US lanes, because airlines charge by volumetric weight and a folded sweater in packaging is usually charged at 600–800 g even if it weighs 400 g. Fine-gauge, lightweight knits sit at the bottom of that range; chunky winter styles at the top.

What quantity makes sea freight worth it?

As a working rule, once a shipment exceeds roughly 300–400 kg or 2 cubic metres, sea LCL starts to beat air clearly on cost, and at 8–10 cubic metres a full container (FCL) becomes the cheapest and gentlest option. Below that, compare air against sea LCL carefully — LCL destination fees can erode the apparent saving on small volumes.

Can I split one order between air and sea?

Yes, and it's often the smartest structure: air-freight enough units to launch and test, sea-freight the balance for the peak. The factory simply packs and documents two shipments instead of one. Tell your supplier this at order stage, not after production, so the packing plan and carton marking are set up for it.
