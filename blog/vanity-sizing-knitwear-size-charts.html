Vanity Sizing: Why You Cannot Copy Another Brand's Knitwear Size Chart

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

There is no binding standard that fixes what a "medium" measures in most apparel markets. Brands set their own base blocks and grade rules, and many have shifted label sizes over time so a given body fits into a smaller label — vanity sizing. For a knitwear buyer the consequence is simple: copying another brand chart copies their fit decisions, not a standard. Build your own from target body measurements, grade it from one approved fit sample, and hold bulk to tolerances around that chart.

We see this a lot when a buyer sends a competitor size chart and asks us to make "the same sizes". The chart is real, the measurements are clear, and it will almost certainly produce a garment that fits nothing like the one in the photo — because that chart belongs to a different block, a different ease allowance and a different idea of who the customer is. Here is how to build a chart that is actually yours, and why knitwear makes it matter more.

What vanity sizing actually is

Alpha sizes such as S, M and L are not defined by any enforceable international standard in most markets. A brand chooses a base size, decides how much ease to add, and grades the rest from there. Over decades, many labels have quietly re-cut their blocks so that a customer who used to buy a large now fits a medium. The label changed; the body did not. Numbered sizes drift too, which is why a UK 12, a US 8 and a European 38 are not three names for one set of measurements. Our size naming guide covers those conventions, and sizing and grading covers how the increments between sizes are set.

The practical consequence for a buyer is that a size chart is a design decision, not a fact. Two brands can both be internally consistent and still differ by several centimetres at the chest on a size M.

Why knitwear makes it worse

Woven garments are cut from a stable cloth and fit mostly by pattern. Knitted garments are built loop by loop, and the fabric itself stretches, relaxes and recovers. Ease is not a fixed number — it depends on gauge, stitch structure, yarn and finishing. A 2 cm difference that would be invisible in a woven shirt changes how a fine-gauge knit hangs, and a rib hem can pull a measurement in by another centimetre after washing.

That is also why a size chart cannot be separated from the silhouette. An oversized drop-shoulder style and a fitted ribbed tank may share a label but need completely different chest measurements. Decisions about unisex blocks or extended sizing change the grade rules again — you cannot simply scale one chart up and expect the proportions to hold.

What copying a chart actually costs you

We have had buyers send a chart taken from a woven shirt brand, or from a competitor with a much younger customer base, and ask us to knit to it. Three things tend to follow. The fit sample feels wrong on the body but nobody can say why, because the reference was never a knit. The size ratio is then planned against the wrong assumptions, so the best-selling size sells out while others sit. And the online size guide ends up describing a garment that does not exist, which is a reliable route to returns and reviews about sizing running small. Getting the chart right is cheaper than any of those.

How to build your own chart

  • Start from the body, not the label. Write down the target customer measurements for each size — chest, waist, hip, shoulder, arm length — from your own market research or a size-study source.
  • Set ease per style. A chunky 5GG crew needs more ease than a 14GG ribbed tank. Record it for each silhouette rather than once for the whole range.
  • Approve one fit sample on a real person. A fit session and pattern block is where the numbers become a garment; wear trials catch what a measuring tape cannot.
  • Grade from that approved base. Fix the increment per size per point of measure, then write the whole chart with tolerances — our measurement guide explains how points of measure are taken and what tolerance is realistic for knits.
  • Put it in the quality standard. The chart, the method and the tolerances belong in the quality standards document so an inspector can check bulk against something specific.

Hold it consistent from sample to bulk

A good chart is worthless if production drifts. Keep a gold seal reference sample, measure after the garment has relaxed and been washed — see the wash-test protocol — and never let a grading change happen quietly to improve fabric yield in the cutting room. If a measurement has to move, move it in the spec and re-approve, so every future reorder still matches the first delivery. If you sell into several markets, plan dual labelling from the start; the conventions differ enough that one chart rarely serves all of them, as our UK market guide and US market guide touch on.

Frequently asked questions

Is there an official standard for what a size M measures?

No binding one in most markets. Brands set their own base blocks and grade rules, which is why a medium in one label can differ by several centimetres from a medium in another. Treat any chart as a design decision you need to own, not a standard you can borrow.

Can you just copy the size chart from my current supplier?

You can, but you are adopting their block, their ease and their idea of the customer. If you are changing factory, fibre or silhouette, approve a fit sample against your own target body measurements instead. Otherwise the first bulk order will fit differently from the sample you liked.

How many sizes should a first knitwear order cover?

It depends on the channel and the market, not on a rule. Most first runs work from a focused core range, then adjust the size ratio from real sell-through data. Our size ratio planning guide covers how buyers distribute quantities across sizes rather than ordering equal quantities of each.
