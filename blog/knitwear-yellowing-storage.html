Yellowing in Stored Knitwear: Causes and Prevention

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Yellowing on white and pale knitwear is usually not a dyeing fault. The most common cause is phenolic yellowing — the fibre reacting with nitrogen oxides in the air, often catalysed by phenolic antioxidants in packaging or by storage conditions. The second most common is the breakdown of optical brighteners. Both are slow, both appear after the garment has been packed for weeks or months, and both are prevented by packaging and storage choices rather than by re-dyeing.

A buyer opens a carton six weeks after shipment and finds the cream sweaters are now a shade nearer yellow, especially where they were pressed against the polybag. Nothing in the dyeing record explains it, the lab dip matched, and the factory insists the goods were correct when packed. They probably were. Yellowing is one of the few knitwear faults that genuinely develops after the factory has finished with the garment.

What is phenolic yellowing?

It is a chemical reaction between the fibre and oxides of nitrogen in the atmosphere, and it is the single most common cause of yellowing on stored pale textiles. The reaction is accelerated by heat, humidity and by phenolic compounds present in some packaging materials — certain antioxidants used in polyethylene film are the classic offender.

The reason it matters commercially is the pattern. It is invisible at inspection, develops over weeks in a carton, and concentrates where the garment touches the packaging. By the time it is visible, the goods are usually in the buyer's warehouse or on a shop floor.

Wool, cotton and cellulosic fibres are all susceptible. The dye or the fibre whiteness is not at fault; the reaction happens at the surface and reads as a shift toward yellow.

What else turns pale knitwear yellow?

  • Optical brighteners breaking down. Whites are often finished with fluorescent whitening agents that make the fabric read brighter than its actual colour. Those agents degrade with light, heat and age, and when they do the fabric reverts to a duller, yellower tone. A garment that was never truly white but optically boosted will drift.
  • Residual chemicals from finishing. Insufficient rinsing after bleaching or washing leaves residues that react over time. This is a process control issue at the factory, not a storage one.
  • Humidity and condensation. Moisture in a sealed polybag or a container creates the conditions for both phenolic yellowing and, at higher levels, mould. Our notes on mould and mildew cover the moisture side.
  • Light exposure. Sunlight and some artificial light shift pale shades over time, particularly where garments sit in a shop window.
  • Contact transfer. Rust, adhesives, certain cardboards and printed materials can stain or discolour pale knitwear in contact.

How is yellowing tested?

Two tests cover the two mechanisms, and they are different.

Phenolic yellowing test. A specimen is exposed to nitrogen oxides under controlled conditions alongside a control fabric, and the degree of yellowing is assessed against a grey scale after a set period. The result tells you how susceptible the material is to the reaction, not whether a particular shipment will yellow — but it does catch a fibre or finish that is unusually prone.

Whiteness and colour measurement. Where a pale shade is critical, whiteness can be measured instrumentally before and after accelerated ageing, which shows how much the optical brightener is likely to fade.

Neither test is routine on every order, and that is the practical point: most buyers discover yellowing rather than test for it. Our notes on lab testing explain which tests are worth adding for a pale range, and restricted substances testing covers the related chemistry.

How is it prevented?

Almost entirely through packaging and storage, which is why the fix sits with the buyer as much as the factory.

  • Specify non-phenolic packaging. Ask the factory to confirm that the polybags are free of phenolic antioxidants. This single change removes the most common catalyst.
  • Do not over-seal. Airtight bags trap moisture and nitrogen oxides against the fabric. A small vent or a looser pack reduces the concentration at the surface.
  • Control humidity in storage. Dry, ventilated storage slows both yellowing and mould. Damp warehouses accelerate everything.
  • Keep cartons off concrete floors and away from exterior walls, where condensation forms.
  • Minimise storage time. Yellowing is a function of time as well as conditions, so a fast-moving pale line has less exposure than a slow one. Our notes on inventory planning cover the commercial side.
  • Separate pale goods from dark and from printed materials, and never store them against cardboard with phenolic inks or adhesives.

What should a buyer do when it appears?

Establish the timing. If the yellowing appears within weeks of packing and concentrates where the garment met the polybag, phenolic yellowing is the likely cause and the packaging is the lead. If it is uniform and developed over months, suspect the optical brightener or the finish.

Check whether it is reversible. Some phenolic yellowing can be reduced by rewashing, which is a useful diagnostic: if a test wash restores the shade, the cause is surface chemistry rather than a dye failure. It is not, however, a fix for goods already in a store.

Do not accept a re-dye as the default answer. If the mechanism is packaging, re-dyeing the goods into the same packaging reproduces the problem. The remedy is the packaging specification.

Document it with photographs and dates, because a yellowing claim depends on showing when the change happened. Our notes on handling complaints cover how to build that record.

How should a pale range be specified?

  • State a whiteness or shade reference and a tolerance, so a drift has a defined limit rather than a subjective one. Our notes on colour consistency cover how the tolerance is set.
  • Ask for phenolic-free packaging in writing as part of the packaging spec, not as a verbal request.
  • Consider a phenolic yellowing test on the first order of a new pale shade, as a one-off rather than a routine.
  • Set storage and transit expectations with the factory — humidity control, ventilation, not against exterior walls.
  • Plan the sell-through so pale goods do not sit for a season, since time is the one variable that cannot be engineered away.

Frequently asked questions

Is yellowing a sign of bad quality?

Not necessarily. Phenolic yellowing can affect well-made garments if the packaging or storage conditions allow it, and it is invisible at the time of manufacture. A useful distinction is timing and pattern: yellowing concentrated where the garment touched packaging, appearing within weeks, points at the packaging rather than at the fibre or the dye.

Can yellowed knitwear be restored?

Some phenolic yellowing can be reduced by rewashing, which is a useful way to diagnose the cause but not a practical fix for goods already in a store. Yellowing caused by degraded optical brighteners is generally not reversible, because the brightening agent has been consumed rather than deposited on the surface.

How do I stop white sweaters yellowing in storage?

The most effective single step is specifying phenolic-free polybags, because phenolic antioxidants in packaging film are the commonest catalyst. Beyond that, keep storage dry and ventilated, avoid over-sealing bags so moisture and nitrogen oxides cannot concentrate against the fabric, keep cartons off concrete floors, and shorten storage time by planning sell-through.
