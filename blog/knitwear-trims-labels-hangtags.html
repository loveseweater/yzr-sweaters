Trims, Labels and Hangtags: The Details That Make a Sweater Branded

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Quick answer

Two sweaters can come off the same machine, in the same yarn, and feel like different products on the shelf. What separates them is rarely the knitting — it is the label at the neck, the care label inside the seam, the hangtag, the button and the bag. These details cost little relative to the garment and are disproportionately visible. Plan them early and you get a brand out of a factory; leave them late and you get a good sweater that looks like everyone else's.

The main label: your name at the neck

The main label is the one item every garment must carry in your own name. In knitwear it usually sits at the back neck, woven in during linking or printed and applied later.

  • Woven labels are the traditional choice — the logo is jacquard-woven from yarn, giving a raised, dimensional look that survives washing well. Damask and satin weaves are common.
  • Printed labels are lighter, softer and cheaper at volume, and suit heat-transfer or satin-print finishes where a woven edge would feel heavy at the neck.
  • Placement matters as much as the label: centre-back neck, side seam or a small hem flag all read differently.

Keep the artwork simple — thin lines and fine serifs are hard to weave cleanly at small sizes, and a logo that works on a business card will not always survive at 30 mm wide in yarn.

Care and content labels: the compliance side

The care label is where branding meets law. Composition and country of origin must be accurate, and washing, drying, ironing and bleaching instructions should match how the garment actually behaves. Fibre percentages are usually listed in descending order of weight, and the tolerance allowed for blends varies by market — so the figures should come from the yarn supplier's documentation, not be estimated late. The practical points:

  • Match the care instruction to the fibre — a wool blend the label invites you to tumble dry is a returns problem waiting to happen.
  • Keep symbols and wording consistent across the range so your care story does not change from style to style.
  • Check the market's language requirements. Several destinations expect care information in the local language, sometimes alongside fibre content and origin.

Care labels are printed on satin or a soft synthetic tape and tucked into a side seam. Test a sample against the actual wash programme before bulk.

Size labels and the size story

Size labels carry the fit system your buyers already know — XS to XL, numeric, or your own scale — and must line up with the size set on the pattern. Where a market requires a specific size designation, show it on the label rather than relying on a website chart.

Hangtags and packaging trims

The hangtag is your only piece of printed communication attached to the garment, and it is where brands most often waste money: ordered in large quantities, easy to over-design, and obsolete the moment the collection changes.

  • Common formats — rectangular, folded or booklet tags, die-cut shapes, and swing tags secured with a plastic barb or string loop.
  • Materials and finishes — recycled or FSC-certified board, textured uncoated stock, foil blocking, embossing or matte laminate.
  • What to put on it — fibre story, origin, care summary, barcode or QR for retail scanning, and your brand line.

Beyond the tag, the packaging set completes the presentation: a polybag with a suffocation warning and size sticker where required, a printed polybag or tissue wrap for a premium feel, a branded box or gift sleeve for holiday programmes, and a woven header card for accessories.

Buttons, zips and other garment trims

Functional trims are part of the design, not an afterthought. Horn-effect or corozo buttons, metal snaps, drawcord tips and contrast tipping all change the price point and perceived quality. Two rules: specify trim colour against the yarn rather than a screen, and ask for pull and wash tests on any hardware.

Order trims early — before the bulk order

Trims are the most common cause of a delayed sweater shipment, and the reason is always the same: they are treated as a small item and approved late. Labels, hangtags and packaging are made by different suppliers, with their own artwork proofing and production runs, and none can be stitched to a garment that is already finished.

  • Place trim orders with the bulk purchase order, or earlier for new woven label designs.
  • Approve artwork proofs before yarn dyeing starts so nothing waits on a label decision.
  • Ask for a labelled sample — a top-sample with the real main and care labels sewn in — and check how they sit.
  • Confirm quantities with wastage for labels and hangtags.
  • Approve the physical components, not a photo. A button's weight, a label's edge against skin and a zip's action cannot be judged on a screen — see our notes on the trim card for how to approve them before bulk.
