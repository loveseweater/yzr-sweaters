Common Knitwear Quality Defects and How Inspection Prevents Them

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Knitwear is forgiving to wear but unforgiving to produce. Because a sweater is knitted panel by panel and then linked together, a problem that starts at one machine can travel through an entire lot before anyone notices. The good news: nearly every defect that shows up in a finished sweater falls into a handful of categories, and all of them can be caught — or better, prevented — with a structured garment inspection process.

The six defects buyers see most in knitwear

If you are new to buying sweaters, train your eye on these first: they account for most re-work, disputes and returns in knit production.

  • Pilling. Small fibre balls that form on the surface after wear and washing. Driven by fibre type, yarn twist and knit density, it is largely designed in rather than caused on the floor. Agree on a pilling grade at sampling and record it on the approved sample.
  • Shade variation. Panels of one sweater are knitted separately, so panels from different dye lots can end up in the same garment — a sleeve a step lighter than the body. Lot control at knitting and linking prevents it.
  • Broken yarn and dropped stitches. A single missed or broken stitch runs down the wale as a visible ladder or hole. Causes range from worn needles to wrong yarn tension; systematic laddering points to a machine setting, not a careless operator.
  • Size deviation. Measurements drifting outside tolerance against the size chart, often after washing or pressing. Shrinkage testing at sampling is how a serious factory sets the right tolerance before bulk.
  • Stains and oil marks. Knitting machine oil, marking pens and dirty handling show up clearly on light colours. Good factories manage this with housekeeping and handling discipline rather than spot-cleaning at the end.
  • Linking seam marks. Where front, back and sleeves are joined. Coarse or uneven linking shows as a bulky, visible ridge, especially on fine-gauge styles. It is a skill issue, and one of the clearest signs of an experienced linking team versus a new one.

What AQL inspection actually means

Most buyers do not inspect every piece — they sample. AQL, or Acceptable Quality Limit, is the statistical standard behind that sampling, defined in tables such as ISO 2859-1. In practice: based on order quantity, the plan tells the inspector how many pieces to pull at random. Each defect found is classified as critical, major or minor, and the lot passes or fails against the AQL levels agreed in advance. In apparel, the common defaults are zero tolerated critical defects, AQL 2.5 for majors and AQL 4.0 for minors — but the numbers are yours to set, tighter for premium knitwear, looser for promotional goods. For sweaters, make sure the checklist also covers a measurement audit against your size chart and a shade assessment under a standard daylight-type light source, because those two account for a large share of knitwear disputes.

In-line, final and third-party inspection

These three stages answer different questions, and a mature QC plan uses all three.

  • In-line inspection (during production). Checks semi-finished panels and garments on the floor. Its value is timing: a tension problem or a shade mix-up found at 20% completion costs a correction, while the same problem found at packing costs a shipment date.
  • Final random inspection (pre-shipment). Done when goods are finished and substantially packed. The inspector samples against your agreed AQL, measures, checks workmanship, packaging and labeling, and issues a pass/fail report. This is the last gate before the balance payment and the container.
  • Third-party inspection. An independent firm — SGS and Bureau Veritas (BV) are the names most buyers use — inspects on your behalf, against your standard, in the factory you hired. It adds cost, but removes the conflict of interest in a factory grading its own work, and gives you documentation if a dispute needs evidence.

Writing quality standards into your contract

Inspection only works if it checks against something agreed. Before you confirm a purchase order, put the following in writing:

  • The pre-production sample, countersigned by both sides, as the physical benchmark.
  • A signed size chart with explicit tolerances for every measurement point.
  • Agreed AQL levels for critical, major and minor defects.
  • Fibre composition and care-labeling requirements, with test reports where needed.
  • The inspection scope: who inspects, at which stages, against which standard.
  • What happens on failure — re-work and re-inspection, and who carries the cost of the delay.

None of this is adversarial: a good factory welcomes a clear standard, because it protects them from moving targets too.

The takeaway for buyers

Most knitwear defects are cheaper to prevent at the machine than to argue about after arrival.
