Worsted vs Woollen Yarn: How the Spinning Route Changes Your Sweater

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

Worsted and woollen are the two main spinning routes, and they change a yarn's surface, strength, warmth and price as much as the fibre itself does. Worsted yarn is combed so the long fibres lie parallel, giving a smooth, strong, dense yarn for refined fine-gauge knits. Woollen yarn is only carded, so shorter fibres sit in every direction, giving an airy, soft, hairy yarn for chunky cosy knits.

Buyers routinely specify fibre, gauge and weight, and yet almost nobody specifies the spinning route — even though two yarns spun from identical fibre can knit into garments that look, wear and age completely differently. The route decides how hairy the surface is, how the fabric pills, how warm it feels for its weight and, to a large degree, what it costs. Understanding the two routes turns a vague conversation about "yarn quality" into a concrete decision.

What is the difference between worsted and woollen spinning?

Both routes turn raw fibre into spun yarn. The difference is what happens just before spinning.

Woollen-spun yarn goes through carding only. Carding brushes the fibres into a loose web, keeping them in many directions, including the shorter fibres. The result is a yarn with a lot of trapped air, a soft halo of fibre on the surface, and relatively low twist density along its length.

Worsted-spun yarn is carded and then combed. Combing removes the shorter fibres and lays the remaining long fibres parallel to the yarn's axis before they are twisted. The result is a smoother, denser, stronger yarn with far less surface hair.

One important clarification: these terms describe the spinning route, not the fibre. A "woollen-spun merino" is merino wool spun the woollen way; a worsted-spun yarn can be made from wool, acrylic, cotton or blends. The route and the fibre are two separate choices, and confusing them is one of the most common misunderstandings in knitwear sourcing.

How does each route change the fabric?

  • Surface. Worsted reads smooth, clean and slightly lustrous; stitch definition is crisp. Woollen reads matte, soft and slightly fuzzy, with a halo that softens stitch edges — which is why traditional Fair Isle and Aran knitting is woollen-spun.
  • Pilling tendency. Short, loosely anchored fibres migrate to the surface and pill more easily. Worsted yarns, built from longer parallel fibres locked in tighter twist, generally pill less than woollen yarns of the same fibre. Expectation management matters here rather than treating pilling purely as a defect — our guide to pilling explains the mechanics.
  • Warmth for weight. The air trapped inside a woollen-spun yarn makes it loftier and warmer per gram, which is why winter accessories and chunky knits have traditionally been woollen-spun. Our notes on how knitwear keeps you warm explain why trapped air is the real insulator.
  • Strength and durability. Worsted yarns resist abrasion and hold fine stitch structures better, which is why refined 14GG polos and tailored knits are almost always worsted-spun.
  • Price. Combing is an extra processing step that also discards a portion of the fibre, so worsted yarns of a given fibre usually cost more than woollen equivalents.

Which products suit which route?

Neither route is better; they are built for different jobs.

  • Worsted suits: fine-gauge polos and shirts, knitted dresses, turtlenecks worn under jackets, tailored or minimal aesthetics, and any design where clean stitch definition is part of the look.
  • Woollen suits: chunky cable and Aran-style sweaters, winter accessories, brushed surfaces, cosy heritage aesthetics, and designs where loft and softness matter more than crispness.
  • In between: semi-worsted routes exist, and many commercial yarns sit somewhere along the spectrum rather than at either extreme. Ask where a yarn sits rather than assuming a binary.

What does the route change in production?

Beyond the fabric itself, the spinning route has practical consequences for an order. Woollen-spun yarns shed more in early wears, so consumer expectations should be set accordingly. Their loftier nature also means gauge and weight behave differently: the same gauge knitted in a woollen yarn produces a rounder, bulkier fabric than a worsted yarn of the same count, which matters when you are matching a reference garment. And because worsted requires longer, combed fibre, sourcing a specific worsted quality can take longer when fine-fibre markets are tight — the same dynamic described in our notes on wool price volatility.

Substitution between the two routes is effectively a different yarn, not a swap: replacing a woollen-spun yarn with a worsted one changes the surface, the weight and the hand entirely. If a supplier ever proposes this, treat it under the full substitution process described in our guide to yarn substitution, not as a minor detail.

How do you ask a factory which route a yarn uses?

Many suppliers will know their yarns by feel and mill without using the worsted/woollen vocabulary, so ask practically rather than academically:

  • Request the yarn specification sheet — spinning route is usually listed alongside count and twist.
  • Ask to see and squeeze two options — a worsted and a woollen version of the same fibre makes the difference obvious in seconds.
  • Describe the outcome you want — "smooth surface, crisp stitches, minimal early pilling" describes worsted; "lofty, soft, hairy, cosy" describes woollen. A competent supplier can route you correctly from either description.
  • Knit a swatch in your target gauge and judge the surface in daylight — the difference between the routes shows up immediately in a swatch.

What are the common misunderstandings?

Three come up repeatedly. First, "woollen means made of wool" — no, the term refers to the spinning route and applies to synthetic and cotton yarns too. Second, "worsted is the premium option" — it is the premium *processing*, but a chunky Aran knitted in a fine worsted yarn would lose exactly the character that makes it an Aran. Third, "the route matters less than the fibre" — in practice the route changes surface, pilling and warmth as visibly as moving between fibre types, so both choices belong in the specification together. Our guide to yarn types covers the fibre side of that decision.

Frequently asked questions

Does worsted-spun yarn always pill less than woollen-spun?

As a general tendency, yes — the longer parallel fibres are locked in more tightly and are less likely to migrate to the surface. But pilling also depends on the fibre's staple length, the twist level and how the garment is worn, so two worsted yarns can pill differently. Judge it on a knitted swatch rather than on the route name alone.

Can synthetic yarns be woollen-spun?

Yes. Worsted and woollen describe how fibres are prepared and spun, not what they are made of. Acrylic, nylon and blended yarns are produced on both routes, which is why the spinning route should be specified separately from the fibre content when you brief a factory.

Why is traditional Aran knitting always woollen-spun?

Because the Aran aesthetic depends on what woollen spinning provides: loft that pushes the cable reliefs outward, a soft halo that blends the stitches, and warmth for weight in a garment worn loose over layers. The same patterns knitted in a smooth worsted yarn look flatter and feel denser, which is why the pairing of structure and route matters.
