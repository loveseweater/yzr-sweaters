Recycled and Organic Fibres in Knitwear: GRS, RWS and What They Mean

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Recycled and organic fibres used to be a niche request that arrived once or twice a season. Now they turn up in ordinary buying briefs, and retailers increasingly ask for fibre documentation before a range is signed off. If you buy knitwear, understanding how these fibres are made — and what the certificates behind them cover — affects both your costing and what you can say on a product page.

Why these fibres moved into mainstream buying

Two forces pushed them there. Green-claim rules have tightened in several European markets, and shoppers question vague sustainability language faster than they used to. At the same time, spinners have improved recycled yarns to the point where a good recycled wool blend feels close to virgin wool in a finished sweater. Certified fibres now appear inside regular knitwear programs, not only as a premium capsule.

Recycled wool and recycled polyester: how they are made

Recycled wool starts as mill clippings or collected garments. The material is sorted by colour and quality, shredded back to fibre, then re-spun — often blended with virgin wool or a carrier fibre such as polyester or nylon so the yarn is strong enough for machine knitting. Colour sorting matters: a well-sorted stream needs little re-dyeing, which is where the melange look comes from.

  • Pre-consumer recycled wool — mill clippings and offcuts; cleaner and more consistent.
  • Post-consumer recycled wool — from collected garments; more sorting, more textured hand.
  • Recycled polyester — usually rPET spun into staple fibre; adds durability and shape recovery.
  • Blends — recycled wool with nylon or polyester generally knits more reliably than 100% recycled wool.

GRS: the Global Recycled Standard

GRS is the certification most buyers meet first. It verifies recycled content and traces it through the supply chain — from recycler to spinner, knitter and trader — while also covering social and environmental criteria at each processing site and restricting certain chemicals. A GRS claim only holds if every stage in the chain is certified and the transaction certificates travel with the goods. Ask for the scope certificate of the mill that spun your yarn, and the transaction certificate tying your order to recycled input.

RWS: the Responsible Wool Standard

RWS addresses the farm rather than the recycling stream. It sets requirements for animal welfare — handling, shearing, health and grazing — and for land management, traced from farm through the wool chain. For markets where customers ask about welfare-assured or mulesing-free wool, RWS is the credential that answers the question. Like GRS, it depends on chain-of-custody paperwork, so check that the certificate belongs to your actual yarn supplier.

Organic cotton and GOTS

Organic cotton is grown without synthetic pesticides or fertilisers and without genetically modified seed, and it is normally certified to a standard that also covers processing. GOTS is the one most buyers reference: it requires a minimum share of certified organic fibre, limits chemicals through dyeing and finishing, and adds wastewater and social criteria. For a knitwear program, a GOTS-certified yarn lets you make a claim that survives scrutiny — provided the dyehouse and knitting mill sit inside the certified chain.

What the certifications mean for EU and US buyers

  • Compliance — documentation that supports your green claims during retailer onboarding and marketplace listing.
  • Risk — chain-of-custody evidence reduces the chance of a claim being challenged after goods ship.
  • Marketing — certifications give packaging and product pages something concrete to say instead of adjectives.
  • Range planning — certified fibres run in the same silhouettes as conventional ones, so they work as a parallel colour story rather than a separate collection.

Hand feel and cost: the honest trade-offs

Recycled fibres are not a straight swap. Recycled wool tends to feel drier and more textured, with shorter fibre length, so pilling resistance depends heavily on the blend and the spin. Colour consistency is harder in post-consumer streams, and shade variation is something to plan for rather than treat as a defect. Certified and recycled yarns generally carry a premium over conventional yarn, and how large it is varies with blend ratio, certification scope and order volume. Ask your manufacturer for a side-by-side costing in the same gauge before you commit.

How to brief a factory on certified yarns

  • Name the standard and the claim — "GRS recycled wool, minimum 50% recycled content" is a usable brief; "sustainable wool" is not.
  • Ask which spinners the factory already uses and whether those mills hold current scope certificates.
  • Confirm who holds the transaction certificate and when it is issued — usually after shipment, which your compliance team needs to know in advance.
  • Decide whether the claim goes on the garment label or only in marketing, since label claims trigger extra verification.
  • Ask about colour development and sampling time — certified yarn may need ordering in from a specific spinner rather than pulled from stock.
