Angora Rabbit Hair in Knitwear: What Buyers Should Actually Specify

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Angora is the down hair of the angora rabbit — exceptionally light, fine and fluffy, which is exactly why it is almost never used pure. Buyers should specify the blend ratio (typically 10–30% angora with wool or nylon), the fibre origin and any animal-welfare documentation, and accept that a fluffy halo means faster pilling and some shedding. On price it sits below cashmere but above lambswool, and it gives a softness no synthetic reproduces. Order it as a blended yarn, not a fibre spec.

Angora shows up in a buyer's brief most often as a feeling: "we want that fluffy, cloud-like halo." The fibre that delivers it is the undercoat of the angora rabbit, and it is one of the more misunderstood materials in knitwear because what you can buy and what the word implies are two different things. We knit angora blends regularly, and the questions we get are consistent — is it real, why does it pill, is it cruel, and how is it different from the cashmere and mohair buyers already know. Here is the plain version.

Where angora actually comes from

Angora is harvested from angora rabbits, not sheep, and the commercial fibre is the rabbit's soft underdown rather than the coarser guard hair. The animals are clipped or plucked at intervals and the down is sorted and de-haired before spinning. China is by far the largest producer, which matters for buyers because provenance and welfare standards vary widely and the supply chain is less formalised than merino or cashmere. If traceability is part of your brand story, ask for it explicitly — a generic "angora" on a composition label tells you almost nothing about the farm, the harvest method or the country of origin, and our notes on traceable animal fibres apply here too.

Why you almost always buy it blended

Pure angora yarn is rare, expensive and technically awkward: the fibre has almost no memory, so a 100% angora garment loses shape fast and the down migrates out of the fabric. What gives angora its value — that weightless fluff — also makes it structurally weak on its own. So the standard commercial approach is a blend: 10–30% angora with lambswool for body and hand-feel, or with nylon for strength and to lock the halo in place. The blend ratio is the single most important number in the spec. At 10% you get a hint of halo at a modest cost; at 30% the fluff is unmistakable but so is the price and the pilling. We help buyers dial this in against the target retail price, much as we do when advising on cashmere blend percentages — the same logic, a different fibre.

The pilling and shedding you should plan for

A fluffy surface means loose fibre ends, and loose fibre ends mean two things buyers need to brief their customers about. First, shedding: a new angora-blend garment will lose a little down in the first wears and washes — it is normal, not a defect, and a gentle lint roller handles it. Second, pilling: the halo fibres agglomerate into small balls at friction zones faster than a tight worsted wool. This is largely a function of the angora content and the knit structure, and the mitigation is a tighter gauge and a higher-twist supporting yarn rather than a QC tightening — the same ply and twist thinking we apply to any fluffy blend. We tell buyers to set expectations on the care card rather than pretend it will behave like a smooth lambswool; our fabric care guide covers what to print.

Angora vs cashmere, mohair and lambswool

Buyers routinely confuse these. Cashmere is warmer per gram and smoother, holds shape better and sits at the top of the price ladder — see our cashmere versus wool comparison. Mohair, from the angora goat, gives a longer, silkier sheen and a different kind of halo; our alpaca and mohair guide covers it. Lambswool is the workhorse base that angora is usually blended with, and merino versus lambswool explains the base-fibre choice underneath the blend. Position angora as the "fluffy halo at moderate cost" option: softer and lighter than lambswool, cheaper than cashmere, but with more care caveats than either.

Welfare, certification and the Asia supply picture

Angora carries a reputational risk that cashmere does not, because of historical live-plucking footage that damaged several Western brands. Most responsible supply now comes from clipped fibre, but the documentation is less standardised than the certifications buyers expect for wool. If your market or retailers require proof, build it into the purchase order from the start: origin declaration, harvest method, and any brand-acceptable welfare standard. We can source clipped angora with a country-of-origin statement, and we recommend buyers who are wary of the category consider machine-washable wool or a brushed lambswool for a similar soft hand without the scrutiny.

Frequently asked questions

Is angora usually blended, and what ratio should I specify?

Yes — almost always. Pure angora has no structural memory and sheds excessively. The practical range is 10–30% angora with lambswool for body or nylon for strength. Ten per cent gives a subtle halo at modest cost; thirty per cent is unmistakably fluffy but more expensive and more prone to pilling. Decide the ratio against your retail price, not against the "fluffiest possible" instinct.

Why does my angora sweater shed and pill?

The fluff is loose fibre ends, so a new garment naturally loses a little down in early wears and forms pills at friction points faster than smooth wool. Neither is a defect at moderate levels. Mitigate with a tighter gauge and a higher-twist supporting yarn, and set customer expectations on the care card — gentle washing and a lint roller handle the rest.

How is angora different from cashmere and mohair?

Angora comes from a rabbit and gives the lightest, fluffiest halo; cashmere comes from goat undercoat and is warmer, smoother and pricier; mohair is goat fibre with a longer, silkier sheen. Angora is the moderate-cost "soft halo" option — softer and lighter than lambswool, cheaper than cashmere, but with more care caveats than either.
