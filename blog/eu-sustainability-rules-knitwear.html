EU Sustainability Rules for Knitwear: What Importers Should Prepare For

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Sustainability rules in the EU are no longer a packaging problem for the marketing team. They are reaching into what a garment is made of, how long it is expected to last, what you can claim about it, and what happens to it after the consumer is finished with it. For knitwear importers the practical question is not whether this affects you, but which parts of your supply chain you can document today.

ESPR: ecodesign rules extended to textiles

The Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR) replaces the older ecodesign framework for energy-related products and broadens it to a long list of product groups, textiles and apparel among them. The regulation itself sets the framework; the binding detail comes later through delegated acts, which define the specific requirements per product group. Textiles are among the priority areas, and delegated acts are still being developed, so the exact obligations and their start dates should always be confirmed against the official text rather than a summary.

What buyers can reasonably expect from this direction of travel:

  • Durability and repairability as design expectations — colourfastness, pilling, seam strength and shape retention become documented properties rather than marketing adjectives.
  • Recycled content requirements or incentives, which for knitwear means being able to evidence fibre origin, not just name a percentage.
  • Substance and material restrictions alongside the existing chemical framework, which most knitwear programmes already touch through Oeko-Tex or equivalent testing.
  • Information obligations attached to the product itself, which is where the digital product passport comes in.

Digital product passport (DPP)

The DPP concept is a product-specific data record, accessible by scanning a data carrier, holding information about composition, origin, care, durability and end-of-life handling. For a sweater that means being able to answer, with evidence: which fibres, from which suppliers, in what proportions, processed where. Nothing in that list is exotic — it is the information a careful buyer already collects during development. The difference is that it would need to be structured, retained and updated rather than kept loosely in email threads.

Start now with the easy wins: keep a fibre composition record per style, log your yarn suppliers and their certifications, and store test reports against the style number rather than a shipment number.

EPR: extended producer responsibility

Several EU member states have already introduced producer responsibility schemes for textiles, with France among the best known and others (including the Netherlands) operating or developing their own regimes. EPR generally means that whoever places goods on that national market registers, reports volumes and pays a fee, often eco-modulated so that lower-impact or more recyclable garments cost less. Obligations and fee structures differ by country, and they change — some schemes also require that the producer be established in the country or appoint an authorised representative. If you sell into more than one EU market, treat EPR as a per-country registration and reporting exercise, and check the current rules for each market you ship to.

Due diligence on supply chains

Two separate pressures are converging on the same paperwork. On deforestation, the EU has introduced due diligence obligations for specific commodities; textiles are not a covered commodity in themselves, but the underlying expectation — that a company can trace materials to their source and evidence that they were legally produced — is the same pattern. Separately, rules addressing forced labour in supply chains are moving through the EU framework, with a focus on products made with forced labour. For knitwear importers, both point to the same preparation: know your spinner, know your yarn mill, and keep documentation that a third party can verify.

Green claims: what you can say

  • Substantiate before you publish. Vague environmental claims with no evidence are increasingly the target of both EU-level rules and national consumer authorities.
  • Be precise about comparison. "Recycled", "lower impact" and "eco" each carry different expectations; a claim comparing two products needs the comparison to be fair and documented.
  • Whole-life claims are the hardest. "Carbon neutral" or "sustainable" applied to an entire garment invites scrutiny of the full footprint, not one attribute.
  • Keep the evidence file. Certification, test reports and traceability records are what turn a claim into a defensible statement.

What importers should prepare

  • Traceability by style. Composition, yarn supplier, mill and certification reference, held in one place.
  • Certification in your own name where possible. Oeko-Tex, GRS or RWS documents should reach you as the buyer, not stop at the factory.
  • Durability evidence. Pilling, colourfastness and shrinkage test reports on the actual production construction.
  • Design for recyclability. Fewer mixed fibres in a single garment, and trims that can be separated.
  • A named owner internally. Compliance slips when it is nobody's job between seasons.

Regulations in this area change frequently and national implementations differ. This article is a general overview for buyers and is not legal advice. Always confirm requirements against the official texts and current guidance, and take advice from a qualified lawyer or compliance consultant before relying on any position described here.
