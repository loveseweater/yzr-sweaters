A Buyer's Guide to Sweater Yarn: Wool, Cotton, Acrylic & Blends

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Quick answer

Almost every commercial sweater uses one of six fibres, alone or blended: wool, merino, cashmere, cotton, acrylic and viscose. Cashmere is softest and most expensive, merino close behind for warmth and next-to-skin comfort, wool the resilient benchmark, cotton cool and smooth, viscose fluid and silky, and acrylic the cheapest and most machine-friendly but less natural in the hand. Choose by target retail price and season, and match the fibre to the market rather than starting from a look.

Pick the wrong yarn and you pay twice — once at the factory, and again in returns. Yarn sets the hand feel a customer notices in the first few seconds, the warmth they judge in winter, the price you can charge, and how long the sweater lasts. Here is how the main sweater yarns compare, and how to choose by target price and season.

The core yarns, and what each one is good at

Almost every commercial sweater is built from one of six fibres, alone or blended. Each has a distinct job. Beyond these six there are speciality fibres with their own constraints — our notes on yak and camel hair and on lyocell, hemp and ramie cover two groups worth knowing.

Wool

Wool is the benchmark for warmth and resilience. Its crimped fibres trap air, so a mid-weight wool sweater feels warmer than its thickness suggests, and natural elasticity helps it hold shape. Merino is the softer, finer version, comfortable against skin and the usual choice for premium basics. Lambswool and standard wool cost less and feel firmer. Wool suits cable knits, chunky cardigans and anything sold as genuine winter wear.

Cashmere

Cashmere, the fine undercoat of the cashmere goat, is lighter, softer and warmer per gram than wool, with a halo that reads as luxury on a shelf. It costs several times more and pills more easily under friction. Use it for hero and gift ranges, or add a small percentage to a wool blend to lift hand feel without matching the price.

Cotton

Cotton gives a smooth, matte surface with no prickle, making cotton sweaters easy to wear across seasons and popular in mild-winter markets. It breathes well, washes better than wool, and takes print, embroidery and dye beautifully. It is heavier per unit of warmth, so it suits spring/summer knits, resort ranges and trans-seasonal basics. Organic and recycled cotton are increasingly requested by eco-positioned brands.

Acrylic

Acrylic is the workhorse of volume knitwear: it mimics wool's look at a fraction of the cost, holds bright colour, resists moths and shrinks less in the wash. Its weaknesses are static, a slightly plastic hand and pilling over time. For budget ranges, fast-fashion drops and styles where colour and price matter more than a natural touch, it is the pragmatic choice.

Viscose and rayon

Viscose is a regenerated cellulose fibre with a soft, fluid drape and a subtle sheen. It adds a silky feel and lightness and helps a sweater hang rather than sit stiffly. It is usually blended — with cotton, wool or acrylic — because on its own it has limited memory and can stretch out of shape.

Blends

Most sweaters on the market are blends, and for good reason: blending tunes softness, warmth, cost and washability at once. Wool-acrylic keeps a winter look at a mid price and improves durability. Wool-cashmere adds luxury with only a small share of the costly fibre. Cotton-viscose gives drape and a premium touch in a summer knit. Blends are how you hit a precise price point.

How the fibres compare at a glance

  • Hand feel: cashmere softest; merino close behind; cotton smooth and cool; acrylic slightly synthetic; viscose fluid and silky.
  • Warmth for weight: cashmere and wool lead; acrylic insulates but feels less natural; cotton and viscose are lightest in warmth.
  • Cost: cashmere highest; merino and organic cotton mid-to-high; standard wool and cotton mid; acrylic and viscose lowest.
  • Care: acrylic and cotton most machine-friendly; wool needs gentle care; cashmere needs the most attention.
  • Best season: wool and cashmere for autumn/winter; cotton and viscose for spring/summer and trans-seasonal.

Choosing yarn by target retail price

The fastest way to narrow the choice is to work backwards from the retail price the sweater must hit. A sweater selling at an accessible high-street price rarely supports pure cashmere; it belongs in acrylic, cotton or a smart blend. A brand selling considered premium basics can justify merino or organic cotton. A luxury or gifting label can carry pure cashmere as its centrepiece.

Price is not only fibre cost. Fine-gauge knits use less yarn and ship cheaper, chunky styles use more, and embroidery or jacquard adds labour. Give your manufacturer the target retail price alongside the look you want — the yarn recommendation follows from those two together.

Matching yarn to your customer and market

  • Northern European and North American winter buyers expect real warmth: wool, wool blends, or wool-cashmere at premium tiers.
  • Mild-climate markets lean toward cotton, cotton blends and lighter acrylic for layering rather than heavy insulation.
  • Eco-positioned brands ask for organic cotton, recycled fibres, responsible wool and traceability.
  • Value and fast-fashion sellers need acrylic blends that hold colour and keep landed cost low at volume.
  • Online sellers fighting returns should favour easy-care, low-pilling yarns and state fibre content clearly on the listing.

Yarn choice is rarely permanent. A small sampling round lets you feel the actual hand, check colour under your own lighting and wash-test before you commit to a volume order.
