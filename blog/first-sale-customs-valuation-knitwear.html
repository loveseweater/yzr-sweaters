First Sale Valuation, Assists and Buying Commissions: What Sets Your Knitwear Customs Value

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

The dutiable value of an imported sweater is not automatically the number on the supplier invoice. Most systems start from transaction value — the price actually paid or payable for the goods sold for export — and then require certain additions. Assists (yarn, trims, artwork or design work you supply free or at reduced cost), royalties and some commissions are added; a genuine buying commission generally is not. Where a trading house sits between factory and importer, some markets allow the earlier factory-to-trader price, or first sale, as the basis if strict conditions are met. Ask a licensed broker before building a landed cost on any of it, and never declare anything other than the true transaction.

We see this a lot with buyers who work through a sourcing agent or a trading house and assume duty is simply a percentage of the number on their invoice. Sometimes it is. Sometimes customs adds the value of the yarn they shipped us for free, or the agent fee turns out to be treated differently from the way it was booked. None of this is exotic, and none of it is optional. Here is the plain version, from the factory side of the paperwork.

Transaction value is the starting point

In the US, the EU, the UK and most other markets, valuation begins with the price actually paid or payable for the goods when sold for export to the importing country. That is the transaction value on the commercial invoice, and it has to be the real price — payment records should match it. The base itself differs by destination: US entries generally work from the FOB price, while EU and UK entries use a CIF-style base that includes international freight and insurance. Our customs clearance walkthrough covers that difference and where it sits in the entry process.

What buyers often miss is that transaction value is a floor with additions, not a ceiling. Three of those additions matter for knitwear, and they are the subject of the rest of this article.

Assists: the things you give the factory for free

An assist is something the buyer supplies to the producer free of charge or at reduced cost for use in making the goods, and its value has to be apportioned into the customs value. In our world that is usually one of:

  • Buyer-supplied yarn or trims. If you send your own labels, buttons, zips or a special yarn and the factory does not pay for them, that value is still part of what the garment cost to make. Our CMT and free-issue guide covers the commercial side of that arrangement.
  • Design and pattern work done outside the importing country — a tech pack, a jacquard graph, artwork for placement printing — where it is supplied to the factory rather than bought with the goods.
  • Tools, blocks or programs provided to the mill, and in some cases moulds or dies for trims.

The value is normally apportioned across the production run rather than charged to one shipment, which is exactly why the paperwork has to be kept tidy from the first order. Tell your broker what you supply; do not leave them to discover a free-issue yarn invoice during an audit.

Buying commission versus selling commission

This one turns on what the agent actually does. A genuine buying commission — paid to an agent who acts for the buyer, finds suppliers and places orders on the buyer's behalf — is generally not part of the dutiable value. A selling commission paid to the seller's agent, or a markup taken by a middleman who buys and resells the goods, is a different thing. The distinction has to be visible in the documents: an agency agreement, who took title to the goods, and how the fee is invoiced. Our trading company versus factory comparison explains the two commercial models, and payment terms covers how the money actually moves. If the same company both sources for you and sells to you, get the broker to look at the structure before you file.

First sale: when an earlier price can be used

Suppose the chain is factory to trading company to importer, and the trading company adds a margin. In the US, where the first transaction was a bona fide sale clearly destined for export and conducted at arm's length, the earlier factory-to-trader price can in some circumstances be used as the customs value instead of the price you paid. The same idea exists in other forms elsewhere, but the conditions and the practice differ, and it is never automatic.

What it demands is documentation for both legs of the chain: the first-sale invoice, proof the goods were destined for export from the start, evidence of a real sale rather than an internal transfer, and a broker prepared to file on that basis. It also demands that the two prices are genuine. We will not write an invoice that says anything other than what was actually paid, and no buyer should ask for one — a valuation error discovered after entry is a penalty problem, not a paperwork problem. Put the commercial structure in the supply contract, keep the document set consistent, and let the broker decide what is claimable. Our quotation guide shows which cost lines sit where when a middleman is involved.

What it does to your landed cost

Duty is applied to the customs value, so every addition flows straight through to the duty line — and any trade-remedy duty is charged on the same number. A free-issue yarn worth a dollar a garment can add real money across a container, especially with Section 301 or AD/CVD layers stacked on top. Model it as a separate line in the landed cost calculation, confirm the HTS line and rate with your broker close to sailing, and keep a contingency. Duty reduction and drawback are separate questions again — helpful where they genuinely apply, never a substitute for declaring the correct value.

A documentation checklist from the factory side

  • One commercial invoice stating the price actually paid, with the Incoterm named — see Incoterms for knitwear.
  • A list of anything the buyer supplied free or below cost, with its value and how it was apportioned.
  • The agency agreement if an agent is involved, and a clear statement of who held title.
  • Both invoice legs if a first-sale basis is being considered.
  • Payment records that reconcile to the invoice, and descriptions that match the packing list and fibre content declarations.

Frequently asked questions

Is the FOB price on my invoice the customs value?

It is usually the starting point, not always the final number. Assists, royalties and certain commissions may have to be added, and the base differs by destination — US entries generally use FOB, EU and UK entries a CIF-style value that includes freight and insurance. Confirm the finished figure with a licensed broker.

If I supply my own yarn or labels, does that change the duty?

It can. Buyer-supplied materials or design work provided free or below cost are typically treated as assists, and their value is apportioned into the customs value. Declare them to your broker from the start and keep the invoices for the free-issue goods.

Can I use the factory price instead of the trading company price?

In some markets, including the US, an earlier first-sale price can sometimes be used where the first sale was a genuine, arm-length transaction clearly destined for export. It requires full documentation of both sales and a broker willing to file on that basis. It is never automatic, and the prices declared must be the real ones.
