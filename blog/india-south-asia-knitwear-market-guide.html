Sourcing Knitwear for the India and South Asia Market

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

South Asia is the opposite problem from Europe: enormous population, but a short, geographically concentrated winter and strong domestic manufacturing. The buyers who win in India, Bangladesh, Pakistan and Sri Lanka import what local factories do not make competitively — fashion-driven wool and blend knitwear, kids' character programmes, gifting product for the Diwali-to-wedding season — and they plan around aggressive calendars, high import duties and payment habits built on long relationships. It is a price-sharp market that rewards suppliers who take labelling details seriously.

Most of our regional guides are about markets that import everything — Germany, the Nordics, Japan. South Asia is the interesting exception: India in particular makes enormous volumes of knitwear domestically, mostly basic cotton, in hubs like Ludhiana and Tirupur that compete hard at the value end. So when a buyer in Delhi or Dhaka talks to a Chinese factory, they are shopping for something specific — and if you understand what that is, the region can be a surprisingly good, high-volume, repeat business. We see this a lot with the subcontinent accounts we serve, and the pattern is consistent.

What the region actually imports, and why

The domestic industry in India and Bangladesh dominates basic cotton knits, so imports concentrate where local production is weak:

  • Fashion wool and blend sweaters. Fine-gauge merino, lambswool, acrylic-wool fashion styles — the yarn spinning and finishing capability gap makes imports genuinely competitive here. The fibre logic in our sourcing countries comparison works in reverse: what China buys and spins cheaply, the subcontinent imports.
  • Kids' and character knitwear. Kids' knitwear with licensed characters and novelty programmes moves in serious volume for the winter gifting season — see our kids' knitwear guide for the compliance notes that still apply.
  • Spring-weight and transitional knits for the south. Southern India, Sri Lanka and coastal Pakistan barely have a winter — but they have air-conditioned offices, hill stations, and travel. Light transitional-weight knits sell there the way they sell in our Southeast Asia guide.
  • Premium gifting. Cashmere and cashmere-blend sweaters sell as wedding and corporate gifting — a demand pool that barely exists in Western markets at the same scale.

The calendar: short winter, hard deadlines

North India, Nepal, Islamabad-and-north Pakistan get a real winter — genuinely cold, November to February. That means one compressed selling season, unlike Europe's long autumn tail. The buying calendar is unforgiving: buyers place knitwear orders through the summer for Diwali-season floorsets and continue through autumn for December and January depth. Miss the Diwali window and you have effectively missed half the season — the buying calendar logic we describe for Western markets compresses into a sharper spike here. Wedding season (roughly November to February, on an astrological schedule buyers track closely) adds a second, less weather-dependent demand layer, especially on premium and embellished product.

Duties, taxes and the labelling details that bite

South Asia is a high-tariff region for apparel, and unlike the EU or US there is no free-trade pathway for a Chinese-origin sweater. India's applied duties on knitted apparel are high — commonly 20% and above depending on the tariff line, plus GST at the point of sale — so landed-cost modelling, the discipline in our landed cost guide, is not optional homework; it decides whether a programme exists. Buyers in the region know their duty rates to the decimal and expect you to engage with them on net-landed pricing rather than FOB wishful thinking.

On labelling, India has a requirement that surprises every first-time exporter: the Maximum Retail Price (MRP) must be printed on the product label under the Legal Metrology rules. Your Indian customer calculates it, but the label itself has to carry it, correctly, in the format the rules specify — and non-compliant labelling is a genuine shipment-stopping issue at Indian customs, not a theoretical risk. Alongside MRP, expect fibre-content labelling requirements and buyer-specific size conventions. Bangladesh, Pakistan and Sri Lanka have their own duty structures — Sri Lanka is notably the most open of the four, with a small but quality-led premium retail segment in Colombo.

Payment habits and commercial culture

Across the region, relationships precede terms. First orders commonly run on advance payment or letter of credit; open account terms arrive only after a history, and even then 30 days is generous. The mechanics are covered in our payment terms guide, but the cultural layer matters: subcontinent buyers negotiate hard, in depth, and expect the negotiation itself to be part of the relationship. Long WhatsApp threads, detailed counter-offers and multi-round price work are normal engagement, not delay tactics — suppliers who respond fast and in detail win disproportionate loyalty. There is also a fast-growing formal retail and e-commerce sector (large domestic e-commerce platforms, quick-commerce and modern trade chains) whose sourcing teams work to international norms and are the easiest entry point for a new exporter.

Logistics and sizing notes

Ocean freight runs mainly into Nhava Sheva (Mumbai) for western and northern India, Chennai and Tuticorin for the south, Chittagong for Bangladesh, and Karachi for Pakistan. Transit from China is short — ten days to two weeks from Shenzhen — which supports the late, compressed season, and air freight becomes genuinely rational for top-up orders in December when a style is selling. Documentation discipline matters at South Asian borders as much as anywhere; our shipping documents guide and customs clearance walkthrough apply unchanged, with the added note that paperwork errors that are a phone call fix in Rotterdam can be a week of demurrage in Chittagong.

On sizing, Indian standard body measurements run smaller than US or EU averages on average, but the market spans a very wide range. Most buyers specify their own size charts — agree them at sampling, per our sizing and grading guide, rather than defaulting to Western blocks.

Frequently asked questions

Doesn't India manufacture its own knitwear?

In enormous volume — but overwhelmingly basic cotton knits from hubs like Ludhiana and Tirupur. That is exactly why the import opportunities sit where local capability is thin: fine-gauge wool and blend fashion styles, character kids' programmes, premium cashmere for gifting. The buyers who import from China are not competing with local factories; they are filling shelves local production cannot fill competitively, and those programmes are surprisingly stable year over year.

What is the MRP requirement, and who is responsible for it?

India's Legal Metrology rules require the Maximum Retail Price to be declared on pre-packaged goods, including apparel. Your Indian customer works out the MRP figure (it depends on their duty, margin and tax position), but the physical label in the garment must carry it correctly at the time of import — an MRP error or omission is one of the most common reasons apparel consignments hit trouble at Indian customs. Treat it as a mandatory line item in the label and trim spec, confirmed in writing before bulk labels are printed.

Which countries in the region are worth starting with?

India first, by a wide margin — it has the population, the formal retail growth and the mid-market that imports fashion knitwear. Sri Lanka is small but pleasant to work with: an open, quality-led premium niche in Colombo. Bangladesh and Pakistan are dominated by their own export manufacturing industries, so import retail is thinner, though Dhaka and Karachi both have growing modern-trade chains that do import selectively. Start with the channel that matches your product, not the country with the biggest headline population.
