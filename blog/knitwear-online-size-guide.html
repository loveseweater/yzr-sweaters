Online Size Guides for Knitwear: Reducing Returns Before They Happen

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Quick answer

A knitwear size guide fails when it lists body measurements but not garment measurements, and when it ignores how much the fabric stretches. Online shoppers cannot feel the knit, so they need the finished garment measurement, the intended fit, and a note on stretch and recovery. Those three pieces of information come from the factory, which means the size guide is a supply-chain document as much as a website page.

Knitwear returns are dominated by fit, and most of those returns are predictable. A customer who orders a size that measures correctly for their body but sits wrong because the knit is designed oversized, or because the armhole is cut close, will send it back and cite "too big" or "too small" — neither of which is true in the way the size chart implied. The size guide is where that gap between spec and expectation is either closed or left open.

Why do standard size charts fail for knitwear?

Most apparel size charts list body measurements: chest, waist, hip, sometimes height. The shopper is asked to measure themselves and pick the size whose body measurements match. That logic works for woven garments with a stable fit, and it breaks down for knitwear for three reasons.

Ease varies by design, not by size. Two sweaters in the same nominal size can have five centimetres of difference in chest width because one is designed close-fitting and one is designed oversized. A body-measurement chart cannot express that, so the customer's correct body size maps to two different garments and only one of them will suit.

The fabric moves. A ribbed knit and a cable knit in the same fibre stretch very differently. A body chart assumes the garment will sit where the tape said, which is not how a knit behaves.

Knitwear is worn over layers. A customer buying a fine merino crew neck to wear over a shirt needs different room than one buying the same sweater as a base layer. The intended use changes the right size, and the chart does not know.

What should a knitwear size guide actually contain?

Three layers of information, in order of how much they help.

  • Finished garment measurements. The actual measurement of the garment laid flat, per size, for chest, body length, sleeve and shoulder. This is the single most useful thing a shopper can have, because it lets them measure a sweater they already own and love. Our notes on measuring a sweater explain how those figures are taken and why the method matters.
  • The intended fit, stated plainly. "Close-fitting, sits at the hip" or "Relaxed, drops 3cm off the shoulder". A shopper who knows the design intent can decide whether that is the look they want, rather than guessing from a photo.
  • Stretch and recovery. Whether the knit has mechanical stretch from ribbing, whether it contains elastane, and how much the measurements can be expected to move. This is the information that turns a flat measurement into a realistic expectation.

Body measurements still belong on the page as a fallback, but they should support the garment measurements rather than replace them.

Which data has to come from the factory?

All of the numbers that matter, which is why the size guide cannot be written by a marketing team alone.

The finished measurements per size come from the approved sample and the graded spec. The fit intent comes from the block and the design brief. The stretch behaviour comes from the yarn, the gauge and the knit structure. Our notes on sizing and grading cover how the chart is built, and knit gauge explains how gauge changes the fabric's character.

The practical ask to a supplier is straightforward: the measured size chart for the approved sample, the grading increments, and a statement of whether the knit is stable, stretchy or very elastic. A supplier who cannot produce that has not measured their own product, which is worth knowing before a season rather than after.

Our notes on fit sessions and pattern blocks explain where that measured data originates, and the bill of materials is where the component specification that drives stiffness lives.

How should the size guide be presented?

Lead with the garment measurement table, not the body chart. Put the body chart below it as guidance rather than as the primary tool.

Show the measurement in context. A statement like "body length 62cm — sits at the high hip on a 170cm model" converts a number into an expectation. Our notes on knitwear photography explain how the model shots and the measurements work together, since the photograph shows the fit and the table quantifies it.

Name the stretch honestly. "This rib knit stretches to fit and recovers" is usable information. "True to size" is not, because it tells the shopper nothing they can act on.

Give one clear recommendation per size. A guide that says "if you are between sizes, take the larger for a relaxed fit" answers the question the shopper actually has, and it is more effective than a long explanation.

How do you know the guide is working?

By returns data, split by reason. Returns coded "too small" or "too big" on a specific style point at a size guide problem or a grading problem, and the two are distinguishable: if the returns cluster on one size, the grading is suspect; if they are spread across sizes, the guide is probably not communicating the fit.

Returns coded "not as described" or "different from photo" point at the photography and copy rather than the numbers. Our notes on reducing consumer returns cover the wider set of causes and which ones a brand can actually control.

The useful habit is to review returns by style and reason at the end of each season, and to feed the findings back into the size guide before the next order. A guide that never changes is a guide nobody is learning from.

What should a buyer ask a supplier for?

  • The measured chart for the approved sample, with tolerances, so the guide reflects real garments rather than a nominal spec. Our notes on quality standards documents explain where those tolerances are recorded.
  • The grading increments per point, so the extreme sizes in the guide are accurate rather than extrapolated.
  • A statement on stretch, in plain language, covering ribbing, elastane content and expected recovery.
  • Model fit information, including the model's height and the size worn, which is what makes the photographs interpretable.
  • Consistency of fit across a range, so the same size means the same thing across styles — a subject our notes on range planning touch on.
  • Fabric weight, since it affects how a garment hangs and how much room it needs. Our notes on fabric weight and GSM explain the measure.

Frequently asked questions

Should a knitwear size guide use body or garment measurements?

Garment measurements should lead, because they let a shopper measure a sweater they already own and compare directly. Body measurements are a useful fallback but cannot express design ease, so a body-only chart gives the same recommendation for a close-fitting and an oversized style in the same nominal size.

Why do customers return knitwear for fit more than other categories?

Because knitwear's fit depends on ease and fabric behaviour in ways a photograph cannot convey. A knit that is designed oversized or cut close will look similar in a flat image and feel completely different to wear, so the customer's only reliable guide is finished measurements plus a clear statement of intended fit and stretch.

How often should a size guide be updated?

Whenever the grading, the yarn or the construction changes, and at least once a season after reviewing returns by style and reason. A guide carried over unchanged across styles with different blocks will drift out of accuracy, which is exactly when fit returns start to cluster.
