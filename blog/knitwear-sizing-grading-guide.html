Knitwear Sizing & Grading: Getting the Fit Right for Your Market

Views: 0

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Sizing is one of the quietest ways a knitwear order fails. A sweater can be beautifully made from the right yarn and still come back as a return or a chargeback because the fit ran small for one market. If you buy, import or design sweaters, the size chart and the grading behind it are a sourcing decision, not a design afterthought. Here is how to get them right before you place a bulk order.

Why sizing is a sourcing decision

Knitwear stretches and relaxes, so the measurements on a finished garment depend on yarn, stitch, washing and finishing — not just the paper pattern. That means sizing has to be agreed with your factory at the sampling stage, and confirmed against a measured sample, rather than copied from an old spreadsheet. When buyers treat sizing as a last-minute detail, problems surface only after thousands of pieces are packed.

How sizing differs across markets

The same nominal label means different things in different regions. A US size M is not an EU size M, and both are larger than a typical Asian M. Body shapes differ too: EU and US ranges often need more length in the body and sleeve, while some Asian ranges are cut shorter and closer.

  • US: often labelled XS–XXL with generous body length and roomier chests.
  • EU: numbered sizes (36, 38, 40), longer sleeves and body, closer shoulders.
  • UK: its own numeric scale, frequently between US and EU cuts.
  • Asia: generally smaller and shorter; often needs upsizing before export.

If you sell across regions, decide early whether you will use one base fit with adjusted charts or separate fits per market. The honest approach is to build the chart around your actual customer, not around a nominal label.

Building a size chart buyers can trust

A useful knitwear size chart lists every key point of measure, not just chest width. For sweaters and cardigans, that usually means:

  • Chest / body width (1 inch below armhole)
  • Body length (from high point shoulder)
  • Shoulder width (seam to seam)
  • Sleeve length and cuff opening
  • Neck width and neck drop
  • Bottom / hem width

State the measurement method and the tolerances clearly. A chart without tolerances invites arguments later. Reasonable knit tolerances are usually given as a small plus/minus figure per point, and they should be written into the spec sheet so both sides measure the same way.

How grading actually works

Grading is the step from one base size to the full size run. Each point of measure increases (or decreases) by a set increment — for example, chest gains a fixed amount per size while body length gains a smaller amount. Good grading is not simply scaling everything up; different points move at different rates so the garment keeps its intended fit and balance.

Two practical rules help:

  • Grade from a base size you have already fitted and approved on a real sample.
  • Check the extremes of the range, not just the middle. The smallest and largest sizes are where grading errors show.

Kids, plus sizes and extended ranges

Children's knitwear grades on age-based charts with their own safety and fit conventions, and the increments are smaller and more frequent. Plus and extended sizes are not just larger bodies — the grade often needs a different rate above a certain point so shoulders, necklines and armholes stay proportionate. If you plan a wide range, flag the smallest and largest sizes for a fit sample rather than relying on a formula.

Aligning sizing with your factory

The most reliable way to avoid size problems is a clear handoff: a written spec sheet with points of measure, tolerances and grading rules; a size-set sample for the base size; then a production sample measured against the approved chart before bulk runs.
