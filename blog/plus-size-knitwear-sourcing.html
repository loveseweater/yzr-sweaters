Plus-Size Knitwear: A Sourcing and Product Development Guide

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Plus-size knitwear is no longer a token few styles at the end of a range. Industry observation across the US, UK and EU markets points the same way: curve and extended sizes keep growing while many brands still under-serve them. The buyers who treat this segment as its own product development exercise — not an add-on to the standard size run — are the ones who see repeat business. Here is what changes when you source size-inclusive knitwear.

Why enlarging a standard pattern fails

Spreading a few centimetres evenly across a base size is the most common shortcut in curve knitwear, and it is the main reason extended-size sweaters fit badly. The proportions of a body do not scale linearly, so the garment should not either.

  • Shoulder and neckline — the shoulder point drifts outward and the neckline gapes. The shoulder slope and relative width usually need to be re-set rather than extended.
  • Armhole depth and sleeve head — scaled armholes tend to sit too shallow or too deep, and the bicep width has to be calculated separately from the sleeve length.
  • Waist-to-hip relationship — the difference between bust, waist and hip is not a constant. A block drawn for a straight silhouette pulls at the hip or leaves excess fabric at the waist.
  • Length and hem balance — adding length only at the bottom drops the hem past the intended line and changes how the hem band reads against the body.

None of this is exotic. It simply means the curve range needs its own block, developed and fitted on its own terms.

Categories that work in extended sizes

  • Mock-neck and crew pullovers in mid-weight yarn — a forgiving neckline, easy layering, strong repeat purchase.
  • Open-front cardigans — worn open they create a vertical line and tolerate a wider fit window, which reduces return risk.
  • Long-line knit dresses and tunics — drape over the hip is the selling point, so length strategy across heights has to be decided early.
  • Relaxed, softly dropped silhouettes — deliberately generous but still structured enough to hold shape rather than hang.

Heavily cropped shapes, rigid double-thick ribs and very chunky constructions tend to work against the segment, because they add volume exactly where customers do not want it.

Yarn and stitch choices

Drape matters more than warmth alone. All-over chunky knits read as volume, while finer and mid-gauge yarns with a soft hand tend to skim the body instead of sitting on it. Viscose, modal and cotton blends, and smoother wool or cashmere blends, generally suit curve ranges well; a yarn with good recovery keeps the garment in shape after a day of wear.

On structure, vertical stitch lines, narrow ribs and fine all-over textures tend to be more flattering than wide horizontal patterns. Weight should be enough to be opaque and hold the silhouette without hanging like a sack — the mid-weight band, in an elastic yarn, usually does both jobs. Test the knit on a real body, never on a hanger.

Pattern development is a separate project

  • Commission a curve block from a pattern maker who works on real bodies, or at minimum insist on dedicated fit samples in two curve sizes.
  • Use fit models who reflect your target customer, not a standard sample size with padding.
  • Decide the length strategy: a single length that works across heights, or a regular and a longer option.
  • Set tolerances that account for the way knits relax and grow with wear.

Treat the curve block as an asset. Once it is right, every subsequent style in the range starts from a known fit instead of a fresh guess.

Test the market before committing to a full size run

Extended size runs do not follow a standard ratio, and the pattern of demand varies by market, age group and product type. Rather than opening a full size run on the first order, many buyers test two or three size breaks, read the sell-through, and build a real ratio for the second order. That approach also keeps the initial MOQ reasonable, particularly when the yarn and colour are shared with a style already in production.

Briefing a supplier on curve sizes

  • Send a measurable spec: height, bust, waist, hip, and where you want the hem to land on the body.
  • Be specific in fit comments — armhole, shoulder, hip, sleeve — rather than "too tight".
  • Share fitting photos and notes from real wearers where you can.
  • Plan two fit rounds, a proto and a size set, before bulk.
