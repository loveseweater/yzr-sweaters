Polyester in Knitwear: A Buyer's Guide to the Workhorse Fibre

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Polyester is the most-used and least-discussed fibre in knitwear. Two decisions drive almost everything: filament or spun yarn, and virgin or recycled. Spun polyester looks and feels like cotton but pills more unless you pay for anti-pill grades; filament is smooth, strong and cheap but reads "synthetic" on the hand. In blends, 20–30% polyester measurably improves shape retention and wash durability at almost no cost penalty — which is why it turns up unrequested in quotes unless you specify against it.

Every buyer we meet can talk for an hour about merino grades or cashmere micron. Almost nobody wants to talk about polyester, even though it is in more of the world's sweaters than wool is. We see this a lot: a brand specifies "80% wool 20% polyester" because a competitor's label says that, but cannot tell us whether they want the polyester filament or spun, regular or modified, virgin or recycled. This guide is the missing conversation — written for buyers who want their polyester decisions made on purpose.

Why polyester is everywhere in knitwear

Three commercial facts put polyester in nearly every knitwear programme. It is cheap and its price is steadier than wool or cotton — the volatility that keeps wool budgets moving up and down barely touches it. It is strong: a small share of polyester visibly extends the life of a garment through wash cycles. And it takes dye cleanly and holds colour. For entry-level retail price points, 100% spun polyester knitwear is the honest workhorse — a category where acrylic and polyester compete directly, with acrylic winning on wool-like loft and polyester winning on durability and softness after repeated washing.

The fibre itself is the same polymer family as the nylon in your sportswear. Where nylon was engineered for toughness, polyester was optimised for cost, dyeability and crease resistance — the reasons it dominates volume apparel. None of this is a quality confession. Bad polyester exists, but so does bad merino; the difference is that buyers know to interrogate wool specifications and let polyester slide through unspecified.

Filament or spun: the decision that shapes everything

Polyester arrives at a knitting mill in one of two forms, and they knit into recognisably different products. Filament yarn is continuous strands spun straight from the polymer — smooth, glossy, extremely regular. In knitwear it appears as shiny rib trims, lining-soft fine gauge pieces and athletic styles; on its own it feels slippy and reads cheap in a sweater context, but blended at low percentages it adds strength invisibly.

Spun polyester is cut into staple fibres and spun like cotton, with a matte, slightly fuzzy surface. This is the version used to imitate cotton and wool in casual knitwear — crews, cardigans, kids' styles. It is the one that behaves differently by grade: longer staple and tighter twist give a smoother, stronger yarn; short staple, loosely twisted is the bargain-bin version that pills within weeks. When a supplier quotes "100% polyester" for a cotton-look knit without stating spun or filament, ask — and then ask again about staple length, because the two quotes that look identical on paper can differ by a season of wear.

What the 20–30% in your blend actually does

Wool-polyester and cotton-polyester blends are the quiet majority of mid-market knitwear, and the percentages are not arbitrary. Around 20–30% polyester adds enough tensile strength to reduce seam slippage and bagging at elbows and seat, stabilises the knit so it recovers better after washing, and cuts the fibre cost meaningfully. Consumers experience it as a sweater that keeps its shape through the season. What it trades away is a little of the natural fibre's breathability and hand — a 70/30 wool-poly sweater is not a 100% wool sweater to a trained hand, whatever the marketing says.

The failure mode we see most often is undeclared blending. A quote for "100% cotton" comes back testing 88% cotton / 12% polyester — sometimes deliberate substitution, sometimes contaminated spinning lines. Either way it becomes a lab result you discover at destination. This is why composition verification belongs in every order: lock the blend in the bill of materials, and test to it at lab stage, with the tolerance rules we cover in our fibre composition tolerance guide.

Recycled polyester: what you are actually buying

Recycled polyester (rPET) is now the default sustainability claim in synthetic knitwear, and most of it is still made from melted bottle flakes rather than old garments. Chemically it performs close to virgin fibre in knitwear applications; the honest differences are a slightly less consistent dye uptake on some lots and a small price premium. The real diligence work is documentation. If your marketing team wants "recycled" on a hangtag, you need a chain: transaction certificates from the recycler through the spinner to your knitter, normally under GRS certification, and a claim that stops where the paperwork stops.

Two cautions from experience. First, "recycled content" percentages get rounded optimistically — if the claim matters, specify the minimum verified percentage, not an average. Second, do not mix claims: an rPET knit that sheds microfibres in every wash is still a plastic-shedding knit, a tension our microfibre shedding guide discusses in full. Buyers positioning on sustainability should read both the certificate and the small print.

Dyeing: the hidden schedule item

Polyester dyes with disperse dyes at high temperature and pressure — different chemistry, different machinery, different batch behaviour from the acid dyes used on wool or reactive dyes on cotton. In practice this matters two ways. If your programme mixes fibre types — a polyester body with wool sleeves, or contrast trims — the two parts dye separately and must be shade-matched across two different dye lines, which is exactly the cross-component colour problem in our colour consistency guide. And lab dips on polyester run on their own lead time, so a delayed colour approval on the poly component delays the whole order, not just the trim.

The upside is genuine: polyester's colour fastness to washing and light is excellent, which is why bright melange and marl effects hold season after season on poly blends. For dark saturated colours on light polygrounds, check for thermomigration — dye moving into the surface over time, especially after heat from pressing — by requesting a fastness-to-sublimation test on dark/light combinations.

The quality problems polyester actually gives you

  • Pilling on spun grades. The classic complaint. Short-staple spun polyester forms pills that the stronger yarn then holds onto instead of shedding, so they accumulate. Anti-pill grades and higher twist fix it at a cost — worth paying for anything positioned above entry level, as our pilling prevention guide explains.
  • Static. Dry synthetic surfaces build charge, especially in winter retail and cold-climate wear. Fabric softeners at finishing and a natural-fibre blend share both reduce it; for static-sensitive markets see our anti-static fixes.
  • Harsh hand. Cheap filament-dominant knits feel scratchy-plastic. The fixes are yarn-side (microfibre, modified cross-sections) or finish-side (softener routes), not stitch-side.
  • Snagging. Fine filament knits snag on rings and Velcro and show every pulled loop. If your customer profile is hard on garments, weigh this against the strength benefit.

What to write in the specification

When you move polyester from "whatever the factory had" to a specified component, four lines cover it: form (filament or spun, and if spun, the staple length class), blend percentage with tolerance, virgin or recycled with the certification chain if recycled, and a pilling requirement expressed as a tested grade (for example, a minimum rating after a fixed number of abrasion cycles) rather than the word "anti-pill". Add disperse-dye lab dip timing to your calendar if polyester is a main component, and your order stops being at the mercy of whichever lot the spinner shipped.

Frequently asked questions

Does polyester in a blend mean the factory is cutting corners?

No — a declared wool-polyester blend at 70/30 or 80/20 is a legitimate engineering choice for shape retention, wash durability and price, and many premium brands use it deliberately. The problem is undeclared or under-declared polyester, which you catch with composition testing against the agreed specification. If the blend is on the label and matches the lab report, it is a design decision, not a substitution.

Is recycled polyester really better for the environment?

It reduces demand for virgin petroleum-based fibre and diverts bottle waste, which is a real if modest benefit. It does not make the garment biodegradable, and microfibre shedding persists, so the claim needs careful wording. Buy it with a GRS transaction certificate trail and keep the claim specific: "recycled polyester content verified at X%" holds up better than a vague sustainability halo.

Why does my 100% polyester knit pill worse than a cotton one?

Because it was almost certainly spun from short-staple fibre with low twist. Pills form when loose fibre ends tangle, and strong polyester yarns hold those pills instead of letting them fall off as weaker cotton does. The fix is upstream: longer staple, higher twist, or a certified anti-pill grade — all available at a modest premium that is far cheaper than returns.
