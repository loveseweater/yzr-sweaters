Split Shipments and Staggered Delivery: Matching Knitwear Stock to the Season

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

A split shipment divides one knitwear order into two or more deliveries — by style, colour or size ratio — shipped weeks apart. It frees warehouse space, spreads duty and freight over the season, and puts stock on shelves closer to when it sells. It also doubles documentation, risks dye-lot differences between drops, and can cost more in freight. It pays when the schedule is agreed before production starts; it hurts when it is improvised mid-order.

Almost every experienced knitwear buyer we supply runs some version of phased delivery, and almost every first-time buyer discovers the need two weeks before their single planned shipment — when their warehouse says the September drop cannot be absorbed, or their cash flow says the duty bill cannot either. We see this a lot. Split shipments are one of the most sensible tools in seasonal buying, and also one of the most casually abused. Here is how to use the tool properly.

Why buyers split knitwear orders

The reasons cluster into four groups. Cash flow: a 3,000-piece order shipped in two drops roughly halves the duty, freight and inventory-financing bill at the first entry, with the second half paid nearer the moment it sells — the same logic that makes bonded warehousing attractive, achieved without a customs facility. Warehouse and shelf capacity: small retailers and boutique chains physically cannot receive an entire autumn programme at once. Risk staging: getting the first tranche into stores early tells you which colours and sizes to push before the second tranche is even packed — a soft form of the testing we describe in inventory replenishment planning. Selling season: lightweight transitional styles sell in September; heavy gauge sells from November, and there is no business case for both sitting in your warehouse in August.

What splitting actually costs at the factory

Buyers often ask for split deliveries as if they were free, so let us be specific about what changes on our side. A second shipment means a second set of export documentation, a second booking and a second customs declaration — modest, but real. More significant is packing and finishing in two waves: garments finished early must be stored properly while the balance is knitted, which involves climate-controlled space and handling, because knitwear held through a humid Guangdong summer without care is how you get the problems described in mould and mildew during storage and shipping.

The cost most buyers never see is schedule risk. A factory plans capacity in machine-weeks; an order split "60% now, 40% in six weeks" leaves the 40% competing for machines against new orders booked in the interim. That is why we treat a split schedule as a production commitment — agreed at capacity booking, not requested at shipment. And freight economics can bite: two part-volume shipments, particularly two LCL consignments instead of one full container, can cost more total freight than a single consolidated load. Whether that matters depends on how your LCL and FCL volumes compare — run both scenarios before committing to the split.

The consistency risk nobody prices in

Here is the issue unique to knitwear: a split order usually means two dye lots. Tranche one is knitted and dyed first; by the time tranche two runs, the first dye lot may be exhausted and the dye house starts a new bath. Garments from the two drops can sit a half-shade apart — individually fine, side by side on a rail, noticeable. A customer who bought the September drop and returns for a second size in November will see the difference in daylight.

This is manageable, but only if it is planned. We hold yarn from one dye lot where the order allows, or dye the full order in one campaign and split at packing instead of at knitting — which raises storage time but guarantees shade consistency across drops. Buyers should decide explicitly which they prefer: splitting at production (cheaper, shade risk) or splitting at packing (consistent, needs storage planning). Raising this at the pre-production meeting takes ten minutes and prevents the worst kind of complaint — the one where both parties behaved reasonably and the outcome is still bad.

Negotiating the terms: what a fair split agreement contains

  • Split points fixed before production. By style, colour or size ratio, with quantities per drop — not "we'll decide later".
  • Payment follows shipment. The deposit covers the order, but each balance tranche is due against its own shipped documents, which keeps the factory's working capital and yours symmetric. Our piece on payment terms for apparel orders covers standard structures.
  • Dye-lot strategy stated. One lot held for both drops, or per-lot approval — buyer's choice, in writing.
  • Storage responsibility and duration. Who holds goods between drops, for how long, and what happens if the second shipment is delayed by the buyer — factories cannot store finished goods indefinitely at no charge.
  • Freight comparison on the record. One FCL versus two LCL, priced both ways, so nobody discovers the premium after the fact. Your freight forwarder will quote both scenarios in minutes.
  • Documentation per shipment. Each drop gets its own full set — invoice, packing list, certificates — because each clears customs separately. Our shipping documents guide lists the set.

When not to split

Do not split when the second tranche is less than roughly 15–20% of the order: the fixed costs of a second shipment — documentation, handling, booking, minimum freight charges — land on too few garments, and the per-unit cost of the split exceeds the financing it saves. Do not split to chase a discount the factory did not give you on the first drop either; some buyers use a promised "second shipment" as a price lever with no intention of ordering it, and factories price the third order of anyone who does that accordingly. And if your season is short — six weeks of selling, then markdown — the second drop usually arrives after the moment it was meant for. Staggered delivery rewards buyers with a genuine rolling season, not a compressed one.

Frequently asked questions

Does a split shipment cost more than shipping everything at once?

Usually yes, but not always by much. The extra costs are a second set of documents and bookings, and freight — two part-volume consignments generally cost more than one consolidated load, though two well-filled containers can match a single one. Get your forwarder to price the split versus single options on your actual volumes before deciding; on large orders the freight penalty is often small relative to the cash-flow benefit.

How do I keep colours consistent across two delivery drops?

Agree the strategy before production: either hold yarn from a single dye lot for both drops, or dye the whole order at once and split at packing. Both cost something — the first limits production flexibility, the second extends storage time — but both beat discovering a half-shade gap between your September and November stock with garments already on sale.

Can I decide the split quantities after production starts?

You can ask, but expect the factory to resist, and rightly so — machine scheduling, dye campaigns and storage are all planned around the agreed split. Late changes usually mean re-booking capacity or re-dyeing, both of which cost money and time. Fix the split points at or before the pre-production meeting; if you genuinely need flexibility, agree a bounded range for each drop upfront rather than reopening the schedule mid-order.
