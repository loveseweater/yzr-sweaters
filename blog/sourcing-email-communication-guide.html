Communicating with a Chinese Knitwear Supplier: Email That Gets Results

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Most problems in a knitwear order are communication problems wearing a technical disguise. A colour that drifts, a size set that is short, a delivery date that slips — they usually trace back to something assumed rather than written down. Supplier communication in China is a learnable skill, and a few habits will cut rework more than any discount.

Write a first enquiry that can be quoted

A vague enquiry gets a vague price, and vague prices are useless for budgeting. A supplier can only quote accurately when they know what they are making.

  • Style reference — a photo, tech pack or sample, plus the product type (crew neck, cardigan, polo, dress).
  • Yarn or composition — merino, cotton, cashmere blend, acrylic; fibre percentages if you have them.
  • Gauge and weight — 3GG, 7GG, 12GG, or a target GSM if you think in fabric weight.
  • Colours — Pantone references and lab dip requests, or the number of colourways under consideration.
  • Size range and ratio — for example S–XL with a stated packing ratio per size.
  • Quantity — per style and per colourway, plus your expectation for repeats.
  • Delivery window — destination port or warehouse, and the date goods must land, not just ship.
  • Label and packaging needs — main label, care label, hangtag, polybag, carton marks.

Send it as a numbered list with attachments rather than a paragraph. If details are still open, say so and ask what the supplier needs to firm up the price.

Time zones and realistic response windows

Most knitwear factories in Guangdong work on GMT+8, roughly seven hours ahead of London and twelve ahead of New York. An enquiry sent at the end of your working day may already be past their office hours. Expect replies within one to two business days, and be patient during China's public holidays — Chinese New Year is the big one, when factories may close for around two weeks and reopen with a backlog.

State your own time zone and preferred reply window once, and keep the conversation in one email thread. Many suppliers use WeChat for quick confirmations; that is fine for speed, but anything that changes price, quantity or spec should be repeated by email.

Keep the language simple and specific

Most export-facing staff read English well and write it less confidently, so clarity beats politeness formulas.

  • One subject per email. A message mixing sampling, pricing and shipping gets one answer and two forgotten questions.
  • Short sentences and plain words. "Please confirm the delivery date" beats "We would appreciate your kind confirmation at your earliest convenience".
  • Numbers, not adjectives. "Bulk must be within 3% of the approved lab dip" is workable; "colour must be very close to the sample" is not.
  • Ask one question at a time when a decision is needed, and use bullet lists for multiple points.
  • Confirm dates explicitly — write "12 October 2026" rather than "10/12", which is ambiguous across regions.

How to send revision comments

Revision rounds go wrong when the supplier has to guess which sample was meant. Make each comment impossible to misread.

  • Attach a marked-up image — arrows, circles and a short note on the photo itself.
  • Give measurements in a table: point of measure, approved figure, sample figure, tolerance.
  • Say which points are blockers for approval and which are minor, so the next round is not spent on the wrong priority.
  • Reference the sample by its tag number so both sides discuss the same garment.

Put important things in writing

Spoken agreements on a call or at a trade fair are convenient and easy to misremember. Price, quantity, gauge, yarn composition, colour approval, delivery date and payment schedule should all appear in writing, ideally in the order confirmation or contract. If a call settles something, send a two-line email afterwards: "Confirming what we agreed today — delivery 20 October, 400 pcs in four colourways, no change to yarn."

Common misunderstandings and how to defuse them

  • Delivery versus shipment. Suppliers often quote ex-factory or shipment date; buyers plan around arrival. Agree which date you mean and allow for transit.
  • Gauge confusion. The same "12GG" can be understood differently; send a photo of a garment you like and ask the supplier to confirm.
  • Quantity versus order quantity. An MOQ quote may not cover all sizes and colours; ask for the minimum per colour separately.
  • Yes as acknowledgement. A quick "yes" may mean "received", not "agreed". Ask for the specific figure back in writing.
  • Silence during production. Factories go quiet while busy. Agree upfront on weekly updates and pre-shipment photos.

Habits that build a long-term working relationship

The strongest buyer-supplier relationships in knitwear are boring in the best way: consistent specs, predictable volumes, and problems raised early. Pay on time, give realistic lead times, avoid changing details after yarn is ordered, and share your season plan so the supplier can hold capacity. In return you get honest warnings about yarn shortages — worth far more than a last-minute discount. A named contact on both sides and a written summary after every call will keep a program running for years.
