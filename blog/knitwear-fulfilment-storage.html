Knitwear Fulfilment: Storing and Shipping Stock

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Knitwear cannot be stored like flat-packed apparel. It is bulky, it deforms under its own weight, it is vulnerable to humidity and pests, and it costs real money to hold per cubic metre. Fulfilment decisions — how cartons are stacked, what humidity is maintained, how stock is picked and how returns are handled — change both the condition of the goods and the cost per order. The failure mode is usually a garment that arrives creased, stretched or musty rather than one that is missing.

Most buyers think about fulfilment as a logistics problem and discover it is a product problem. A sweater that sat at the bottom of a stack for eight weeks arrives with a flattened shoulder. One stored in a humid corner arrives smelling of damp. One picked from a mixed carton with the wrong shade ships a slightly different cream and comes back. None of this shows in a stock report, and all of it costs.

Why is knitwear harder to store than other apparel?

Four physical properties, each with a consequence.

  • Bulk. A sweater occupies far more cubic space than the same value of T-shirts, so storage cost per unit is high and freight is charged on volume rather than weight. Our notes on sea freight cover why volumetric charging matters.
  • Compression set. Knitted fabric held under weight for weeks takes a crease that does not fully recover. Stacking a pallet high compresses the bottom cartons.
  • Deformation. A garment hanging on a hanger for months grows at the shoulders and loses shape. Our notes on caring for knitwear cover the same principle for the end customer.
  • Moisture sensitivity. Wool and cellulosic fibres absorb moisture readily, and a damp environment risks mould, odour and yellowing. Our notes on mould and mildew and yellowing in storage cover the two failure modes.

What storage conditions actually matter?

Humidity and temperature. A dry, stable environment is the single most important condition. Fluctuating temperature causes condensation, and condensation is what turns a merely humid store into a mould problem. Cartons should not sit directly on concrete floors or against exterior walls, where condensation forms.

Stack height. Limit the number of cartons stacked, and consider shelving for long-held stock so the bottom layer is not permanently compressed. This is a cost trade-off — shelving uses more floor space per unit — but it protects the goods.

Pest control. Wool and natural fibres attract moths and other insects. Our notes on repair services touch on moth damage from the customer side; in a warehouse the control is cleanliness, sealed cartons and a pest programme.

Light. Prolonged light exposure shifts pale shades and degrades some dyes. Our notes on colour fastness cover the light dimension.

What goes wrong in picking and packing?

Knitwear has a specific set of picking hazards that general apparel 3PLs do not always anticipate.

Shade-lot mixing. A warehouse holding two lots of the same cream shade will pick whichever is nearest unless the system segregates them. The customer receives a garment that does not match the one they bought last month. Our notes on colour consistency explain why lots differ and how segregation prevents the problem.

Size substitution. Because knitwear folds small, a picker working quickly can grab the adjacent size. Barcode scanning at pick resolves this; visual checking does not.

Compression in transit. A sweater shipped in a thin mailer without protection arrives creased. Our notes on packaging and shipping cover the carton and inner-pack choices.

Return handling. A returned sweater has usually been worn, washed or stored badly. It needs inspection, refolding and repacking before it can be resold, and that labour is a real cost against the return. Our notes on reducing consumer returns cover the upstream prevention.

What does holding stock actually cost?

More than the storage invoice suggests. Five components compound.

The space itself — at a cubic rate that is unfavourable for bulky goods. The capital tied up in goods that are not selling. The risk of markdown, which rises the longer stock sits, and which our notes on dead stock cover. Condition deterioration, since a garment stored for a season is worth less than one stored for a month even if it is not visibly damaged. And the cost of obsolescence, because a seasonal colour that misses its window is worth far less next year.

The commercial conclusion is that knitwear rewards a fast, shallow inventory model over a deep one, which is why our notes on first order versus reorder argue for width over depth on a first buy.

How should a buyer set up fulfilment?

  • Ask any prospective 3PL whether they handle knitwear specifically, and what they do differently. A warehouse that stores only flat-packed goods will stack your cartons the same way.
  • Specify stack limits and shelving for long-held or premium stock, in writing.
  • Require lot segregation and lot marking at carton level, so the picking system can distinguish two lots of the same shade. Our notes on shipping documents cover the carton marking.
  • Insist on scan-based picking rather than visual, particularly where sizes are similar and colours close.
  • Set a humidity and temperature expectation and ask how it is monitored.
  • Plan the return process before the first order ships, including who inspects, refolds and repacks.

What should a brand do about storing versus drop-shipping?

Holding stock gives control over condition, presentation and delivery speed. Drop-shipping from the factory avoids the storage cost but transfers the packing and the customer experience to the supplier, and the transit is longer.

The practical middle ground for a small brand is to hold a shallow buffer of the styles that sell and to have a reorder structure in place for depth — which is the model our notes on inventory planning describe. What does not work is holding deep stock of a broad range on the assumption that storage is cheap; for knitwear it is not, in either money or condition.

Frequently asked questions

How should knitwear be stored in a warehouse?

Dry, stable, and protected from compression. Keep cartons off concrete floors and away from exterior walls where condensation forms, limit stack height so the bottom layer is not permanently compressed, segregate dye lots, and control pests since wool attracts insects. For long-held or premium stock, shelving rather than floor stacking protects the goods at the cost of floor space.

Why do my sweaters arrive creased from storage?

Usually compression set — knitted fabric held under weight for weeks takes a crease that does not fully recover. It comes from over-stacked pallets or from thin transit packaging. The controls are stack limits in the warehouse, shelving for slow-moving stock, and adequate inner packing for the shipment itself.

Is it cheaper to drop-ship knitwear or hold stock?

Drop-shipping avoids storage cost but hands the packing and customer experience to the supplier, and extends transit. Holding stock gives control over condition and speed but costs money in space, capital and deterioration, and knitwear is bulky so the space cost is high per unit. Most small brands do best holding a shallow buffer of proven styles and reordering depth.
