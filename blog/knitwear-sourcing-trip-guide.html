Planning a Knitwear Sourcing Trip or Trade Show Visit

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

A week in Guangdong can replace six months of email. Buyers who visit knitwear factories in person make faster decisions, catch problems photographs hide, and negotiate with better information. But an unplanned trip turns into a blur of taxis and business cards. Here is how to structure a sourcing trip or trade show visit so you come home with a real supplier shortlist.

Why the trip is worth the cost

You cannot feel a sweater over email. A factory visit answers questions a catalogue never will: is the knitting floor actually running, are the linking and mending stations staffed, is there a separate sample room, and does QC keep written records or just a table and a lamp? The floor is also the fastest way to learn which gauges a factory is genuinely strong at — and which it quietly subcontracts. Factories also prioritise buyers they have met, which makes sampling, MOQ and payment terms easier to raise face to face.

Trade shows: how to work them efficiently

Shows such as the Canton Fair in Guangzhou and the Hong Kong fashion and knitwear fairs concentrate hundreds of suppliers into a few halls. The mistake is treating them as a browsing exercise.

  • Book before you fly. Register early, download the floor plan and mark the knitwear halls. Booths are grouped by category, so plan a route rather than wandering.
  • Bring a one-page profile. Your brand, market, categories, typical order quantity and timeline. It saves ten minutes of explanation at every booth.
  • Target a realistic number. Twelve to fifteen meaningful conversations in a day is a good result. Depth beats coverage.
  • Ask the qualifying questions early. Gauge capability, MOQ per style per colour, sampling lead time, in-house knitting versus outsourcing, certification status.
  • Collect a physical sample. Swatches beat brochures when you compare suppliers later.
  • Log each booth the same day, photographing the business card beside your notes, or you will not remember who claimed what.

For unhurried conversations with senior staff, go in the afternoon or on a middle day.

What to inspect on a factory visit

Ask for a full walk-through, not a showroom tour. Reluctance to show you the following is itself information.

  • Knitting floor — machine gauge and brand, how many machines, whether they are running, and how yarn is separated by lot.
  • Linking and seaming — hand-linking skill shows here. Look at how many operators there are and how tidy the work in progress is.
  • Mending and finishing — good factories have dedicated mending lights and a pressing area. Cheap factories skip this and hide it in the price.
  • Quality control — inspection tables, light boxes, measurement charts, and a documented AQL process.
  • Sample room — how many sample makers, and whether pattern makers sit nearby.
  • Housekeeping and safety — clear aisles, labelled chemicals, exits. It predicts how your order will be treated.

Bring samples and documents

Bring what lets the factory respond on the spot:

  • At least one physical reference garment per category, plus a competitor sample if price positioning matters.
  • Your tech pack, or at minimum a measurement chart and colour references.
  • Your swing tags, labels and packaging if you want private label quoted accurately.
  • Order quantity ranges for the next two seasons, so MOQ and price breaks can be discussed honestly.
  • A notebook, a camera and a small measuring tape.

Communication and negotiation points

Come with specific questions, not just a wish list:

  • Which processes are in-house, and which are subcontracted?
  • What is the real MOQ per style, per colour and per yarn?
  • How many sampling rounds are included, and are sample fees credited against bulk?
  • What are the payment terms, and can they be staged against milestones?
  • Do you accept third-party inspection, and who pays for it?
  • Which certifications do you hold, and may we see the audit reports?

On price, resist negotiating the headline number alone. Discuss what changes it — yarn count, gauge, stitch density, packaging, quantity and payment terms. A supplier who explains the trade-offs clearly is worth more than one who agrees to everything.

Visas, timing and logistics

  • Visa — apply well in advance and confirm current business visa requirements for your nationality; rules change.
  • Time the trip — avoid Chinese New Year, when most knitwear production stops. Autumn is the busiest sampling season.
  • Cluster by distance — Dongguan, Guangzhou, Foshan and Shenzhen are close enough to visit several suppliers in one day.
  • Build in buffer — allow half a day per factory, and keep one day free for follow-ups.
  • Interpretation — check whether the factory has English-speaking merchandisers, and arrange an interpreter if not.

How to verify a supplier in a short visit

You rarely have time for a full audit, so watch for signals that are hard to fake: production running during your visit, sample makers working on real buyer requests, inspection records with dates and signatures, and staff who answer technical questions without leaving the room. Where a claim is hard to verify yourself — labour standards, chemical safety, social compliance — ask for third-party audit reports and, if your volume justifies it, commission your own inspection.
