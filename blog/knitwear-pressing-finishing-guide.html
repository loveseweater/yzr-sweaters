Pressing and Finishing Knitwear: Where Final Measurements Are Made

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Pressing is the step where a sweater gets its final dimensions and surface appearance. A garment is knitted and linked slightly off-spec, then relaxed onto a pressing table with steam and vacuum so the stitches settle into their intended size. If you measure a sweater before pressing you are measuring the wrong garment, and if the pressing standard is not written into the tech pack, the factory will press to its own habit — which is how shine, flattened cables and quietly shrunken bodies end up in bulk.

Of all the stages we walk buyers through, pressing is the one people most often treat as a formality. It isn't. A skilled presser can pull a body length up two centimetres or set a collar so it lies flat forever, and a careless one can do the reverse. We see this a lot: a shipment measures fine in the factory report, then fails the buyer's own check on arrival — because the two sets of numbers were taken after different amounts of pressing and relaxation. Here is what actually happens at the pressing table, and how to make it work for you.

What pressing actually does to a knitted garment

Knitted fabric is a structure of loops under tension. When a panel comes off the machine it carries that tension, and linking and seaming add more. Pressing — steam heat applied on a form or table, followed by vacuum to remove the moisture and fix the shape — lets the loops relax into the dimensions the pattern was engineered for. In that sense it is closer to "blocking" in hand knitting than to ironing a shirt.

Three things happen at once:

  • Dimensions settle. Length, width and sleeve measurements move towards their final values. This is why garment measurement must be taken after pressing and a defined relaxation period, not straight off the linking line.
  • The surface cleans up. Steam evens out loop distortion from handling, and the stitch definition becomes uniform — the difference between a "hand-knitted at home" look and a shipped garment.
  • Stability improves. Properly relaxed stitches are less prone to the distortion and spirality problems that show up later when the customer washes the garment.

The defects that come from the pressing table

Most pressing damage is permanent, which is what makes it worth understanding:

  • Shine. Pressing wool too hot, too dry or too long polishes the fibre surface and leaves a gloss that no amount of re-steaming fully removes. It shows up worst on shoulders, pocket positions and anywhere the presser dwells.
  • Flattened texture. Cables, ribs and pointelle are supposed to have dimension. A heavy press turns a 3-gauge cable sweater into something that looks felted. If texture is part of the design, the tech pack should say the texture must be preserved after finishing.
  • Press marks and clamping lines. Where the pressing form or buck meets the garment unevenly, you get visible lines — common on hems and plackets of button-through cardigans.
  • Scorching and yellowing. Temperature too high for the fibre — acrylic blends are the usual victim. It pairs badly with the storage-side issues covered in our notes on yellowing.
  • Dimensional drift. An over-enthusiastic press can mask a knitting fault for exactly as long as it takes the garment to relax back after packing. The measurement looks right on the day and wrong two weeks later in the warehouse.

Machine pressing, hand finishing, and when each is right

Fine-gauge jersey and simple rib styles usually go through a steam-air finishing machine or a standard pressing table, and that is entirely appropriate. But several categories need hands:

  • Heavy textured hand-knitted-look styles — the cable and multi-gauge looks where flattening is fatal — get spot pressing only: seams, collar, hems.
  • Structured collars and lapels on knitted jackets are pressed piece by piece on shaped forms.
  • Beaded, embellished or printed garments often cannot take direct steam heat at all and are finished around the decoration.

If your style needs hand finishing, it belongs in the price conversation, not in a discovery meeting after bulk starts. Hand finishing is slower and costs more, and factories that quietly skip it to hold a price produce the "flat" garments that end up discounted at retail.

How to specify finishing so you get what you approved

The sample you approved was pressed a particular way. The fix is to make that repeatable:

  • State the measurement point. All measurements taken after pressing, after 24 hours' relaxation, flat on a table. Write it in the tech pack and repeat it on the quality standard your inspector works from.
  • Define the appearance standard. "Texture must be preserved; no pressing shine on main body; collar pressed to lie flat" is three sentences that prevent three of the most common finishing claims.
  • Ask for the pressing details on complex fibres. Wool, cotton, acrylic and blends each have a temperature range they tolerate. If you are running a new blend, the pressing window should be agreed at sample wash test stage, not discovered in bulk.
  • Include finishing in inspection. Pressing defects are visual, so they are caught by looking. Our notes on pre-shipment inspection and AQL sampling cover how to make appearance faults a scored defect class rather than a shrug.

The relaxation point everyone forgets

A sweater measured one hour after pressing is not the same sweater measured three days later — knits continue to relax, and garments compressed in polybags for a container journey move again. This is why we tell buyers to include a relaxation window in their own goods-in check. If your inbound QC measures cartons straight off the truck against factory numbers taken hot off the press, you will generate claims that are not really defects — and the argument that follows wastes more money than the measurement.

The professional habit is symmetrical: the factory measures after pressing plus relaxation, you measure the same way, and both of you record it. It sounds pedantic until the first time it saves a shipment.

Frequently asked questions

Should sweater measurements be taken before or after pressing?

After pressing — and after a defined relaxation period, typically 24 hours, laid flat. Pressing is the step that sets final dimensions, so pre-pressing numbers describe an intermediate garment. The relaxation window matters because knits continue to settle after steam is removed, and garments compressed in polybags move again in transit.

Can pressing shine be fixed after it happens?

Partially, at best. Light shine on wool sometimes responds to re-steaming with moisture, but heavy shine means the fibre surface has been polished or damaged and it will read as a permanent defect. Prevention is cheaper: agree a pressing temperature and dwell standard in development, especially for wool and wool blends.

How do I keep cables and heavy textures from being flattened?

Write it into the tech pack as an appearance requirement — "cable and rib texture to be preserved; no flat pressing of main body" — and agree that such styles receive spot pressing of seams, collar and hems only rather than full-body pressing. Then score flattened texture as a defect class at inspection so it is caught before shipment.

Does every sweater style need hand finishing?

No. Fine-gauge jersey and simple rib styles finish well on standard steam equipment. Hand finishing earns its cost on heavy textured styles, structured collars and lapels, and garments with embellishments or prints that cannot take direct heat. If a style needs it, agree it during costing rather than assuming it is included.
