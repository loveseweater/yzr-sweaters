Acrylic Knitwear: Fibre Grades, Pilling and What the Price Hides

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Acrylic is the highest-volume fibre in entry-level knitwear, but "acrylic" is not one material — regular and anti-pill grades behave very differently, and two sweaters with the same composition tag can pill on opposite timelines. The gap between them is a per-kilogram yarn price difference the buyer controls through the specification: name the grade, set a pilling grade with test cycles, and re-verify on bulk yarn, not on the salesman's word.

We see this a lot: a buyer approves a soft, bright sample, orders 3,000 pieces, and by the second month on the shop floor the garment looks tired and fuzzy. The fibre tag never changed. What changed is that the sample was knitted from a better acrylic grade than the bulk. This guide is about how that happens and how to write a specification that prevents it.

Why acrylic dominates entry-level knitwear

Acrylic holds the volume end of the sweater market for simple economic reasons. It costs a fraction of wool and cotton, takes bright and deep colours easily, is machine washable without special care, resists moths and mildew, and delivers a wool-like bulk that works for chunky winter styles. For promotional, gifting, supermarket and fast-fashion programmes, nothing else hits that price point. The trade-off is not appearance but durability — the failure mode of cheap acrylic is pilling, and pilling is a public failure your customer sees within weeks.

Regular acrylic versus anti-pill acrylic

The single most important grade decision in acrylic knitwear sits inside the yarn, invisible on the composition label.

  • Regular acrylic — spun from standard tow. It is cheap, bulky and soft, but loose fibre ends work their way out of the yarn under abrasion and form pills quickly, especially at underarms and where seatbelts and bag straps rub.
  • Anti-pill acrylic — engineered with modified fibre cross-sections and tighter spinning so fibre ends stay anchored. It pills dramatically less, at a price premium per kilogram that typically moves unit cost by a few percent on a sweater. If a style will be worn and washed repeatedly, it is almost always worth it.
  • High-bulk acrylic — heat-treated to develop extra loft after knitting. It gives wool-like warmth and volume at light weights, and is common in blanket-stitch and chunky gauges. Bulk is not the same as anti-pill performance — specify both separately.
  • Micro and soft-touch acrylics — finer denier filaments for a softer, less plasticky hand. Mostly used for next-to-skin styles where regular acrylic feels squeaky.

When a factory quotes two prices for "the same" acrylic sweater, the grade difference inside the yarn is usually the answer.

Acrylic blends: what each partner fibre contributes

Most commercial acrylic knitwear is a blend, and each blend has a logic worth understanding before you approve one.

  • Acrylic / wool — wool adds warmth, resilience and a natural hand while acrylic holds the price down; 30/70 and 50/50 are common. Pilling improves and the composition story improves.
  • Acrylic / nylon (polyamide) — nylon adds strength and shape retention, common in school and workwear knits that must survive hard wear and frequent washing.
  • Acrylic / cotton — cotton softens the hand and the story for spring-weight knits, but the cotton component raises price and changes shrinkage behaviour; blend tolerances matter, so confirm how composition will be declared and verified — our notes on fibre composition tolerance apply directly.
  • Acrylic / viscose or polyester — used to soften drape or cut cost further; test these harder, because pilling and hand can move in either direction depending on ratios.

How to specify pilling instead of hoping for it

Pilling is where acrylic programmes are won and lost, so make it a number, not an adjective. The working method is simple: agree a pilling grade (typically grade 4 or above, on the 1–5 scale where 5 is pill-free) after an agreed number of test cycles, using the standard method your market accepts — for knitted fabric that is normally the Martindale or pilling-box route — and require the test on bulk-production yarn, not on sample-stage yarn. Then keep a retention sample of the approved bulk swatch. When a shipment pills early, you can test the retention swatch against the complaint garment and the argument ends in minutes.

The same discipline applies to the other common acrylic complaints: shine after pressing, static in dry climates, and a limp hand after washing. All three can be controlled through finishing and rinse formulas, but only if the buyer names them in the spec. Our pilling prevention guide covers the mechanical and chemical levers in more depth.

Reading an acrylic quotation honestly

Because acrylic yarn is the cheapest major input in knitwear, small spec changes move the price in ways buyers often misread. A quotation that is 8–10% below comparable offers usually means one of three things: a lower acrylic grade, a lower yarn weight per piece than the approved sample, or an unstated fibre substitution in the blend. The fix is to quote-compare on three fixed terms — named yarn supplier or grade, verified grammage, and test requirements — rather than on price alone. The broader framework for comparing quotes fibre by fibre is in our guide on sweater price factors.

One more costing note: acrylic's yarn minimums are usually smaller than wool's, which makes it attractive for small-batch programmes. But recycled-content claims are difficult — recycled acrylic yarn exists but has limited supply and spotty traceability — so if sustainability claims matter to the brand, price that constraint in from the start rather than discovering it at marketing stage.

When acrylic is the wrong choice

Be honest about the ceiling. Acrylic cannot match wool's warmth-to-weight, moisture handling or odour behaviour, and no finishing trick changes that. For premium positioning, alpine outdoor claims or slow-fashion storytelling, acrylic will disappoint reviewers even if the garment is well made. The fibre suits value programmes, trend-led styles and layering pieces — not hero products. Making that call early is cheaper than making it after returns data arrives.

Frequently asked questions

Is anti-pill acrylic worth the extra cost?

For any garment worn more than a handful of times, yes. The premium is a few percent of unit cost, while pilling complaints drive returns, markdowns and lost reorders. The one place regular acrylic is defensible is single-season promotional or gifting knit that will be worn a few times on purpose.

Two suppliers quote the same "100% acrylic" sweater at very different prices. Why?

Usually the yarn grade, the grammage, or both. A quote can be honest on composition and still use the cheapest regular acrylic at a lower weight per piece. Ask each supplier for the named yarn grade, the grammage of the approved sample, and the pilling grade with test cycles — then compare on those three terms.

How do I verify the factory actually used anti-pill acrylic in bulk?

Three controls work together: a yarn lot record from the spinning mill, a pilling test on yarn taken from bulk production, and a retention swatch kept from the bulk lot. If the factory cannot offer the first two, treat the anti-pill claim as marketing until testing proves otherwise.
