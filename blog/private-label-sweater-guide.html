Private Label Sweaters: A Complete Guide for Brands

Views: 0

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Private label is how most independent knitwear brands reach the market without owning a factory. You define the product, the label carries your name, and the manufacturing partner builds it to your brief. Done well, it gives you a collection that looks made for you. Done carelessly, it produces a stock item with your name sewn in. Here are the decisions that separate the two.

Private label vs white label: know which one you are buying

The terms get used interchangeably, but they promise different things.

  • White label means a supplier's ready-made sweater that you brand. The style, yarn, fit and colours already exist; you add your neck label and sell it. It is fast and low-risk, but other sellers can buy the same sweater, so differentiation is limited to branding.
  • Private label means the product is made to your specification — your yarn, colours, fit and labels — and sold under your brand. It usually needs sampling and a higher order quantity, but it is what gives a brand a genuine signature piece.

Many brands start with white label to test demand, then move into private label once a style proves itself. That is sensible, as long as you know which one you are buying at each step.

What "custom label" actually covers

Branding a sweater is more than a logo. A full private label package can include:

  • Neck label: woven or printed, with your brand name, size and sometimes fibre content.
  • Care label: fibre composition, washing instructions and origin — often legally required in the destination market.
  • Hang tag: card or recycled-board tag with your logo, price space, barcode or QR.
  • Woven side or flag tag: the small branded tab on a hem or seam that signals quality.
  • Polybag, tissue and box: the unboxing layer — printed bags, stickers, thank-you cards, gift boxes.
  • Branded zip pulls or buttons: small hardware details engraved with your name.

Labels are often the strongest signal of a real brand versus a reseller, and one of the cheapest parts of the order to customise. Ask early what is included and what is charged separately.

Minimum order quantity: what to expect

MOQ is where most first-time brand owners get surprised. Knitwear factories set minimums because yarn must be dyed in batches, machines need setup time, and quality is checked per style. As a guide:

  • Higher MOQs apply to custom yarns and colours, because dye lots have minimums.
  • Lower MOQs are possible when you choose a yarn and colour the factory already stocks.
  • MOQ is usually quoted per style and per colour, and can often be split across sizes.
  • Some manufacturers accept a smaller trial run for a first order, then scale.

Treat MOQ as negotiable within reason: stay flexible on colour and yarn for your first styles, and save fully custom combinations for pieces you are confident about.

The sampling process, step by step

Sampling is where the product becomes real, and it should not be rushed.

  • Brief: you send references, sketches or a tech pack — style, yarn, gauge, colours, measurements and brand details.
  • Yarn and colour selection: the factory proposes yarns and lab-dips colours for your approval.
  • Fit sample (proto): the first garment, made to check silhouette, measurements and hand feel.
  • Revisions: you give consolidated feedback, and the factory revises.
  • Pre-production sample: the near-final version, often used for your marketing shoot.
  • Bulk approval: you sign off, the factory produces, and quality is checked against the approved sample.

Two or three sampling rounds is normal. Give all your comments in a single round rather than trickling them in — it saves days.

Protecting your brand and your designs

  • Sign an NDA before sending tech packs, sketches or original artwork.
  • Share on a need-to-know basis. Send the measurements and materials a factory needs to quote, not the full commercial picture.
  • Keep a paper trail. Date and archive your designs, tech packs and approvals.
  • Confirm pattern ownership. Budget for a development or sampling fee so the pattern and samples are yours, and state this in writing.
  • Choose a manufacturer that respects exclusivity. A reputable partner will not resell a private label style to another buyer.

Garment designs are rarely protected by copyright alone, so contracts, records and a trustworthy manufacturing partner do the real work.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Treating white label as private label. If the sweater is stock, do not market it as exclusive.
  • Underestimating MOQ and lead time. Plan the calendar backwards from your launch or season.
  • Approving a sample by photo only. Feel the hand and check colour in your own lighting.
  • Skipping care labels. Missing or wrong composition can block a shipment or a marketplace listing.
  • Changes after bulk starts. Late design or colour changes are the most common cause of cost overruns.
  • Choosing on price alone. The cheapest quote often hides weaker yarn or looser quality control, which shows up in returns.
