Sweater Shrinkage: Why It Happens and How Buyers Control It

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

Sweater shrinkage comes mainly from felting in animal fibres such as wool and from stored tension releasing in the yarn. Buyers control it by choosing the right yarn and shrinkage treatment, agreeing a wash-test method and per-point tolerances in the tech pack, and approving measurements taken after washing rather than straight off the machine.

Shrinkage cannot be fixed once goods leave the factory. A sweater that measures correctly on the inspection table but loses a size in the first home wash becomes a return or dead stock. It is predictable and testable, provided it is written into the order before the yarn is dyed.

Why do knitted sweaters shrink?

Knitted fabric is a network of loops holding stored tension. Fibre that has been spun, twisted, wetted or stretched tries to return to a calmer state, and shrinkage is that return.

Wool and animal fibres: felting

Wool fibres carry microscopic scales lying in one direction. When moisture, heat and mechanical action happen together, the scales interlock and the fabric tightens permanently. This is felting, and it is irreversible: one hot machine wash can visibly shorten a wool sweater.

Cotton, viscose and blends: relaxation

Cellulosic fibres do not felt, but they relax. Cotton and viscose sit under tension through spinning, knitting and finishing; once wetted, the loops settle into a denser, shorter state. The loss is generally smaller and more even, and most occurs in the first wash.

The three triggers

  • Moisture — swells the fibre and unlocks the structure.
  • Heat — speeds relaxation and drives felting in animal fibres.
  • Mechanical action — agitation pulls loops and scales together.

Remove one trigger and shrinkage usually drops sharply; remove two and it becomes negligible.

Shrinkage, relaxation and dimensional stability: what is the difference?

Buyers and factories use these terms loosely, and that is where disputes start. Used precisely:

  • Washing shrinkage — the measurement change produced by a defined washing and drying procedure. This is the figure that appears in a test report.
  • Relaxation shrinkage — the change caused by letting the garment rest, often in steam or dry heat, without laundering. It reflects tension stored during knitting and pressing.
  • Dimensional stability — the garment's ability to hold its measurements through wear and care. It is the goal; shrinkage is the symptom.

A sweater can pass a relaxation check and still fail in a customer's laundry, because relaxation testing applies neither the moisture nor the movement that trigger felting.

Which factors decide how much a sweater shrinks?

  • Fibre type and blend ratio — animal fibres felt; cotton, viscose and synthetics relax. Even a modest wool content can drive noticeable felting.
  • Yarn preparation — shrink-resist treatments and pre-shrunk yarns reduce movement before knitting starts; see our knitwear yarn guide.
  • Knit density and gauge — a tighter structure has less room to contract, but feels firmer and uses more yarn, as knit gauge explained sets out.
  • Wet processing and pressing — dyeing and washing decide how much tension is released before shipping; pressing can stabilise the shape or leave fresh tension in it.
  • Laundry habits — wash temperature, drying method and detergent multiply whatever the factory did or did not control.

Why buyers approve relaxed measurements, not off-machine measurements

A sweater straight off the knitting machine is longer and narrower than the one a customer wears: it still carries needle tension and the pull of handling. The industry answer is the relaxed measurement, where the garment rests, or is steam-relaxed under a defined procedure, and is measured afterwards. Tech packs, fit comments and inspection reports are written against relaxed measurements. If a supplier quotes off-machine figures against a relaxed spec, the order looks correct on paper and arrives short.

How do you write shrinkage control into the order?

  • A wash test method — normally an ISO or AATCC related washing and drying method, named explicitly and agreed with your testing lab.
  • A drying condition — line dry, flat dry or tumble dry changes the result more than most buyers expect.
  • Tolerance per measurement point — body length, chest, shoulder and sleeve move at different rates, so one overall figure rarely suffices.
  • Re-measurement after washing — state that measurements are taken from the washed garment, not the flat one.

Agree what happens if the test fails: re-testing, re-finishing, sorting or rejection.

How is shrinkage checked at inspection and testing?

Factories generally run in-house wash tests at development and pre-production. For bulk, buyers often add third-party verification from an agency such as SGS or BV. Retain pre-wash and post-wash comparison samples from the same bulk lot, and ensure the AQL inspection confirms garments still sit inside the relaxed tolerance after the agreed wash procedure. Reporting conventions are covered in our notes on sweater lab testing standards.

Two misconceptions worth clearing up

  • Shrinkage always means the factory made a mistake. Sometimes it does — loose knitting, a skipped relaxation step, the wrong drying method. But wool that felts in a hot wash is behaving as wool behaves; where the care label and the fibre do not match the customer's laundry habits, the fix is design and labelling.
  • A washed sweater is always safer. Pre-washing reduces residual shrinkage but adds cost, lead time and a softer hand feel. Ask what was actually done.

How should you raise a shrinkage problem with your supplier?

Send the test report, the washed sample and the measurement point that failed. Ask which stage caused it — yarn, knitting density, wet finishing or pressing — and what the correction costs. Frequently asked questions

Does cold water washing stop a sweater shrinking?

It reduces the risk but does not remove it. Cold water and gentle handling take away much of the heat and movement that drive felting, yet cotton and viscose can still relax in the first wash.

What shrinkage tolerance should a tech pack carry?

No single figure suits every style. Tolerances are set per measurement point, because body length, chest, shoulder and sleeve move at different rates. Agree them after seeing a pre-production wash test on the actual yarn.

Are pre-shrunk sweaters worth the extra cost?

For wool or high-cotton blends washed at home, a relaxation or pre-shrink finish generally pays for itself in fewer returns. For dry-clean-only or heavy-gauge styles it often adds cost without much benefit.
