Choosing a Freight Forwarder for Knitwear Imports

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

A good freight forwarder earns their fee three times over: an all-in quote with no destination surprises, a container or consolidation plan that fits knitwear's awkward cube, and paperwork that clears customs the first time. Get quotes from two or three, force every fee into the open — origin and destination, separately — and give the job to the one who asks about your cartons before quoting a rate. The cheapest rate per cubic metre is rarely the cheapest shipment.

Most buyers meet their first forwarder at the worst possible moment: goods finished, vessel closing, and a factory asking who to release the cargo to. We ship knitwear out of Dongguan every week, and the difference between a good and bad forwarder shows up most clearly in the boring places — the bill of lading, the arrival charges, the demurrage clock nobody warned you about. Here is what we have learned watching our customers' freight for years.

What a forwarder actually does (and what they don't)

A forwarder is not a shipping line — they buy space from carriers, book it on your behalf, arrange the trucking at both ends, handle export and often import clearance, and assemble the document set. They are a logistics contractor, and like any contractor, their value is mostly in what they prevent: missed sailings, mis-declared cargo, containers stuck at the terminal. They are not automatically your customs broker, they are not your insurer, and they are not responsible for quality — a surprisingly common confusion when goods arrive damaged and everyone points at everyone else. Cargo cover still needs arranging separately, as our guide to cargo insurance and claims explains.

Knitwear-specific things your forwarder should ask about

Apparel is a freight category of its own, and knitwear doubly so. A forwarder who works with garments will ask about these before quoting; a generalist will not:

  • Carton plan and cube. Poly-bagged sweaters are light for their volume, so sea freight bills on measure, not weight. Carton dimensions decide your CBM, and your CBM decides your freight bill. We provide a carton plan with every booking so the forwarder can see total cube before quoting — see our packing and carton guide for how we set carton sizes.
  • LCL versus FCL crossover. Somewhere around 13–15 CBM, sharing a container stops making sense and booking a full one gets cheaper per unit, destination charges being what they are. A good forwarder tells you where that line sits for your lane; a lazy one just books whatever you asked for. Our guide to LCL shipping for knitwear goes through the maths.
  • Moisture and container condition. Knitwear crossing the equator in a container roof absorbs condensation unless the container is inspected, cartons are kept off the floor, and desiccant is used. Ask the forwarder about their container inspection practice — the answer separates apparel people from box movers. The failure mode is ugly; see mould and mildew in shipped knitwear.
  • Shipping marks and labelling. Carton marks, style and colour breakdowns on the packing list, and country-of-origin marking matter at destination — mismatches between cartons and documents are a classic cause of customs holds, covered in our piece on the shipping document set.

How to compare quotes without being fooled

The classic trick in freight is the attractive rate followed by the ugly invoice. When you collect quotes, insist on three things. First, origin and destination charges listed separately — a USD 95/CBM rate with USD 400 of undocumented destination fees is worse than USD 120 all-in. Second, a written list of what is excluded (customs duties, exam fees, demurrage, delivery beyond the port). Third, the validity window — ocean rates move, and a quote from April is worthless in September's peak season. Send identical cargo details to every bidder: pieces, cartons, CBM, weight, Incoterms and port pair, because Incoterms decide who books what and ambiguous instructions produce incomparable quotes.

Also ask who runs the destination side. Some forwarders are strong at origin and hand your cargo to a random agent at destination, who then bills you whatever they like. The best structure for a smaller importer is often one forwarder with their own office or a long-standing agent at the destination port, accountable end to end.

Should you use the factory's forwarder?

We get asked this constantly, and the honest answer is: sometimes, with your eyes open. On CIF or CFR terms we arrange the freight ourselves — that is literally what the terms mean — and we are happy to show buyers the cost breakdown. On FOB terms, you book, and we will work with any forwarder you nominate. The risks of factory-arranged freight are obvious (margin hidden in freight) and the risks of buyer-arranged freight are less obvious: your forwarder does not know the factory, the cartons, or the local trucking, and every handover is a chance for delay. Whichever way you go, the protection is the same — itemised costs and the document set agreed in advance.

When air freight is the forwarder conversation worth having

Air freight on knitwear sounds extravagant until you calculate the cost of missing a season launch: three or four USD per unit by air versus markdowns on the whole late shipment. The decision hinges on weight — our comparison of air versus sea for knitwear runs the numbers — and a good forwarder will quote both lanes unprompted when your dates are tight. For samples, courier accounts are simpler; see shipping samples by courier. Once you know your freight, feed the real number into your landed cost calculation rather than guessing.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a forwarder at both ends?

No — most importers use one forwarder who coordinates both ends, either through their own destination office or a contracted agent. What you want to avoid is nominating an origin forwarder in China and a separate, unrelated destination agent, because when something goes wrong in the handover, each will blame the other. One accountable party with authority over the whole lane is worth a small premium.

How many quotes should I collect per shipment?

Two or three is plenty, provided they are genuinely comparable: same ports, same Incoterm, same carton data, and all origin and destination charges itemised. Beyond three quotes you are mostly collecting noise — spot rates that will not exist when you book. Long-term, a single reliable forwarder with negotiated rates beats an auction for every shipment.

What does a forwarder cost for a typical knitwear LCL shipment?

For a few CBM of knitwear from South China to Europe or North America, all-in origin and destination charges typically run a few hundred US dollars on top of the ocean rate, plus clearance and delivery if you buy the full service. The exact number varies by port and season, which is precisely why the itemised quote matters more than any single line of it.
