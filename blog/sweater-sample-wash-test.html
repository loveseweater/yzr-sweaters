How to Wash-Test a Sweater Sample Before You Approve Bulk

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Measure the sample first — chest, body length, sleeve length and shoulder, written down. Then wash it the way the care label will tell your customer to wash it, dry it the same way, and measure again. A sample that survives three honest home wash cycles with less than 5% dimensional change and no new pilling problem is a sample you can approve. Everything else is a conversation to have before bulk, not after delivery.

Most buyers approve a sweater sample on how it looks and feels on the table. That's the right check for design — but the garment your customer reviews on the internet is the one after its third wash, not the one out of the polybag. A wash test at sample stage is the cheapest quality control you will ever do, and you can run it at home with a tape measure and a washing machine.

Why the wash test matters more than the fitting

Knitwear is mechanically different from woven clothing. The loops hold stress from knitting, linking and steaming, and water releases it. That's why a sweater can measure perfectly at approval and come back a size smaller after one careful hand wash. Our article on why sweaters shrink explains the two mechanisms — relaxation and felting — but for approval purposes you don't need the science. You need to know whether it happens to your garment.

The same cycle also surfaces the other two return-drivers: pilling and colour bleeding. Neither shows up reliably on an unwashed sample.

The protocol: what to do, in order

This is the routine we suggest to buyers, and the one we run in-house before shipping pre-production samples:

  • 1. Measure before washing. Lay the sample flat, smoothed but not stretched, and record chest width, body length from the highest shoulder point, sleeve length from the shoulder seam, and shoulder width. Our points of measure guide shows exactly where to place the tape so your numbers match the factory's.
  • 2. Photograph it. Front, back, one close-up of the fabric surface. You'll want these when you're trying to describe a change to someone on another continent.
  • 3. Wash it the way the label will say. If the care label will read "hand wash cold", hand wash cold. If it will say "machine wash 30°C", use the machine. Testing to a harsher standard than the label creates problems your customers will never have; testing gentler creates problems they will.
  • 4. Dry it the way the label will say. For knitwear that's almost always flat drying. Never hang a wet sweater — the weight of the water stretches it, and you'll be measuring a drying error, not a washing one.
  • 5. Measure again, then repeat twice more. One wash can be an accident. Three cycles is a trend. Compare each set of measurements against the originals, not against the previous wash.

What counts as a pass

Different fibres get different allowances, but as a buyer's working rule:

  • Dimensional change within ±5% on any measurement is normal knitwear behaviour; within ±3% is good. Beyond 5%, or any change that moves the garment across a size boundary, needs a fix — usually a finishing adjustment on the factory side, sometimes a yarn change.
  • Pilling: rub the underarm and side-seam area with your palm after drying. A few loose surface fibres releasing in the first wash is normal. Distinct pills forming on low-friction areas of a synthetic or blend garment after three washes predicts what your reviews will look like. Our pilling guide covers which factory fixes actually work.
  • Colour: check the wash water and the whites. Deep reds, blacks and navies on cotton and viscose are the usual offenders. If the water is tinted, wash the sample once with a white towel and look at the towel. That's your customer's white shirt.
  • Shape: lay it flat and look at it. Twisting side seams, a hem that no longer sits level, or a neckline that has grown are all structural findings, not cosmetic ones.

Writing results the factory can act on

"It shrank a bit" starts a negotiation. Numbers end one. Send the before/after measurements in the same format the factory uses, with the wash method spelled out ("machine washed 30°C gentle cycle, flat dried, three cycles"). Attach the photos. Then ask for the corrective proposal in writing: will they adjust the knitting tension, change the washing/finishing route, or relax the measurements at spec? Our sample review checklist has the full template, and the wash test slots into it as one section.

One thing worth agreeing in advance: whose wash test counts. Serious orders put a line in the spec — often a reference to a lab standard like the AATCC or ISO methods in our lab testing overview — so a dispute about "your machine versus mine" can't happen at bulk stage.

Frequently asked questions

How much shrinkage is acceptable in a sweater?

As a working rule, dimensional change within ±5% on any point of measure is normal for knitwear, and within ±3% is a good result. Anything beyond 5%, or any change that pushes the garment across a size boundary, should be corrected before bulk — usually through finishing adjustments or a yarn change.

Should I wash-test to the care label or to a harsher standard?

To the care label. Testing harsher than the label creates failures your customers will never cause; testing gentler misses the ones they will. If you suspect customers will ignore the label — which happens — that's a reason to change the label or the yarn, not to quietly test to a different method.

Is a home wash test enough, or do I need a lab?

A home wash test on the sample is enough to approve design and catch obvious problems. For compliance-level evidence — shrinkage to a written standard, colour fastness grades, pilling ratings — you need a lab test to a named AATCC or ISO method, which is worth doing on the pre-production sample for any serious volume.
