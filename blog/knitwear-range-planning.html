Knitwear Range Planning: How Many Styles and Which Price Points

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

In knitwear range planning, structure matters more than style count: a few hero styles carry most of the season's volume, basic core styles underpin cash flow across seasons, and a small number of statement styles set the brand's tone. Arrange the collection on a three-tier price ladder — entry, mid, premium — and let style count follow your channels and budget rather than ambition.

Knitwear range planning is where collections quietly win or lose money. The problem is rarely one bad style; it is structure — how many styles you carry, at which price points, in which categories, and how deep each one is stocked. This guide breaks a knitwear range into its working parts: the three roles every collection needs, the three-tier price ladder, the category mix, and the breadth-versus-depth decision.

What three roles should every knitwear range play?

Apparel assortment planning works best when every style is assigned a job before it is designed. Three roles cover almost everything a knitwear collection contains.

Hero styles

A hero style carries the majority of your sales expectation for the season. It deserves the deepest stock, the strongest photography and the most colourways. If a hero underperforms, the whole range feels it.

Basics and core styles

Basics are perennial shapes — crew necks, V-necks, simple cardigans — that sell across seasons and carry the highest reorder probability. They anchor cash flow between seasonal bets.

Statement and image styles

Statement styles exist to be noticed: unusual textures, bold colours, fashion silhouettes. Their sales expectation is deliberately modest, but they set brand tone and pull the perceived value of the range upward.

Structure follows from the roles: depth behind the heroes, continuity behind the basics, and a controlled minority of statement pieces.

How many styles should a knitwear collection have?

No fixed number is honest — the right count follows three inputs: how many channels you sell through, how much budget you can actually deploy, and how wide the category runs (sweaters only, or sweaters plus cardigans, dresses and accessories?). A small range concentrates budget behind fewer, deeper bets; a wide range trades depth for shelf presence and shopper choice. The dependable path is to start narrower than feels comfortable, read the first sell-through — the share of delivered stock sold within a period — and extend the range where the data, not the excitement, supports it.

How do you build a price ladder that works?

A price ladder (sometimes called price architecture) is the deliberate arrangement of a range across entry, mid and premium tiers, each with a distinct job. Entry-price pieces draw first-time buyers and give marketplaces and press a low-risk hook. Mid-tier pieces carry the volume — most of your units and most of your revenue should sit here. Premium pieces lift perceived brand value and margin, and they make the mid tier look sensibly priced by comparison. The common failure is a flat range: when everything is mid-priced, shoppers have nothing to compare against and no reason to trade up. Build visible steps between tiers, and let one or two premium pieces anchor the ladder even at small volumes.

Which categories should the range cover, and in what proportion?

Think in roles rather than fixed percentages. Sweaters form the core of the collection. Cardigans extend the wearing season because they layer over other clothing. Knit dresses can drive womenswear volume within a single SKU, and accessories such as scarves and beanies create low-ticket entry points while using up leftover yarn. Neckline and silhouette spread matters as much as category count: an all-crew range competes with itself, while ten different necklines fragment production and dilute every fit lesson you have learned. Breadth or depth: which strategy fits your brand?

Breadth means many styles with shallow stock behind each; depth means fewer styles bought deeper. Breadth gives shoppers choice but spreads capital thin and raises the risk that any single style stocks out. Depth concentrates money, simplifies production and — critically in knitwear — makes reorder arithmetic work, because one steady style bought deep is far easier to replenish than ten styles each hovering at their minimum. How should colour work across the whole range?

Colour decisions belong at range level, not style by style. Set a neutral base palette that runs through most of the collection so the pieces read as one family and share yarn demand; add a small accent palette of seasonal colours on selected pieces for energy and newness. Concentrating accent colours on two or three styles also keeps custom dyeing — the expensive part of any knit program — proportionate. What are the most common range planning mistakes?

Five appear again and again:

  • Too many styles bought too shallow, so nothing arrives in sellable quantity.
  • A flat mid-price range with no entry hook and no premium anchor.
  • Statement styles over-weighted — a photogenic range that cannot pay for itself.
  • Rebuilding the range from zero every season and abandoning the cross-season basics that fund the next bet.
  • Choosing colourways style by style, so no two pieces share yarn.

All five are fixable at the planning stage, which is exactly when fixing them costs nothing.

Frequently asked questions

Hero style or basic — what is the difference in practice?

A hero style is a seasonal driver: it needs deep stock now and will fade when the season turns. A basic sells across seasons and is the likeliest style to be reordered. In practice, heroes get the launch budget while basics get standing replenishment.

Should a first knitwear collection include premium price points?

Yes, but sparingly. One or two premium pieces anchor the ladder, frame the mid tier as good value and lift brand perception — while entry and mid tiers do the actual selling. A first collection dominated by premium pricing is a hard sell.

How do I plan colourways across a range instead of style by style?

Fix a neutral base palette shared across the collection, then add a small seasonal accent palette applied to selected styles. Sharing colours across styles also consolidates yarn demand, which improves pricing and makes small production runs easier to schedule.
