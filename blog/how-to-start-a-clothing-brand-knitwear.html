How to Start a Clothing Brand: Sourcing Knitwear from a Manufacturer

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Starting a clothing brand is mostly a sourcing problem disguised as a design problem. You can have a clear vision for your knitwear line and still stall for months because you don't know how to turn a sketch into a sampled, produced, packaged garment at a price that leaves margin. This guide walks a first-time founder through the decisions that matter, in the order they usually come up.

1. Define your niche and price point first

Before you contact a single knitwear supplier, get specific about what you are selling and to whom. "Sweaters" is too broad. A better brief looks like: heavyweight cotton-blend crewnecks for a mid-market streetwear label, or fine-gauge merino cardigans for a minimalist womenswear brand.

Your niche sets your price point, and your price point sets your yarn, gauge, and finishing. A founder who knows they need to land at a certain wholesale cost can have a far more productive first conversation with a manufacturer than one who says "something like this, but cheaper."

  • Who is the customer? Age, style, climate, and how often they buy.
  • What is the retail price? Work backward to your target production cost.
  • What makes it different? Fit, fibre, colour story, or a detail nobody else offers.

2. Choose the right knitwear supplier

You have roughly three options: a trading company, a trading-and-manufacturing hybrid, or a direct factory. For knitwear specifically, working with a team that actually sits inside a knitting cluster usually pays off, because yarn access, machine capacity, and skilled linking are all local.

Dongguan, in southern China, is one of the world's denser knitwear clusters, which is why so many brands source sweaters there. A Dongguan knitwear manufacturer can often offer a wider yarn library and faster sampling than a distant middleman. When you shortlist suppliers, look for clear communication, English-language documentation, and a willingness to answer technical questions about gauge and yarn.

Ask them to explain their OEM/ODM process. OEM means they make your design; ODM means they can also adapt an existing style to your needs. A supplier fluent in both is far more useful to a young brand.

3. Sample before you commit

Never skip sampling. A knit's hand feel, drape, and colour can only be judged in the hand, not on a screen. A proper sampling round gives you a fit sample and a pre-production sample, so you can correct the pattern, yarn, and trims before bulk.

Budget for at least two rounds. First samples almost always need adjustment: a neckline that sits too high, a sleeve that runs long, a colour that reads differently in daylight. Experienced factories build sampling into the schedule and will tell you honestly how long a revision takes.

4. Start small with a flexible MOQ

Most new brands cannot afford thousands of units per style, and they shouldn't try. Flexible, small-batch MOQ lets you test a style with real customers, see what sells, and reorder the winners.

Talk to suppliers about per-colour and per-style minimums early. Some manufacturers will accept a smaller trial order for a first run, then scale with you as your volume grows. Be transparent: a factory is more likely to be flexible for a founder who plans to reorder than for one shopping purely on the lowest price.

5. Private label, branding and packaging

The difference between "a sweater" and "your sweater" is often in the details. Private label production lets you add your own woven labels, neck tags, care labels, hangtags, and polybag branding. These small touches raise perceived value far more than their cost.

  • Custom woven main label and size tag
  • Care label with your fibre composition and washing instructions
  • Hangtag and branded polybag or tissue wrap
  • Retail-ready folding standard if you sell wholesale

6. Control cost without ruining quality

Cost engineering is about choices, not shortcuts. Yarn composition is the biggest lever: a wool-acrylic blend can hit a much lower price than pure wool while still feeling good. Gauge, garment weight, and the amount of hand-finishing are the next biggest factors. Ask your supplier which changes save money without hurting the wearability of the product.

Common mistakes first-time brands make

  • Ordering too much inventory before testing the market.
  • Skipping samples and discovering a problem in bulk.
  • Choosing a supplier on price alone, then losing weeks to poor communication.
  • Forgetting that lead time includes production plus shipping, not just knitting.
  • Under-budgeting for labels, packaging, and freight when calculating margin.
