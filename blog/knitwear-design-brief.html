The Design Brief: What to Decide Before You Write a Tech Pack

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

A design brief answers what you are making and for whom — the customer, the price point, the season, the mood. A tech pack answers how to make it — measurements, yarn, construction, trims. Brief first, pack second: if the brief is unclear, the tech pack will be technically correct and commercially wrong, and sampling becomes a search for something nobody has defined.

Most development problems that get blamed on the factory start one step earlier. A sweater arrives, it matches the measurements, the yarn is right, and it still does not feel like the product the buyer had in mind. Nothing in the tech pack was wrong — the problem is that the brief was never written down. The tech pack specifies a garment; it does not know who is going to wear it or what they were expecting to pay.

What is a design brief, and how is it different from a tech pack?

The two documents are often conflated, and separated they become much more useful.

  • The design brief sets intent. It describes the customer, the occasion, the season, the price band, the competitive context and the feeling the garment should create. It is written in language, not numbers.
  • The tech pack sets specification. It converts the brief into measurements, tolerances, yarn, gauge, structure, trims and packing. It is written in numbers and technical terms.

The brief is the input; the tech pack is the output. A buyer who writes the tech pack first is effectively specifying a garment without having decided what it is for — which is why so many first samples come back "close but not right". Our guide to the tech pack covers the specification side in detail; this article covers what has to be decided before that document makes sense.

What should a design brief settle?

Six things, and each one changes a decision further down the process.

  • Who the customer is. Age range, style orientation, and how they shop. This drives silhouette, colour and price tolerance more than any other single factor.
  • The price point. Retail target, and therefore the manufacturing cost ceiling. Yarn choice, gauge and structure all follow from the number — deciding this late is how a design becomes unmakeable at its intended price.
  • The season and the delivery window. Autumn/winter and spring/summer imply different weights, yarns and colours, and the delivery date determines when development has to start — our notes on the buying calendar cover the timing side.
  • The occasion and use. An everyday layering knit and a special-occasion piece are different products even at the same price. Intended use determines weight, care requirement and finish.
  • The competitive set. What the garment will sit next to on a rail or a category page. This is what makes a brief concrete rather than aspirational.
  • The style intent in plain words. A short statement of mood and character — refined, cosy, oversized, minimal — plus reference garments or images. Vague intent is the most common gap, and the easiest to fix.

Why does the brief matter if the tech pack is complete?

Because a specification can be followed precisely and still miss. Three examples show how:

  • Price and yarn in conflict. A brief that implies a premium customer but a mid-market retail price will produce a yarn choice that satisfies neither. Caught in the brief, it is a design adjustment; caught in sampling, it is a lost development round.
  • Silhouette and customer in conflict. A relaxed oversized shape on a customer who expects a closer fit does not fail on measurement — it fails on intent, and no tolerance will fix it. Our guide to silhouettes and fits covers how the shape decision should follow from the customer.
  • Season and weight in conflict. A winter brief with a lightweight construction reads thin in the shop, however well made. Our notes on how knitwear keeps you warm explain the weight and gauge levers involved.

In each case the factory did exactly what was specified. The gap was between the specification and the intention behind it — which is the brief's job to close.

How does the brief change what a factory can offer?

A supplier who receives a brief can contribute far more than one who receives only a spec. Given intent — customer, price, season, mood — an experienced knitwear supplier can suggest a yarn that reaches the price without losing the hand feel, propose a gauge that hits the weight, and flag when a design idea will not work at the target cost. Given only numbers, they can only execute.

This is also where development time is saved. A supplier working from a brief can often propose two or three routes to the same intent during sampling, which is useful when the first route turns out to be over budget. Our notes on starting a knitwear brand cover how this fits into a first collection, and on private label for buyers adding their own branding to an existing shape.

How long should it be?

Short. A single page is usually enough, and a page that goes unread is worse than no page. The practical format is a paragraph of intent, the customer and price band, the season and delivery window, and a handful of reference images or a reference garment. That is enough to align a supplier, and enough to catch an internal disagreement before it reaches production.

Frequently asked questions

Can I skip the design brief if I have a reference garment?

A reference garment is a strong part of a brief, but it does not replace one. It shows what the garment looks like without stating the price band, the customer or the delivery window — and those are what determine whether the reference can actually be produced at the intended cost. Use the garment as the visual half and write the rest in a short paragraph.

Who should write the design brief?

Whoever owns the commercial decision: at a small brand that is usually the founder, and at a larger one the buyer or product manager. The important thing is that a single person signs it off, so the factory receives one consistent set of intentions rather than a composite of several people's preferences.

What is the most common thing missing from a brief?

The price band. Buyers often describe an aesthetic and a customer in detail and leave the cost implication unstated, which is precisely the constraint that decides yarn, gauge and construction. Stating the target manufacturing cost early prevents the most expensive kind of rework — a design that has to be dismantled to become affordable.
