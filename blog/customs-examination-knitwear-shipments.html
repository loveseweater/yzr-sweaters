Customs Exams on Knitwear Shipments: VACIS, Intensive Exams and Who Pays

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Most containers clear on documents alone. When customs flags one, it is usually a non-intrusive X-ray scan (hours to a day, modest cost) or an intensive exam where the container is unloaded and cartons opened (several days to a fortnight, real money). Neither is a penalty, but on most trade terms the importer pays for both. The delay is decided less by the exam than by how clean and consistent your documents were before loading.

We see this a lot with buyers who get a charge on a freight invoice labelled "exam fee" and assume someone made a mistake. Nobody did. Customs selected the container, a third party physically handled it, and the bill follows the cargo. What surprises people is how much the outcome depends on paperwork done weeks earlier in Dongguan. Here is the plain version of what an exam is, what it costs, and how to keep it short.

Why a knitwear container gets flagged

An exam is not automatically suspicion. Containers are selected randomly, by risk profile, and by targeting rules that vary by country and change over time. The triggers buyers can actually influence are documentary: a vague goods description, a declared value that sits oddly against the HS code and fibre content, a first-time importer record, a mismatch between the invoice and the shipping documents, or an ISF filing that does not match the bill of lading.

Classification is a recurring one for knitwear. A "100% cashmere knitted pullover" and a "sweater" are not the same declaration, and the duty line in HS codes and duties is exactly what an officer is checking. Our customs clearance walkthrough covers the normal path; an exam is what happens when that path is interrupted for a physical look.

The three levels, in plain terms

Names differ by port, but the physical reality is consistent enough to plan against:

  • Non-intrusive scan (VACIS or X-ray). The container stays sealed and passes through a scanner. If the image is accepted, it clears in hours; if the image is unclear, it gets escalated. Cost is a scan fee and possibly a short chassis or terminal move.
  • Tail-gate or partial exam. The door is opened and the outer cartons inspected, sometimes with a few boxes pulled. Typically a day or two, with a devan-and-reload charge for the part handled.
  • Intensive exam. The whole container is unloaded, cartons opened, quantities and marks checked against the entry, then reloaded. In a busy port this runs from several working days to a fortnight, plus devan, storage, reloading and driver waiting time.

The expensive part is rarely the exam fee itself. It is the time the box is off the clock, and the charges that accrue while it sits — which is where an exam quietly turns into a demurrage and detention problem.

What it costs, and who actually pays

Under most terms the importer of record carries exam costs, because the intervention happens at destination against their entry. FOB or CIF decides who books and insures the freight; it does not move the destination exam bill back to the factory. A buyer who assumes "FOB means the supplier handles everything until arrival" gets an unpleasant invoice. Ask your forwarder before the vessel loads how exam fees are billed — some fold them into a destination package, others pass through every terminal charge at cost plus a handling fee.

Two more honest caveats. Cargo insurance covers physical loss or damage, not a delay or a compliance intervention, so do not expect it to absorb an exam. And where an exam finds a genuine discrepancy — wrong quantity, undeclared items, a misdeclared fibre — the consequences move from fees into penalties and amended entries, which is a different conversation entirely.

The document errors that cause avoidable exams

In our experience the escalations are mostly self-inflicted and mostly about consistency between documents that were prepared by different people:

  • Goods description too generic. "Ladies sweater" on the invoice, "knitted pullover 100% cashmere" on the packing list, and a third wording on the bill of lading.
  • Carton marks versus packing list. Style, colour and size breakdowns that do not reconcile with the physical cartons — the same mismatch that causes a label-to-document question at destination.
  • Value that looks engineered. A declared value well below the valuation basis the officer expects for that fibre and construction.
  • Origin and marking. Country-of-origin marking on cartons and garments that does not match the certificate of origin.

One clean document set, with identical descriptions, weights, carton counts and marks across invoice, packing list and bill of lading, removes most of these. It is boring work and it is the cheapest insurance in the whole shipment.

How to shorten it when it happens

Speed comes from having a broker who already holds a power of attorney, a single person authorised to answer questions, and documents that can be produced as one coherent PDF within the hour — not assembled from three inboxes. Pre-clearance or pre-arrival filing where the market offers it helps, and so does a forwarder who tells you the exam status daily instead of you chasing it. Our note on why documents get rejected lists the specific inconsistencies to eliminate before loading.

Build a buffer in the plan rather than hoping. A routine exam is a day; an intensive one in peak season can be two weeks. If a retail launch date is fixed, that buffer belongs in the production lead time and the buying calendar, not in a last-minute air freight decision that costs more than the delay would have.

What to agree before the container is loaded

Four questions, asked before production finishes, settle almost everything: who is the importer of record and who holds the power of attorney; how destination exam and devan fees are billed; who answers a customs query and within what hours; and whether the packing list carries a lot-level breakdown that would let you isolate a single batch if an officer asks. Put the answers beside the quality standards document so the same references appear on the cartons, the samples and the entry.

We see buyers spend real energy negotiating a few dollars off the ocean rate and none on this. An exam is a low-probability, high-inconvenience event; the preparation for it costs nothing but attention.

Frequently asked questions

Does a customs exam mean my shipment did something wrong?

No. Most exams are random or driven by a risk profile that has nothing to do with your particular order. What turns an exam from a short delay into a problem is a document inconsistency found during it, so treat the selection as neutral and the paperwork as the part you control. Ask your broker which level of exam was ordered, because a scan and an intensive devan have very different timelines.

How long does an intensive exam add to a knitwear shipment?

A non-intrusive scan usually clears within hours to a day. An intensive exam, where the container is fully unloaded and cartons opened, runs from several working days to a fortnight in a congested port, plus reloading time. Peak season makes both worse because terminal labour and chassis availability are tight. Build a one-to-two week buffer for any order with a fixed launch date.

Can I avoid exam fees by shipping FOB?

No. FOB decides who books and pays the ocean freight and where risk transfers; it does not change who is the importer of record, and destination exam charges are raised against the import entry. On most FOB shipments the buyer pays them. What you can do is agree with your forwarder in advance how those fees are passed through, and keep the documents consistent so the exam stays at the cheapest level.
