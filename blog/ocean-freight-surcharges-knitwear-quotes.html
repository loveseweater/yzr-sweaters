Reading an Ocean Freight Quote: GRI, BAF, PSS and THC on Knitwear Shipments

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

A freight quote is a base ocean rate plus a stack of surcharges: fuel (BAF), currency (CAF), peak season (PSS), terminal handling (THC) at both ends, then documentation, seal, filing and security fees. A general rate increase (GRI) moves the base rate itself. The trap is comparing the headline rate between forwarders while the surcharge lists differ. Ask for an itemised, all-in quote with a validity window, and put that number — not the headline — into your landed cost.

We see this a lot with buyers who budget freight from a rate quoted in April and then get an invoice in September with three extra lines on it. Nothing was hidden maliciously; ocean pricing is simply built in layers, and knitwear is billed on volume, so those layers land on the CBM figure that drives everything. Here is what each line means and how to get two quotes you can actually compare.

The base rate is the small number

Poly-bagged sweaters are light for their volume, so sea freight is charged on measure (CBM), not kilos. A 40HQ normally cubes out long before it weighs out, which is why container planning and the LCL to FCL crossover matter more to your freight bill than the garment weight ever will. The base rate — dollars per CBM for LCL, or a lump sum for a container — is just the starting point. Everything below sits on top of it.

A forwarder quote that shows only that number is not a quote; it is an opening. The useful comparison is the all-in figure for identical cargo details.

GRI: the increase that moves the base rate

A general rate increase is a carrier announcement that the base rate on a lane goes up, usually effective from a specific date and often mid-month. It is not a surcharge and it is not negotiated by your forwarder — the carrier files it and the market follows. GRIs cluster before and during the strong shipping window, which for winter knitwear means roughly August to October. If a quote from April is still sitting in your cost sheet in September, the base rate in it is fiction.

This is the single biggest reason to record a validity window on every quote. Our peak season shipping guide covers the same window from the space-availability side; GRI is what the same pressure does to price.

BAF, CAF, and the fuel and currency adjustments

BAF (bunker adjustment factor, also called a fuel surcharge) passes changes in bunker fuel cost through to the shipper. CAF (currency adjustment factor) does the same for exchange-rate movement between the currency the carrier prices in and the one it collects in. Both are usually quoted per CBM or per container and both move without much notice. Carriers sometimes fold them into an "all-in" rate, which is fine — but then you need to know they are folded in, or you will add them twice when comparing quotes.

The currency one is worth watching alongside your FX clause with the factory. A buyer who hedges the goods price and ignores the freight currency can still lose the margin on the shipping line. Ask which currency each surcharge is denominated in and who absorbs the conversion.

PSS, congestion and equipment surcharges

PSS (peak season surcharge) is a temporary add-on during demand spikes. Congestion surcharges appear when a port is backed up, and equipment or imbalance surcharges show up when empty containers are in the wrong place. These are the lines that appear suddenly, are quoted for a specific period, and disappear again. They are also the ones most likely to be missing from a forwarder's first quote and present on the final invoice.

They are not negotiable line by line, but they are predictable in aggregate. If you are shipping autumn/winter stock in Q3, assume one of them will apply and put a buffer in the landed cost rather than treating it as a surprise. Pair that with the pre-CNY rush planning, which creates the second annual spike.

THC and the origin and destination charges

THC (terminal handling charge) covers the physical handling of the container at the terminal, and it exists at both ends — origin THC and destination THC. Then come the smaller items: documentation fee, seal fee, AMS/ENS or ISF filing fee, security or ISPS charge, telex release fee, and sometimes a bill of lading amendment fee. Individually small, together they routinely add 15 to 30 percent to the headline rate on LCL shipments.

Who pays which end is an Incoterms question, not a freight question. An FOB quote that includes origin charges but excludes destination THC is normal; the buyer then pays destination THC, clearance, duty and delivery. That is exactly why the same "USD 95 per CBM" can produce two very different invoices for two buyers. Our shipping documents note covers the telex release item that is often bundled here.

How to compare two quotes without being fooled

Send identical cargo details to every bidder — pieces, cartons, CBM, gross weight, port pair and Incoterm — then ask for three things in writing: origin and destination charges listed separately, an explicit list of what is excluded (duty, exam fees, demurrage, delivery beyond the port), and the validity window. Then reduce each quote to one all-in number for the same CBM. A USD 95 per CBM rate with USD 400 of undocumented destination fees is worse than USD 120 all-in.

Our forwarder comparison guide makes the same point from the relationship side, and the landed cost stack is where that all-in number belongs. Do not file the headline rate in the cost sheet; file the all-in figure and the date it was quoted.

What to write into the contract and the plan

Two habits remove most of this pain. First, when a rate is agreed, write the validity date and the surcharge basis into the supply contract or the booking confirmation, so a mid-season GRI is a documented adjustment rather than an argument. Second, for a fixed launch date, consider a contract or allocated-space rate instead of spot booking — it will not eliminate surcharges, but it makes the timing and the space predictable. Neither is worth much for flexible replenishment stock, where a cheaper spot rate plus a one-sailing buffer is usually the better trade.

Frequently asked questions

Is a GRI the same thing as a peak season surcharge?

No. A general rate increase changes the base ocean rate on a lane, usually from a specific effective date filed by the carrier. A peak season surcharge is a temporary add-on on top of the base rate for a defined period. Both cluster in the same Q3 window, which is why they get confused, but one moves the headline number and the other is a separate line on the invoice.

Can I lock an ocean freight rate for the whole season?

A contract rate or an allocated-space arrangement can hold the base rate for an agreed period and volume, but surcharges such as fuel, currency and peak season adjustments normally still pass through. Spot rates cannot be locked at all. For a fixed launch date the value of a contract is predictability of space and timing as much as price; for flexible replenishment stock a spot rate with a buffer is usually cheaper.

Why is my knitwear freight billed per CBM when the sweaters are so light?

Ocean LCL is charged on whichever is greater, volume or weight, and poly-bagged knitwear is light for its volume, so a container cubes out long before it reaches its weight limit. Carton dimensions and the folding method decide your CBM, and your CBM decides the freight bill and most of the surcharges on it. That is why carton planning and packing method are freight decisions, not just warehouse ones.
