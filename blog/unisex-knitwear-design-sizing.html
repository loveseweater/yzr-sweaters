Designing and Sizing Unisex Knitwear That Fits Both

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Unisex knitwear works when the silhouette does the compromising, not the customer. Relaxed blocks with dropped shoulders, generous armholes and a straight body fit a wide range of bodies of both sexes; slim, body-following blocks fit one of them. Grade one range (XS–XXL) on a menswear-informed base with slight length adjustments, publish garment measurements honestly, and fit-test every style on both a male and a female model before bulk. The reward is half the SKUs, easier inventory and a brand story that is currently in demand.

Every season we get more unisex briefs, and the reasons are practical as much as philosophical: one size range means half the styles, half the SKUs and half the inventory risk of running separate men's and women's versions of the same sweater — see the planning logic in our range planning guide. But unisex is also where we see the most fit failures, because "unisex" is often just a men's sweater with a size chart relabelled. The garments that work are designed for it from the block up. Here is how we approach it.

The silhouette decides everything

Fit lives or dies on the silhouette choice, and unisex narrows your workable options in a helpful way:

  • Relaxed and boxy blocks are the unisex sweet spot. A straight body with room through the chest and hip fits both a 96cm male chest and a female body without pulling at either. Boxy fits read intentional on both bodies, which is exactly what you want when the same garment must photograph well on two different frames.
  • Dropped shoulders are your best friend. The shoulder point is where men's and women's blocks differ most sharply — a set-in sleeve sized for a male shoulder is visibly wrong on a female frame, and vice versa. Dropping the shoulder seam 4–8cm and widening the armhole makes the garment tolerant of both, and it is the signature look of the category anyway.
  • Avoid the body-following middle ground. The fit that fails unisex is the semi-fitted one — too slim on male shoulders, too straight through the female waist and hip. Either commit to genuinely relaxed, or design a separate women's block. The in-between is where returns come from.
  • Cropped and longline cuts choose an audience. Cropped reads womenswear to most men; extreme longline reads the opposite. Classic body lengths — hem at high hip to mid-hip — are neutral.

Grading one range instead of two

The practical question we get asked most: do we grade one size run or two? Our answer, almost always, is one — and here is the reasoning. A unisex range typically runs XS to XXL on a base block drafted to menswear conventions: cross-back and shoulder width lead the grade, chest and waist grade together, and sleeve length grades generously. Womenswear bodies pick their size from the chest measurement in the chart; menswear bodies often size up once. The grading mechanics are unchanged — what changes is which measurements drive the grade rules.

Two refinements worth the effort: give sleeve length a generous grade increment (sleeve length is the most gendered measurement in knitwear and the most common unisex complaint), and consider a slight body-length grade adjustment toward the larger sizes so the garment does not read cropped on taller customers. Whether your labels say XS–XXL or use a dual-naming approach, keep the labelling consistent with the advice in our size naming guide — one system, clearly explained, beats two systems ambiguously mixed.

Fit sessions: both models, every style

This is the discipline that separates real unisex from relabelled menswear. Every unisex style gets fit-tested on a male and a female fit model, at minimum one size at the small end and one at the large end, following the process in our fit session guide. Watch four points: the shoulder drop looks stylish (not sloppily oversized) on both models; the armhole allows layering without swimming; body length flatters both; and the neckline sits cleanly on both neck/collarbone geometries. A half-day of dual fit sessions before bulk saves a season of returns — and the patterns of complaint in our returns handling guide are dominated by exactly these four points.

Fabric choice supports the block: a yarn with honest stretch and recovery forgives a wider range of bodies than a rigid one. And if the same style serves a plus-size customer base, extend the grade rather than scaling up the relaxed fit — the reasoning in our plus-size guide applies to the top end of a unisex run exactly as it does to dedicated plus ranges.

Selling it: the size chart and the photography

Unisex online retail succeeds or fails on the size chart. Publish garment measurements — chest, length, sleeve — with the note "designed for a relaxed unisex fit; women may prefer to size down", and let customers decide with a tape measure. The approach in our online size guide guide is the template: actual garment numbers beat body-size guessing every time, and doubly so when the customer is shopping outside their habitual section. Photography carries the story: shoot every style on both a male and a female model, and make both images visible on the product page — our ecommerce photography guide covers the shooting mechanics, and the dual-model gallery is the single highest-impact addition for a unisex range. Product copy should say "relaxed fit, dropped shoulder" explicitly; customers cannot feel the block through a screen.

Frequently asked questions

Should I start from a men's block or a women's block?

Start from a men's or menswear-informed base block, then relax it. Shoulder width and cross-back are the hardest dimensions to fake in either direction — a women's block on a male frame fails at the shoulders visibly and immediately, while a relaxed men's block works on most female frames once the body is straightened and the armhole is opened. Then confirm with dual-model fit sessions; the block is a starting hypothesis, the fit samples are the evidence.

How many sizes should a unisex range run?

Four to six: XS–XXL is the common full run, and S–XL covers a focused launch. The temptation to add a third XXL+ size is real if your audience includes larger male customers — but that is the point to apply plus-size grading logic rather than extending the relaxed grade, because shoulder-to-chest proportions start changing faster than the base grade assumes. Keep one grade rulebook per range and document it.

Does unisex actually save money, or just simplify?

Both, with caveats. One style instead of two means one development, one sampling cycle and half the SKU count — real savings in sampling and inventory. But per-SKU volume doubles in the successful sizes, which improves knitting efficiency, while the size curve flattens because one range now serves two populations. The financial win is inventory risk more than unit price: fewer styles to mark down when a season misses, and the markdown exposure is where unisex buyers usually find their profit.
