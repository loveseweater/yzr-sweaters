Selling Knitwear in Southeast Asia: A Seasonless Sweater Market

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Southeast Asia buys knitwear all year, not in seasons. Demand is driven by air-conditioned interiors and modest-coverage dressing rather than cold weather, so the range skews lightweight cotton, viscose and blends in fine gauges, with knitted polos and cardigans carrying the category. Operationally, three things decide whether the programme works: humidity-proof packing and storage, delivery timed to the festival calendar — year-end sale events, Chinese New Year and Ramadan in the Muslim-majority markets — and sizing built per market rather than graded down from a Western block.

Buyers who have built a European or North American programme often look at Southeast Asia and see a small sweater market, because nobody there needs a winter coat. What they miss is that the region buys the light end of the knitwear range every month of the year, through some of the most active e-commerce channels in the world. We see this a lot with customers in Singapore, Malaysia and the Philippines: modest order quantities per style, but reorder frequency like a basics programme. It is a different rhythm, and it rewards suppliers who understand it.

The air-conditioning market — all twelve months of it

Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand, the Philippines and Vietnam share a pattern: 30°C outside, 22°C in the office, the mall, the cinema and the bus. The customer's problem is not cold, it is the transition — and the solution is a layer that lives in a bag or over a chair back.

That produces a demand profile quite different from Europe:

  • Fine-gauge, low-weight knits that fold to nothing — open cardigans, shrugs, light wraps. Much of the range sits closer to a spring/summer programme than an autumn/winter one, except it replenishes year-round instead of once a season.
  • Breathable fibres dominate. Cotton, viscose, lyocell and their blends — our notes on cotton knitwear and lyocell and plant fibres cover the yarn choices. Wool survives only in tiny travel-wardrobe niches.
  • Coverage matters in the Muslim-majority markets. Malaysia, Indonesia and Brunei drive long-sleeve layering and modest silhouettes for office and formal wear — closer to the coverage logic we describe for the Middle East, but in lighter fabrications.
  • Knitted polos are a serious category, worn as smart-casual office wear across the region — see our guide to knitted polos at wholesale.

Humidity is the operational risk

In Europe, a polybagged sweater mostly sits dry. In Southeast Asia, monsoon-season humidity reaches deep into warehouses and stockrooms, and cardboard, labels and garments all absorb moisture. Three consequences we plan for with customers shipping into the region:

  • Mould and mildew risk on natural fibres. Cotton and viscose programmes need the same dry-pack discipline as wool — the failure modes and prevention measures are covered in our notes on mould and mildew in storage and shipping, including desiccants and container humidity control.
  • Storage at destination is part of your spec. If the goods will sit in a non-climate-controlled warehouse through a monsoon, say so when you plan packaging — sealed inner packs earn their cost back.
  • Metal trims and needles corrode. Humidity accelerates tarnish on cheap hardware, which matters for closures and zips on cardigans destined for the region.

The calendar has three peaks, not one

This is where buyers new to the region lose the most money. There is no single "season"; there are three separate commercial moments, and they follow different clocks:

  • Year-end festival and sale period (October–December). The 11.11 and 12.12 e-commerce festivals, Christmas gifting in the Philippines in particular, and year-end travel. Goods need to be in-market by late October.
  • Chinese New Year — the biggest gifting and new-clothes moment in Singapore, Malaysia and Vietnam's ethnic-Chinese retail. It moves with the lunar calendar, so the buying calendar has to be rebuilt each year rather than copied; our notes on the buying calendar apply with that adjustment.
  • Ramadan and Hari Raya / Eid in Malaysia, Indonesia and Brunei — a major new-wardrobe moment that also drifts through the calendar annually, exactly as it does in the Gulf. Missing Hari Raya in Kuala Lumpur or Jakarta is like missing Christmas in Europe.

Between these peaks, the seasonless baseline keeps replenishment going. The practical result is smaller, more frequent orders — which makes low-MOQ production and inventory replenishment planning more important here than in a two-season European programme.

Sizing varies more between markets than buyers expect

There is no "Asian size" — the differences run market by market. Vietnamese and Filipino size expectations sit smaller on average than Malaysian; Singapore's sizing follows a mix of local and international standards because its retail is fully internationalised. Two practical rules:

  • Build the block per market rather than grading one Western block down. The logic is the same as our notes on plus-size programmes: a market-specific block is cheaper than fighting a wrong one. Our guides to sizing and grading and size naming cover the mechanics.
  • State sizing in body measurements, not letters. "M" means different things in Manila and Munich; a measurement-based size guide matters disproportionately in a region where most of the selling happens online.

Logistics, duties and the e-commerce reality

  • Duties vary sharply by country, and several ASEAN members apply preferential rates to Chinese-origin goods under the regional trade agreement, typically with a certificate of origin. Which forms apply and to what value depends on the destination and the HS classification — the mechanics of classification and duty planning are covered in our notes on HS codes and duties.
  • E-commerce is the channel. Shopee, Lazada and TikTok Shop move a large share of regional apparel, which changes packaging (individually sealed, retail-ready), listing photography, and return rates — our notes on reducing consumer returns matter more in a high-return online channel.
  • Freight is mostly sea, with LCL common because of smaller, mixed consignments. Consolidation discipline and pack integrity pay off here — and the humidity point above means container conditions deserve attention in the incoterms and freight planning conversation.
  • Labelling is per-market: fibre declarations and care labelling rules differ, and Indonesian-language labelling is enforced in Indonesia. Verify requirements per destination rather than assuming one regional standard — our notes on care labelling rules are the starting point.

Where buyers go wrong

Three recurring mistakes. Treating the region as one market — it is at least six, with different sizing, festivals and labelling. Shipping a European autumn range with the labels changed. And underestimating humidity, which quietly destroys stock in a monsoon-season warehouse. The fixes are all conversational, not technical: decide the market list first, build the range for the climate second, and pack for the warehouse the goods will actually sit in.

Frequently asked questions

Do people in tropical countries actually buy knitwear?

Yes, in volume — but not winter knitwear. Demand is driven by cold air-conditioned interiors and modest-coverage dressing, so it concentrates in lightweight fine-gauge cardigans, wraps, layering pieces and knitted polos, in cotton, viscose and blends. Unlike Europe, the demand is seasonless, which suits smaller and more frequent reorders.

Which festivals should I plan deliveries around?

Three peaks: the year-end period including the 11.11 and 12.12 sale festivals and Christmas (strongest in the Philippines), Chinese New Year for Singapore, Malaysia and Vietnam, and Ramadan followed by Hari Raya or Eid for Malaysia, Indonesia and Brunei. The last two follow lunar calendars and shift each year, so rebuild the delivery plan annually rather than reusing last year's dates.

What is the biggest operational risk shipping knitwear to the region?

Humidity, particularly during monsoon months. Natural fibres in unsealed packs can develop mould in warehouses and containers. Use dry-packing, desiccants and sealed inner packs for natural-fibre programmes, and factor in how and where the goods will be stored at destination.

Can I use the same size range across Southeast Asia?

Not reliably. Size expectations differ between markets — for example Vietnamese and Filipino averages sit smaller than Malaysian, and Singapore is fully internationalised. Build blocks per market, express sizes in body measurements for online selling, and treat the region as several markets rather than one.
