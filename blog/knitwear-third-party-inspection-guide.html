Third-Party Knitwear Inspection: A Buyer's Guide to Using SGS, BV and Intertek

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

A third-party inspection is an independentQC check — typically SGS, Bureau Veritas or Intertek — performed at the factory against your approved specification and an AQL sampling plan, before shipment. Use it when the factory is new, the order is high-value, or a retailer demands independent proof. Budget a few hundred US dollars per man-day plus travel; book it once goods are 100% made and 80% packed; and pair it with the factory's own inline checks rather than treating it as a substitute. The report is your leverage if something is wrong.

The phrase "we inspect our own goods" is reassuring until the goods arrive and they are not what you approved. That gap — between a factory's self-check and an independent verdict — is exactly what a third-party inspection fills, and it is one of the most cost-effective risk controls a knitwear buyer has. Buyers new to Asian sourcing often assume the factory's QC is enough, or assume an external inspector will catch everything; both assumptions cause avoidable losses. Here is how the major inspection companies actually work and when they earn their fee.

What third-party inspection actually is

A third-party inspection is an independent QC company — most commonly SGS, Bureau Veritas (BV) or Intertek, with TÜV and QIMA also active — sending its own inspector to the factory to check your finished goods against your specification. It is distinct from the factory's own quality control, including the inline inspection we run during production and the pre-shipment check we perform before packing. The value is independence: the inspector works for you, not the factory, and their report is a neutral record you can act on or, if needed, use in a dispute. For many retailers it is also a contractual requirement — they will not accept a self-declared pass.

When it is worth the money

Third-party inspection is not free, so spend it where it changes outcomes. The clear cases: a new or unproven factory, where you have no track record to trust; a high-value or high-volume order, where a few hundred dollars of inspection protects thousands of dollars of goods; a retailer or contract mandate that demands independent proof; and any programme where a defect recall would be catastrophic — children's knitwear is the obvious example, where our children's safety compliance work already pairs with independent testing. For a repeat order with a factory you have inspected three times and never failed, the marginal value drops and you can rely more on inline checks plus a spot audit. The judgement is risk versus cost, not a blanket rule.

How AQL sampling works in practice

Inspectors do not check every garment — that would be a full sort, not an inspection. They use an Acceptable Quality Limit (AQL) sampling plan, and our AQL guide explains the maths; the short version is that the batch size and your chosen AQL level (commonly 2.5 for general knitwear, tighter for premium or children's) determine how many pieces are randomly pulled and checked. Those pieces are assessed for workmanship defects, measurements against your quality standard document, colour, packing and presentation. The result is a pass, a fail, or a pass-with-defects that you decide whether to accept. The randomness is the point: a clean sample is statistically meaningful only if the pull was truly random across cartons and colours.

What a good report covers — and what it misses

A proper inspection report records the sampling plan, the pieces checked, defect counts by type, key measurements, photographs of good and bad, and an overall verdict with the inspector's signature. It should also capture metal-needle detection results where relevant and flag any restricted-substance concern for lab follow-up. What it does not do is replace laboratory testing: an inspector can see a colour mismatch but cannot measure formaldehyde or phthalates, which is why physical inspection pairs with lab testing rather than replacing it. We tell buyers to read the report's defect breakdown, not just the headline pass/fail, because a "pass" at the edge of the AQL can still hide a systematic issue that will show up in returns.

Costs, booking and the factory's role

Pricing is straightforward: a man-day rate (commonly a few hundred US dollars) plus any travel surcharge to a remote factory, and you pay the inspector directly, not through the factory. Book when the order is 100% completed and around 80% packed, so the inspector can sample from closed cartons. The factory's cooperation matters — we provide the inspector access, the approved samples, and the production documents without friction, because a smooth inspection reflects on us too. If an inspection fails, the path is rework and a re-check, and our failed-inspection rework process is how we handle it. The cheapest outcome is preventing the failure upstream through inline control, with the third party as the independent confirmation rather than the only net.

Frequently asked questions

When should I pay for third-party knitwear inspection?

Use it when the factory is new or unproven, the order is high-value or high-volume, a retailer mandates independent proof, or a defect would be catastrophic — children's wear especially. For established repeat programmes with a clean history, lean on the factory's inline checks plus an occasional spot audit. The decision is risk versus cost, not a blanket rule.

Does an AQL inspection check every sweater?

No. AQL sampling pulls a statistically determined random sample — set by batch size and your chosen AQL level, commonly 2.5 for general knitwear — and inspects those pieces for defects, measurements, colour, packing and presentation. A clean random sample is meaningful; a non-random or tiny pull is not. It is a risk-based check, not a full sort.

Does third-party inspection replace lab testing?

No. An inspector can see a seam fault or a colour shift but cannot measure formaldehyde, phthalates or fibre content. Physical inspection and lab testing are complementary: the inspector flags what to send to the lab, the lab gives the certifiable numbers. Use both on programmes where compliance proof matters.
