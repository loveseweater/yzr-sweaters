First Order vs Reorder: Right Quantities for Knitwear

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

A first order and a reorder are doing different jobs. The first buys information at the lowest sensible cost — enough depth to read sell-through on a few styles, not so much that unsold stock funds a bad guess. A reorder buys volume on a proven style. Brands that plan the two together, with the same colour and yarn decisions in mind, avoid the usual outcome: a first order too thin to read, followed by a reorder that cannot match the original or arrives after the selling window.

The quantity question gets asked as a single number, and it is really two questions. How much should go into the first order to learn what sells, and how should the reorder behind it be set up so that it can actually happen? Buyers who answer only the first end up with a bestseller they cannot restock in time; buyers who answer only the second over-commit before they have any evidence.

What is a first order actually for?

It is information, purchased as cheaply as the production minimums allow. The purpose is to learn sell-through by style, colour and size on real customers, in a real selling window, before committing to depth. That framing changes how the quantity should be set.

Two failure modes follow from ignoring it. An order too thin to read gives you a sell-out on three sizes and dead stock on the rest, which tells you nothing actionable — the sample was too small and too skewed. An order too deep converts a bad guess into a warehouse problem, and the cost of that stock is capital you cannot spend on the styles that did work.

The realistic target is the smallest quantity that clears the factory's minimum while spreading across enough styles and colours to produce a readable result. Our notes on sweater MOQ explain how the minimum is set, and low-MOQ manufacturing covers the paths available when the number is small.

How should the first-order quantity be divided?

Division matters more than the total. A single number placed on one style tells you about that style; the same number spread across four styles and two colours tells you something about each and about the range as a whole.

  • Width before depth. More styles at shallow depth reads the range; fewer styles at deep depth reads nothing until it is too late to act. Buyers testing a market should almost always widen before deepening.
  • Enough per size to be readable. A style that lands four units in a size cannot tell you whether that size sells. Our notes on size ratio planning explain how to split across sizes from expected demand rather than evenly.
  • Colour depth where the colour is core, breadth where it is fashion. Neutrals earn depth because a sell-out is likely and a reorder is worth planning. A seasonal colour earns breadth across styles, because the point is to see which silhouette carries it.
  • Include a deliberate control. Carrying one core style at slightly more depth gives you a baseline — if even the safe style does not sell, the problem is the price, the channel or the season rather than the design.

The result is often a first order that is larger in total than the buyer first imagined but shallower per style, which is usually the right trade.

What has to be true for a reorder to be possible?

Four conditions, and all four have to be set up before the first order ships. Missing any one of them turns a sell-out into a lost season.

The yarn has to still exist. This is the binding constraint more often than capacity. A bespoke colour dyed for the first order may need a fresh dye lot for a reorder, and that lot may not match. Holding yarn from the original lot is the reliable answer, and our notes on yarn stock planning explain why it is a strategic decision rather than a purchasing detail. Our notes on colour consistency cover what happens when it is not held.

The lead time has to fit inside the selling window. A reorder that arrives after the season is stock, not sales. Production plus freight plus clearance is typically measured in months, so the decision to reorder has to be triggered early — often while sell-through data is still thin. That is uncomfortable, and it is the reason a shorter first cycle is worth paying a little more for.

The price has to be agreed in advance. A reorder negotiated from scratch at the moment of urgency is negotiated from a weak position. Our notes on supply contracts cover where reorder terms belong.

The specification has to be fixed. Any change to yarn, trim or colour between orders makes the reorder a new style — with a new sample, a new fit approval and a new lead time. Our notes on the bill of materials explain how to keep the specification stable.

How should the reorder quantity be set?

From the sell-through rate rather than from the original quantity, and with the production reality in mind.

If a style sold 70 percent of its first order inside the planned window, the reorder should aim to cover the remaining demand plus the expected tail, not simply repeat the original number. Our notes on inventory planning set out the arithmetic, including safety stock and the lead-time rule.

There is also a minimum consideration. A reorder below the factory's minimum may not be producible at the original price, and a mill may require a full dye lot even for a modest garment quantity — the constraint our notes on yarn minimums describe. Working out the smallest sensible reorder before the season starts avoids discovering it in the middle of one.

What should a buyer agree with the supplier up front?

  • The reorder price and its validity period, so the second order does not start from a fresh negotiation. Our notes on price negotiation cover how to structure that.
  • Whether yarn will be held for a reorder, and at whose cost. Holding yarn is the single most effective way to make a reorder possible, and it has to be agreed before production rather than after a sell-out.
  • The reorder lead time, stated separately from the first-order lead time — it is usually shorter because sampling is not repeated, but only if nothing changes.
  • What cannot change without becoming a new style. Yarn, colour, trims and construction. Our notes on changing an order cover what can be adjusted and when.
  • The lot marking convention, so a reorder can be identified and merchandised separately if the shade steps.
  • A decision on stock yarn versus bespoke colour for the styles most likely to be reordered. A core neutral in a stock yarn is a reorder waiting to happen; a signature shade in a bespoke dye is not.

Frequently asked questions

How many styles should a first knitwear order cover?

Enough to read the range rather than bet on one design — commonly four to eight styles at shallow depth for a new brand, within whatever the factory minimum allows. Width before depth is the principle: a sell-out on one style at four units tells you far less than a readable result across several.

Why can't I simply reorder the same quantity later?

You can, but the number should come from sell-through rather than from the original order. More importantly, the reorder has to be possible at all: the yarn may no longer match, the lead time may not fit the selling window, and the price may not be held. Those conditions are set up before the first order ships, not after.

Should I hold yarn for a reorder?

For styles you expect to repeat, yes — holding the original yarn lot is the most reliable way to make a colour-matched reorder possible, and it is far cheaper than a mismatched second delivery. For fashion colours and one-season styles, holding yarn converts a possible sell-out into certain dead stock.
