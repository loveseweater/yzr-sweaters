Custom Sweaters Explained: The OEM/ODM Process from Sample to Shipment

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Buying stock sweaters is simple. Ordering custom, private label knitwear is a different process — and understanding it is the key to getting exactly what you want. If you've never worked with an OEM or ODM factory before, the amount of back and forth can be surprising. But once you know the steps, it becomes straightforward.

This guide breaks down the whole journey so you know what to prepare, what to expect, and where the common mistakes happen.

OEM vs ODM: what's the difference?

The two terms are often used interchangeably, but they mean different things.

  • OEM (Original Equipment Manufacturer) means you bring your own design and the factory produces it to your spec. You own the design and the brand; the factory is the maker.
  • ODM (Original Design Manufacturer) means the factory offers its own designs, patterns and samples that you can take, adjust and sell under your label. Useful if you don't have a design team but want to move fast.

Many buyers combine both: they pick a manufacturer with solid design capability, adapt an existing style, and then make it their own. That's the flexibility a good knitwear manufacturer should offer.

What to send your manufacturer

The more complete your information, the faster and more accurate your sample will be. You don't need everything, but the clearer you are, the fewer revision rounds you'll need:

  • A tech pack — measurements, garment sketch, materials and construction notes. The gold standard if you have one.
  • A reference photo — often enough to start, especially with a sketch and a few measurements.
  • A physical reference sample — the best option, since the factory can measure and copy the hand-feel directly.
  • Target yarn and gauge — wool, cashmere blend, acrylic, cotton, etc., plus the look you're after.
  • Size range and quantity — so the factory can plan grading and production cost.

If you only have a photo and an idea, don't worry. A competent manufacturer will ask the right questions and help turn your vision into a producible spec.

The sample and revision loop

Sampling is where your idea becomes physical. The first sample is likely a close approximation rather than a perfect match. Treat round one as a starting point and be specific about what needs to change — fit, sleeve length, collar shape, stitch density, color.

Expect one or two rounds of refinement before you're happy. Once you approve, keep a reference copy and sign off on the specs in writing: yarn, gauge, colors, sizes, trims. This is the benchmark your bulk order will be checked against, so make sure it's clear.

Bulk production

After approval, the factory places yarn orders and begins production. This is the longest stage, and it's where you want good communication. Ask for updates at key points and confirm that the QC process includes in-process checks during knitting and assembly, plus a final inspection before packing.

You can also arrange a third-party inspection before shipment. Agree on acceptance criteria and tolerances up front — things like color shading and gauge consistency — so there's no ambiguity when the goods are ready.

Private label and packaging

For brands and retailers, the branding is what makes the sweater yours. Before bulk production, decide on:

  • Woven neck labels and a care/washing label
  • Hang tags with your logo and price
  • Polybags, tissue, boxes or gift packaging
  • Any special finishing like a branded button or zip puller

Baking all of this into production is far more efficient than trying to add it later. Provide your artwork and the factory will handle the sourcing of the trims.

Protecting your design

If you've invested in a design, you understandably want to keep it yours. A few practical steps: work with a manufacturer you've vetted, keep your tech pack and reference samples documented, and consider a non-disclosure agreement if you're sharing sensitive designs. For a truly distinctive design, look into design registration or a design patent in your own market, since these are enforced where you sell rather than at the factory.

Beyond legal protection, the best safeguard is a working relationship built on trust — a manufacturer who values repeat business won't risk it by copying your work.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Skipping the sample. No sample, no contract. This is where you catch the problems.
  • Vague specs. "Just make it like the photo" leads to surprises. Be specific or let the factory ask questions.
  • Ignoring packaging until the end. Plan your labels and packaging early.
  • Not agreeing on acceptance criteria. Decide what "pass" means before production, not after.
