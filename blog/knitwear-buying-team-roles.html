Who Decides? Understanding the Buying Team at a Knitwear Brand

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

A knitwear buying decision is rarely made by one person. Designers set direction, buyers decide styles and quantities, merchandisers own delivery and cost execution, and quality and compliance teams control inspection and supplier approval. Reaching the right person with the right information matters more than writing a polished email to the wrong inbox.

Suppliers lose orders to internal structure as often as to price. A message lands with someone who has no authority over the decision, gets forwarded to nobody, and dies quietly. Understanding who does what inside a knitwear brand — and what each role is judged on — turns a contact list into a conversation with someone who can actually place a purchase order.

What does each role in a knitwear buying team care about?

Seven roles matter most in apparel sourcing.

  • Designer — owns the material hand feel, silhouette and colour direction. Judges whether a supplier can realise the design intent: yarn quality, stitch, gauge and finishing.
  • Buyer (also merchandiser in some companies) — owns what sells, the retail price band, the launch date, initial buy and reorder. Judges commercial viability, not craft.
  • Merchandiser — owns delivery dates, capacity planning, replenishment and order execution. Judges whether a factory holds its promised dates.
  • Sourcing manager — owns supplier onboarding, quotation comparison, contracts and payment terms. Judges total landed cost and risk.
  • QA (quality assurance) — owns inspection standards, defect tolerance and testing requirements. Judges consistency between the approved sample and bulk.
  • Compliance — owns certifications, social audits and material regulation, including the UFLPA (Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act) in the US and EU textile rules. Judges documentary evidence.
  • Founder or owner, in small brands — frequently covers several roles at once and judges cash flow and risk above everything else.

How does brand size change the decision structure?

  • Small brand: the founder decides everything. Fast answers, small quantities, and often a first order below a factory's standard MOQ — minimum order quantity, the smallest order a factory will accept for a style. Our guide to sweater MOQ covers how to fit a first order into that limit.
  • Mid-size brand: buyers and merchandisers split the work. They value reliable delivery, repeatable quality and a supplier who flags problems early.
  • Large brand or retailer: multi-department process with formal onboarding, capacity evidence and compliance documentation. Slower decisions, larger volumes and long-term programs.

Who should the first email go to?

Start with the person who owns the product decision, not a general inbox. A customer service or webshop address will not route your message to the buying team. Prioritise the buyer, sourcing manager or founder, depending on brand size. To identify them, check the brand's team page, search LinkedIn by job title and brand name, or look for names on press releases and trade show exhibitor listings. A named person with a role beats a generic mailbox every time.

How should you write to each different role?

  • To a designer: lead with material and construction — yarn options, gauge, stitch and finish — and offer a sample in a chosen colour.
  • To a buyer: lead with commercial fit — style references, price band, and whether the factory can hit their launch date.
  • To a merchandiser: lead with capacity and timing — knit capacity, sample lead time, production windows and their effect on delivery.
  • To sourcing: lead with structure — price basis, Incoterms (the International Commercial Terms setting who pays freight, insurance and duties), MOQ and lead time.
  • To QA and compliance: lead with evidence — audit summaries, certification documents, test reports and a willingness to accept inspection.

Sending the same pitch to all five reads as a bulk mailing. One reference garment, one target quantity and one clear next step carry further than a full catalogue — the same discipline that makes a sourcing enquiry quotable.

How do you address several roles in one email?

Assume your message will be forwarded. Structure it in labelled sections so a colleague can read only the relevant block: material and sampling for design, price and MOQ for sourcing, delivery for merchandising, certification for compliance. Close with one question directed at the recipient, and ask to be pointed to the right colleague if they are not the decision maker. That single sentence often produces an internal referral that no cold approach would earn.

What if the decision chain is long?

Map the chain rather than pushing against it. Work out who sees the product first, who can reject it, and who signs the order. Then give each stage what it needs: a sample for the first conversation, a clear price and lead time for the evaluation, and documentary evidence when the discussion turns formal. Ask early who else needs to be involved, and keep every reply short enough to forward. When a sample is approved by design but the price is queried by sourcing, answer with the cost breakdown rather than a discount — the quotation breakdown is what gets compared.

Frequently asked questions

Is a fashion buyer the same as a merchandiser?

No, though small brands often merge the two. A buyer decides what to purchase: styles, quantities, colourways and price points. A merchandiser executes the purchase: order placement, delivery schedules, replenishment and problem-solving when production slips. In supplier terms, the buyer sets the target and the merchandiser holds you to it.

Should a supplier contact the designer or the sourcing manager first?

Contact the person who owns product selection — usually the buyer, or the founder in a small brand — and let them route you internally. Designers are excellent starting points when you have a strong material or construction story, but they rarely control the purchase order. Sourcing managers are the right audience once a style is already under consideration and price is the live question.

How does a supplier get onto a brand's approved vendor list?

Onboarding is a process, not an email. Expect to provide company information, capacity details, certification documents, and a quotation against a specific style, followed by samples and often a factory audit or video walkthrough. Passing a trial order is the most reliable way onto the list, because it proves delivery and consistency rather than presentation.
