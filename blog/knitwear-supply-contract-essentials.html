Knitwear Supply Contracts: Key Terms Buyers Should Nail Down

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Most sourcing disputes do not start with bad faith. They start with a purchase order that left something unsaid, or a sourcing agreement where two sides read the same sentence differently. For knitwear the gaps are predictable: a colour that was never pinned to a standard, a size spec that drifted, a lead time with no consequence attached to it. Here is what a garment supply contract should say before a single cone of yarn is booked.

Specification comes before price

A knitwear contract is only as firm as the specification attached to it. Treat the tech pack as a binding annex, referenced by version number, and make sure it fixes:

  • Fibre composition with a tolerance for blend variation, plus a stated allowance for what is acceptable in testing.
  • Construction — gauge, stitch, yarn count or count range, and the target weight (GSM or garment weight).
  • Measurements by size, with a point-of-measure list and the tolerance per point.
  • Colour standard — a Pantone or a submitted lab dip, and who signs it off. "Navy as discussed" protects nobody.
  • The approved sample, sealed and signed by both parties, which becomes the reference in any later argument about quality.

Quantity and shipping tolerance

Knitted garment production is not exact. Write the over/under shipment allowance into the contract as a percentage rather than relying on custom, state whether the buyer must accept the shortfall or the surplus, and say how the difference is invoiced. For programmes split across several colourways, it is worth setting the tolerance per colour, not only on the order total — otherwise a factory can deliver the full quantity in the two colours that were easiest to run.

Price, currency and what is included

Fix the currency, the Incoterm and the price basis (per piece, FOB or otherwise). Then list what the price covers and what it does not: yarn, trims, labels, hangtags, polybags, cartons, testing, inspection fees, and any export documentation. Yarn prices move, so agree in advance whether a quoted price holds for a stated period, and on what basis it may be revisited.

Payment schedule

Deposit and balance should be tied to milestones, not to a calendar. A deposit against order confirmation covers yarn and machine booking; the balance is best linked to a completed, inspected shipment rather than to a fixed date. If your terms include a mid-stage payment, attach it to something observable — goods knitted or linked, photographs sent, an on-site check. Payment terms and the inspection plan belong in the same document.

Delivery dates and delay liability

A date without a consequence is a wish. Agree the delivery window, what counts as an on-time handover (ex-factory date or vessel departure, for example), and how delay is handled — remedy, credit, partial cancellation or a right to renegotiate the shipping method. Buyers usually see a stronger position if the contract also lists what does not excuse a delay, and what notice the supplier must give when a problem is foreseeable.

Quality standard and how it is verified

  • Name the inspection standard. AQL on a defined defect classification, with a stated level for major and minor defects, is clearer than "good quality".
  • Say who inspects and when. In-line, pre-shipment, or both — and confirm the buyer's right to appoint a third party such as SGS or BV.
  • Fix the cost of failed inspection. Who pays for a repeat check, rework or re-inspection?
  • Include measurable durability expectations such as colourfastness, pilling and shrinkage, tested to a named method.

Claims, remedies and packaging

A claim mechanism that no one can use is decoration. Set the window for raising a defect claim after delivery, the evidence required, and the ladder of remedies: replacement, repair, credit or return. For packaged goods, specify carton marks, barcodes, polybag and labelling requirements, and the packing list. Shipping marks are cheap to state and expensive to get wrong.

Force majeure, IP and dispute resolution

  • Force majeure should be narrow and notice-based. Broad clauses that cover any supply difficulty are hard to enforce and easy to abuse.
  • Intellectual property — who owns the design, the pattern, the artwork and the labels, and whether the supplier may sell the style to others or display it in marketing.
  • Dispute resolution — governing law, venue, and whether you arbitrate or litigate. Choose a forum both parties can realistically use.

This article is general information for buyers, not legal advice. Contract law varies by jurisdiction and by transaction, so have your own lawyer or compliance adviser review the terms you intend to sign.
