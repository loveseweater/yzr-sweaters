Changing a Knitwear Order: What Can Be Adjusted and When

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

A knitwear order can be changed as long as the change is asked for early enough. Before yarn is purchased almost anything is free; once yarn is dyed, a colour change means writing off that yarn; once knitting has started, style changes are effectively impossible and quantity changes are limited by remaining capacity. The cost of an amendment is decided by the production stage, not by negotiation skill.

Orders change. A retail buyer cuts a size, a colour tests badly, a container slips. What decides whether the change is cheap or expensive is not the factory's goodwill but where the order stands on the production timeline. Knowing that timeline lets you ask early and ask once.

What is the production timeline, and what can be changed at each stage?

Knitwear production runs through a fixed sequence: quote and confirmation, yarn purchase, yarn dyeing, knitting, linking and trimming, washing and finishing, then packing. Each stage locks something in.

  • Quote or pre-confirmation — nothing is committed. Colour, style, quantity and delivery can all change at no cost.
  • Order confirmed, yarn not yet bought — changes to colour, quantity and trims are still cheap.
  • Yarn ordered or dyed — a colour change writes off that yarn; quantity moves only within a small band.
  • Knitting under way — style changes are out; quantity changes are capped by remaining machine capacity.
  • Finishing or packing — only packing, labelling and shipping details remain open.

Which changes are cheapest, and which are worst?

Quantity

An increase is usually met with a yes: the factory has the yarn recipe, the program and the machine time, and a bigger run spreads fixed cost. A reduction removes capacity the factory may already have blocked, and it can push the yarn order below the mill's minimum order quantity (MOQ).

Colour

A colour change after dyeing discards dyed yarn and adds a new dye lot, so it is normally the most expensive change that leaves the garment itself unchanged.

Style

A style change is a new order in disguise: new program, new sample, new approval. Agree a new delivery date rather than expecting the original one to hold.

Delivery date

Moving a date out is usually manageable. Pulling one forward depends on whether a machine and a finishing slot are genuinely free, not on whether the order exists in the plan.

Why do factories ask to be compensated for a quantity reduction?

Because the commitment was made against the original quantity. The mill was told how much yarn to spin or dye, the knitters were allocated, and other customers may have been turned away for that window. A reduction leaves the factory holding materials and idle capacity it cannot always resell, so the standard industry practice is for the buyer to cover costs already incurred. In practice this is handled three ways: you take the original quantity, you pay for the yarn already committed, or you agree a compensation figure both sides can live with.

What happens if you cancel the order outright?

Cancellation is not a single event but a point on the same timeline. Cancel before yarn is bought and there is little to argue about. Cancel after dyeing and the yarn becomes the buyer's liability; cancel mid-knitting and the factory will typically invoice the knitting already done plus work in progress. The buyer generally owns the costs the order has already generated, and the factory keeps or resells whatever can be salvaged. Read the cancellation clause before you sign.

How do you make a change as cheaply as possible?

  • Say it early. A message the day you decide beats a message the day before shipment.
  • Send all changes in one message. Five separate amendments cost the factory five re-plans, and you will pay for them.
  • Trade volume for flexibility. Adding styles or units to a later programme is often worth more to the factory than the money you are arguing about.
  • Accept a close colour. A shade already on the dyehouse's colour card can be knitted from existing yarn; a new lab dip adds time and a minimum dyed quantity.
  • Ask what the change costs before confirming it. The updated costing tells you whether the change is worth making.

How should you negotiate and document an amendment?

Put the change in writing and ask for a written response. A workable amendment record covers the new specification, the revised quantity and colour references, the effect on unit price, the revised delivery date, and which costs are accepted by whom. Ask directly whether the change affects capacity already booked for your order, because that answer determines the answer to everything else. Request the revised costing line by line, the same discipline as reading a sweater quotation, rather than accepting a single adjusted unit price.

How do you prevent amendments altogether?

Most changes come from details left loose at confirmation. Before you place the order, get the colour reference signed off, confirm the size ratio and grading, settle the packing, labelling and carton details, and check the delivery date against your own shipping plan. Two weeks of slack in the delivery date absorbs most small shocks without a formal amendment.

Frequently asked questions

Can I add quantity after the order is confirmed?

Usually yes, provided the yarn is still available and the machine time has not been reallocated. Increases are the easiest change to agree because they improve the factory's utilisation. Ask for the revised delivery date at the same time, because the added units may need a second production slot.

Is a colour change possible once the yarn is dyed?

Only by treating the dyed yarn as a write-off and dyeing a new lot, which the buyer normally pays for. If the amount is small, ask whether the yarn can be used on another style or colourway instead of being discarded.

Who pays if a delivery date has to move?

It depends on why it moved. If the factory is late, the delay is the factory's responsibility and penalty terms in the contract apply. If the buyer moves the date, the factory may still charge for holding finished goods or for the gap created in its schedule.
