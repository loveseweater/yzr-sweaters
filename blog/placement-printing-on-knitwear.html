Printing on Knitwear: Logos and Placement Prints That Survive Stretch

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

You can print on knitwear, but a knit is not a woven and the print has to be planned for it. Loops, stretch and surface texture mean inks and transfers behave differently: water-based screen printing and knit-friendly inks stretch with the fabric; heavy plastisol and cheap heat-transfer vinyl sit on top and crack or peel. Print on smooth, stable areas, keep artwork away from ribbing and heavy cables, and always wash-test and stretch-test a printed sample before approving bulk.

A print that looks perfect in the artwork file can fail on a sweater in three different ways — it cracks when the customer pulls the garment on, it disappears into the loops, or it peels after two washes. We see this a lot with brands whose print experience comes from jersey T-shirts, where almost anything works. Knitwear forgives less. Here's what actually holds up on a knitted surface, and how to brief it so your factory produces the version you imagined.

Why knits are harder to print than wovens

Three properties of knitted fabric change everything for a print shop:

  • The surface is loops, not a plane. Ink has to bridge the peaks of the stitch structure. Fine detail and thin lines that survive on a poplin shirt get swallowed between loops on a 12-gauge knit and simply vanish on a 5-gauge chunky one. This interacts directly with gauge choice — the coarser the knit, the bigger and simpler the artwork must be.
  • The fabric stretches. A sweater body can stretch 20–40% across. A print layer that cannot stretch with it turns into a crack network after a month of wear. Any method that leaves a thick film on the surface is fighting the fabric's basic behaviour.
  • Texture is often the design. Ribs, cables and pointelle are dimensional. A print that flattens or fills them damages the garment's look even when the ink itself is fine — the same reason pressing habits matter, as covered in our notes on pressing and finishing.

The main methods, and how each behaves on knit

  • Water-based screen printing. The workhorse for knitwear decoration. The ink soaks into the yarn rather than sitting on top, so the print stretches with the fabric and keeps a soft hand. Best for solid simple artwork in a few colours, on smooth areas of fine-gauge knits. On dark fabrics it needs discharge or an underbase, which is worth a proper sample.
  • Plastisol screen printing. Cheaper, more opaque, sits as a film on the surface. On a stretchy knit it is the one most likely to crack — usable for small chest logos on stable areas, risky for large placement prints. If a quote is much cheaper than the alternatives, it is often plastisol; ask.
  • Heat-transfer vinyl (HTV). Cut vinyl pressed on. Fine for team-kit style numbers and small logos on stable panels, poor on anything that stretches a lot or gets washed hot — edges lift and peel. It also tends to look and feel like a sticker, which matters at premium retail.
  • Digital / DTG-style printing. Good for multi-colour artwork and small runs, but slow and finicky on textured knits; loop shadows show through the print. Usually better on smooth fine-gauge cotton.
  • Alternatives that aren't printing. For logos specifically, embroidery or a woven label is often more durable than any print on a heavy knit, and jacquard or intarsia can knit the graphic into the fabric entirely — see our notes on knitted patterns for when that is the better route.

Placement: where the artwork meets the fabric

Placement is half the engineering. The rules we give buyers:

  • Print on the smoothest, most stable zone — usually the upper chest below the collar, on the plain jersey or flat panel section. Keep artwork off stripe junctions, ribbed welts, cables and pointelle.
  • Avoid stretch-critical zones. Anything across the armhole, shoulder seam or side seam gets pulled every time the garment goes on and off, and along the hem where the rib flexes.
  • Mark placement from a measurement, not a photo. "X cm down from the HPS, centred" survives production; "like the reference image" does not. The tech pack should carry the print placement, print size and the Pantone or ink standard.
  • Check scale against the garment, not the screen. A print that reads well on an A4 proof can overwhelm a fine-gauge piece or disappear on a chunky one. Ask for a photo of the printed sample on the actual garment.

Testing before you commit the bulk

Prints fail in the wash and in the fitting room, so both need testing on the printed sample:

  • Stretch test. Pull the printed area firmly in both directions. Fine hairline cracking that appears immediately will widen over months of wear. Water-based inks show some fine checking but should not flake.
  • Wash testing. Multiple home launderings, then re-inspect for cracking, peeling, colour loss and any effect on the knit itself. Our notes on colourfastness testing apply to printed areas too — the wash programme should reflect your care label instructions.
  • Lab testing where retail demands it. Formal print-durability and crocking tests slot into the same process as lab testing and restricted substances testing — printed inks are an RSL item, so phthalates and formaldehyde in the ink system matter for EU and US programmes.
  • Second-unit check. Print consistency across units is a real defect class — placement drifting a centimetre between size S and XL is common because placement is often scaled poorly across the size grading. Specify how placement scales with size, or fix it by measurement per size.

Where buyers go wrong

Three patterns account for nearly every print problem we are asked to rescue. Taking artwork designed for a T-shirt and applying it unchanged to a 5-gauge chunky knit, then wondering where the detail went. Choosing a method on unit price without asking whether it stretches. And approving a print on a sample photograph instead of wash-testing the physical garment. All three are cheap to prevent and expensive to discover at goods-in.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best print method for knitwear?

For most applications, water-based screen printing on a smooth, stable area of the knit. The ink penetrates the yarn, stretches with the fabric and keeps a soft hand. For small logos on stable panels, embroidery or a woven label is often even more durable. Heavy plastisol films and cheap heat-transfer vinyl are the methods most likely to crack or peel on stretchy knits.

Can you print on chunky cable knits?

Not usefully. Coarse gauges, cables and heavy texture swallow fine detail — the loops are larger than the lines of the artwork. On textured knits, the graphic is usually better knitted in (jacquard, intarsia) or applied as embroidery or a patch on a smooth area. As a rule of thumb, the coarser the knit, the simpler and larger any applied print must be.

Why did my print crack after washing?

Because the print layer does not stretch as much as the knit beneath it — typical of thick plastisol or transfer films — or because the ink system was under-cured, so adhesion failed in the wash. Both are preventable: match the method to the stretch, and wash-test a printed sample several times before approving bulk.

Should print placement scale across sizes?

Yes, and specify how. If the print size and position are fixed for all sizes, the graphic sits differently relative to the neckline and chest on each. Give placement as a measurement from the HPS per size, and state whether the artwork scales. This is one of the most common print defects we see at goods-in.
