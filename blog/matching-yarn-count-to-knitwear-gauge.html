Matching Yarn Count to Gauge: Why a 2/28 Nm Will Not Run on a 3GG Machine

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Yarn count (how fine the yarn is) and machine gauge (how many needles per inch) have to be chosen together. A fine 2/28 Nm wool runs comfortably on a 12GG machine and produces a flimsy, open fabric on a 3GG one; a chunky roving that suits 3GG will not pass through a 16GG needle. State both in the tech pack, and have the factory confirm the pairing on a knitted swatch before you approve the sample — most "wrong fabric" complaints we see trace back to a count and gauge that were never matched.

We see this a lot with buyers sending a photo of a chunky fisherman sweater alongside a yarn spec copied from a fine-gauge supplier. Both documents are internally sensible, and together they are impossible. Count and gauge are not two independent dials you can set in any combination; they are the two halves of a single decision about how big a loop the machine makes. Here is how the pairing works, and what to write down so the first sample comes back right.

Two numbers that describe different things

A yarn count tells you how much length you get from a given weight. In the Nm system used for most wool and acrylic knitting yarn, a higher number is finer: 28 Nm means 28 metres per gram, while 15 Nm is nearly twice as thick. Gauge, written GG, is needles per inch on the machine bed — a 12GG machine packs 12 needles into each inch, a 3GG machine only 3. Both scales read "bigger is finer", which is exactly why buyers assume they can be mixed freely. They cannot. The needle spacing sets the size of the loop, and the yarn has to fill that loop without jamming it. Our gauge explainer covers what the number means for weight and season, and the count systems guide covers Nm, Tex and Denier if you are reading a spinner's sheet.

The pairing is a band, not a formula

There is no universal table, because fibre, ply and the number of ends run together all shift it. But the working ranges are stable enough to brief against:

  • 3GG to 5GG, chunky. Heavy singles or several fine ends plied together at the feeder. Think roving-like yarns, or two to four ends of a medium count. The fabric is lofty and the garment is warm.
  • 7GG, classic mid-weight. Often a 2/28 Nm type yarn run with two ends, or a single heavier count. This is the autumn workhorse across most of our Dongguan programs.
  • 12GG, fine-mid. Broadly 2/28 to 2/48 Nm, single end or lightly plied. Smooth face, the standard merino and lambswool territory.
  • 14GG to 16GG and finer. 2/48 to 2/60 Nm and finer, usually single end. Lightweight, dense, close to a woven shirt in drape.

Treat those as conversation starters, not specification values. A spinner's 2/30 in a lofty wool behaves differently from the same count in a compact cotton, which is why the factory will always want to knit a square before committing. Our notes on hand feel and fibre fineness explain part of that difference.

What a mismatch actually looks like

Yarn too fine for the gauge: the loops cannot fill the space between needles, so the fabric comes out open, sleazy and see-through, with poor cover and a low grams-per-square-metre figure. It drops at the shoulders, bags at the elbows, and no amount of finishing gives it body. We have had buyers reject a whole sample round for "cheap fabric" when the real problem was one line in the spec.

Yarn too thick for the gauge: worse, because it damages things. Needles deflect or break, stitches drop, the face shows barre and needle lines, and the fabric ends up board-stiff with no drape. On fine gauges you can also exceed what the yarn feeders and tensioners will handle. If a factory says a yarn will not run on the requested gauge, believe them — our fabric defects guide shows what those faults look like once they are in the panel.

Ply and ends matter as much as the count

"2/28 Nm" describes two 28 Nm singles twisted together, which behaves roughly like a single 14 Nm yarn. Running three ends of 2/28 at the feeder is a different fabric again from running one end of a heavier 2/15. Same fibre, similar delivered weight, completely different surface and hand. This is why we ask buyers to specify ply, ends per feeder and target fabric weight, not just a count. The ply and twist guide goes deeper, and worsted versus woollen explains why two yarns of identical count can still knit very differently.

What to write into the tech pack

  • Count with its system and ply — "2/28 Nm, 100% merino" beats "fine merino yarn". State the ends per feeder too.
  • Gauge — the GG value, plus a target fabric weight in GSM so the pairing has a measurable outcome. Our fabric weight guide explains how buyers use that number.
  • A tension or stitch reference — a swatch, a photo of the stitch, or a previous style number. Ambiguity here is what produces two different fabrics from one spec.
  • Swatch approval before garment sampling. A 20 cm knitted square settles count, gauge and hand in a day. It is the cheapest insurance in the whole process, and it fits naturally into the tech pack.
  • Consumption and cost assumptions. A finer yarn at a finer gauge uses less weight but more machine time; yarn consumption and price factors spell out why.

A worked example

A buyer wants a 7GG chunky crew in wool-acrylic, referencing a photo of a heavy retail sweater. We would normally propose a 2/28 Nm type run with two ends, knit a square, check the GSM and cover, and only then cut the first sample — because the photo may have been 5GG with four ends, and copying the look by changing only the gauge gives a fabric that is neither heavy nor stable. If the buyer then decides the hand should be smoother, that is a gauge change and a re-development, not a tweak. Approving the square first is how you find that out at the cost of a day rather than a sample round. Review it against a written checklist so the comment comes back once, not in three emails.

Frequently asked questions

Can I change the gauge and keep the same yarn?

Usually not. Gauge changes the loop size, so the same yarn will either leave gaps or jam the needles. A gauge change is normally a re-development: new count or ends, new tension, new pattern program and a new swatch. Our guide to changing sweater gauge walks through why it costs a sample round rather than an afternoon.

Is a higher yarn count always a finer yarn?

Within one system, yes — 48 Nm is finer than 28 Nm. Across systems, no: Nm, Ne, Tex and Denier run on different logic and some run in the opposite direction. Always name the system alongside the number, and ask the spinner for the equivalent if you are comparing quotes.

Who decides the pairing, the buyer or the factory?

The buyer owns the target — gauge, hand, weight, season. The factory proposes the count, ply and ends that will deliver it on their machines. Get the proposal in writing, approve it on a knitted square, and keep those values on the tech pack so bulk cannot quietly drift.
