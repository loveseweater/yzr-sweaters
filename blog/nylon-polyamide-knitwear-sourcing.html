Nylon and Polyamide in Knitwear: What the Fibre Actually Does

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Nylon (polyamide) is the durability fibre of knitwear. Blended at 10–30% with wool, cotton or other fibres, it multiplies abrasion resistance, tensile strength and shape retention — which is why heritage sock knitting and modern merino outdoor knitwear both rely on it. It costs less than most natural fibres, dyes with the same acid dyes as wool, and its only real penalties are a slightly smoother hand-feel and heat sensitivity in finishing and care. A well-specified nylon blend is a quality upgrade, not a downgrade.

Every few weeks a buyer asks us the same question with slightly defensive phrasing: "There's nylon in this yarn — is that them cheapening the sweater?" The answer surprises people who learned to read fibre labels in the era of shiny 100% nylon shirts. Nylon is the reason your grandfather's army socks survived, the reason premium hiking brands blend it into merino at 20% or so, and the reason a school-uniform sweater survives a second child. We use it deliberately, at specific percentages, for specific reasons — and the sourcing conversation deserves more nuance than "synthetic bad, natural good".

What nylon brings to a blend

Polyamide is a synthetic fibre family (nylon 6 and nylon 6,6 are the ones you will meet in knitwear). Its properties complement wool and cotton almost perfectly where durability is the goal:

  • Abrasion resistance. Nylon's standout property. In a wool-nylon blend, the nylon carries the friction of wear while the wool carries comfort and insulation. School knitwear, workwear sweaters and hiking layers are blend programmes for exactly this reason.
  • Tensile strength. Blend yarns break less in knitting (fewer fabric faults from yarn breaks) and seams and shoulders hold their shape through seasons of wear.
  • Shape retention and recovery. Nylon resists stretching out, so cuffs, ribbing and body shape recover better after wear — the practical partner to what we cover in stretch and shape retention testing.
  • Pilling resistance. Pills form when loose fibre ends work loose and tangle. Nylon's strength holds those ends in the yarn structure, so a wool-nylon blend pills noticeably less than the same wool knit pure. It does not eliminate pilling — yarn twist and knit structure still matter — but it shifts the curve, which matters for programmes where pilling complaints are expensive.
  • Weight efficiency. Nylon is light and strong, letting a fine, light garment achieve durability that would otherwise demand a heavier structure.

What it costs you: hand-feel, heat and perception

The trade-offs are real, and honest sourcing means naming them:

  • Hand-feel shifts cooler and smoother. At 10–20% the change is barely perceptible in a woollen-spun knit. Push nylon content toward 40% and higher and the fabric reads as sleeker, denser and slightly less "wooly" — exactly the aesthetic some modern merino programmes want, and wrong for a heritage-style lofty lambswool.
  • Heat sensitivity. Nylon softens at temperatures well below cotton's tolerance band. In final pressing a too-hot iron or calendar can glaze or shine a nylon-rich surface, and in consumer care a hot wash or tumble dry can set distortion. Care labels must reflect the blend, and finishing temperatures must be set by the fibre with the lowest tolerance in the mix.
  • Perception management. Some markets and buyers discount any synthetic content on principle. The counter-position is the outdoor and technical sector, where merino/nylon blends command premium prices and performance positioning. The mistake is positioning a nylon blend as "pure natural luxury" — position it as durability engineering and the story is honest and sellable.

Dyeing and colour behaviour

This is where nylon is unusually easy to live with in wool programmes: polyamide takes acid dyes, the same dye class used for wool. A wool-nylon blend can be dyed to a solid union shade in a single bath, which keeps colour consistency management no harder than pure wool — with one caveat: the two fibres exhaust dyes at different rates, and at nylon contents above roughly 30% a shade can drift slightly between fibre phases if the mill's process control is sloppy. Ask the mill for union-shade history on the exact blend you are buying, and approve shade through the normal lab dip process rather than assuming.

Cotton-nylon is the harder dyeing case — cotton wants reactive dyes, nylon wants acid, so solid shades need a two-stage process and skilled mills. For cotton programmes where you want nylon's recovery benefits, consider yarn-dyed melange approaches or keep nylon at sock-grade low percentages where a slight tone difference between fibres reads as texture rather than defect.

Specifying a nylon blend that behaves

  • Match content to job. The working sweet spots from our own programmes: 80/20 wool/nylon for classic durable sweaters; 70/30 for heavy-duty and schoolwear; 90/10 or 95/5 cotton/nylon where you want recovery without changing the cotton character; and the outdoor register of ~75/25 merino/nylon for technical lightweights.
  • Declare honestly and test the declaration. Blend claims carry lab-verification expectations — see our guide to fibre composition tolerances. "Wool blend" marketing copy that hides nylon content is a returns-and-compliance risk in markets with strict labelling.
  • Set care labelling to the nylon. Wash temperatures and drying instructions should follow the most delicate fibre rule of the blend, not the wool alone, once nylon is present above nominal content. This feeds directly into care label compliance.
  • Consider recycled polyamide. Recycled nylon from pre- and post-consumer waste is commercially available and increasingly requested in sustainability-driven programmes. It performs on par with virgin fibre and carries a defensible story — at a modest cost premium.
  • Price it as an upgrade path. Nylon is typically cheaper than wool and cotton per kilo, so a blend usually lands between its components. The honest sales story is not "cheaper" but "lasts twice as long in wear" — the durability delta is what buyers actually receive.

Frequently asked questions

Is nylon in a sweater a sign of poor quality?

No — that reading is decades out of date. Nylon at 10–30% is a deliberate durability specification used by premium outdoor brands and heritage sock knitters precisely because it extends wearable life. Quality depends on the yarn as a whole: fibre grades, twist and spinning quality matter far more than the presence of polyamide. The genuine quality signal to check is honest labelling — a factory declaring its blend accurately has nothing to hide.

What nylon percentage should I choose for a hard-wearing school or work sweater?

For schoolwear and heavy-rotation workwear, 70/30 wool/nylon is the classic working formula — enough nylon for abrasion life and wash survival, enough wool for comfort and insulation. If the programme is cotton-based, 90/10 to 95/5 cotton/nylon improves recovery and strength without visibly changing the cotton hand-feel. Above roughly 30% nylon in a wool blend, hand-feel moves toward sleek and technical, which suits performance positioning more than comfort positioning.

Does a wool-nylon blend dye the same as pure wool?

Largely yes — both fibres take acid dyes, so solid shades are achievable in one bath, which keeps colour approval simpler than it sounds. The watch item is differential exhaustion: wool and nylon take up dye at different rates, so shade control at higher nylon contents depends on the mill's process discipline. Approve shade through lab dips on the exact blend, and ask for the mill's union-shade track record if the blend is new to your programme.
