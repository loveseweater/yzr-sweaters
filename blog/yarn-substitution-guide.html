When the Yarn Isn't Available: How Substitution Works in Knitwear

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

When a yarn is discontinued, delayed or priced out, knitwear production often has to switch to a substitute — but substitution is not a case of finding a yarn that looks similar. It changes weight, hand feel, colour, pilling and shrinkage behaviour, and usually forces colour samples and performance tests to be redone. Substitution should only proceed with the buyer's written agreement, and both sides should agree in advance who pays for the rework it causes.

Yarn problems are one of the most common disruptions in knitwear production, and substitution — replacing one yarn with another — is how factories keep an order moving. Handled quietly, it turns into a quality claim at delivery. Handled as a documented decision, it is a manageable event with predictable costs.

Why does yarn substitution become necessary?

Substitution is rarely a first choice; it is a response to a supply problem. The usual triggers are:

  • The yarn or colour is discontinued — the mill stops the article, or one shade in the colour card is dropped.
  • The mill's lead time no longer fits — the yarn arrives after the knitting window, putting delivery at risk.
  • Price moves beyond budget — a fibre price rise or minimum-order change makes the agreed yarn uneconomic.
  • Certification or traceability needs change — the buyer requires a different certified or traceable source.
  • The end customer's policy changes — a new recycled-content or restricted-substance rule rules the original yarn out.

What are the three levels of yarn substitution?

Substitution is not one action; it comes in three grades, each with a different risk profile.

  • Same yarn, new dye lot. A dye lot is a batch of yarn dyed together in one bath, and colour can shift slightly between lots. This is the lightest substitution: the yarn is identical, so only the colour tolerance needs confirming.
  • Drop-in substitution. A different supplier's yarn in the same composition and count, intended to go straight into the existing program. Weight and construction hold, but hand feel, colour consistency and processing stability must be verified.
  • Functional substitution. A change of fibre or yarn count, where the new yarn performs differently. Every performance assumption — insulation, pilling, shrinkage, feel — is invalidated and must be re-evaluated before bulk knitting.

What else does a substitution change?

This is the part buyers most often underestimate: substitution is never local. It cascades through the specification.

  • Weight and hand feel. Yarn count and bulk change stitch density, so piece weight and the feel of the garment move with it.
  • Colour. A new yarn takes dye differently, so the original shade may not be reproducible; a fresh lab dip — a small dyed sample sent for approval — is normally required.
  • Pilling and shrinkage. Fibre length, twist and spinning route change how the knit behaves in wear and wash, so earlier test conclusions no longer apply; see our shrinkage control guide.
  • Cost and quotation. A different yarn price, minimum quantity and extra development work all move the unit price, which may need re-quoting.
  • Lead time. Sampling, colour approval and testing restart, so the delivery date is recalculated.
  • Labelling. If the fibre composition changes, the care label and the buyer's product data must change with it — a compliance point.

What order should the substitution decision follow?

Work through the questions in order before committing. Is the original genuinely unavailable, or just slower and dearer than preferred? Is a substitute technically viable on the agreed construction? What will it cost in money, testing and days? Only when those answers clear should substitution be executed — otherwise the order should not change at all.

How should a substitution be presented to the buyer?

Buyers accept substitutions they understand and reject ones they discover late. A workable proposal states the reason, the substitute's composition and count, and exactly what will differ: weight, feel, colour route, test plan and delivery impact, with two options and their cost and timing difference where possible. Then wait for written approval before any yarn is bought or knitted. Silence is not consent.

What belongs in the contract about substitution?

Keep it specific rather than repeating general terms. Four clauses carry most of the value:

  • Written buyer approval before execution — no equivalent yarn used on the factory's own judgement.
  • Cost responsibility for rework — re-sampling, re-dyeing and re-testing after an approved substitution.
  • Colour tolerance — the acceptance window for the new lot or yarn, agreed as a lab dip or reference.
  • Delivery extension — how many days the schedule moves when substitution triggers new sampling and testing.

When should a substitution be refused?

Not every availability problem justifies a change. Refuse, or escalate instead, when the performance cannot move — a certified organic or recycled claim a substitute cannot support, a colour the brand sells on and cannot restage, or a technical knit that depends on the original yarn's bulk. Refuse too when the cost of substitution, re-testing included, approaches the cost of cancelling or shipping late: the honest answer then is to renegotiate, not to knit something different.

How can substitution risk be reduced?

Most substitution pain is bought at the start. Reserve yarn on the first order, agree dye-lot retention with the mill, and avoid building a program around an exclusive or discontinued article; our yarn stock and reorder planning guide covers reserve quantities. Where a substitute is likely, qualify it during development so an approved alternative exists before the primary yarn fails.

Frequently asked questions

Can a factory change the yarn without telling me?

Only if the contract allows it, and it should not. A substitution changes weight, feel, colour behaviour and sometimes fibre composition, so it affects what you can sell and what your label must say. Standard practice is to require written approval before any substitute is knitted, and to record the decision so both sides know what was agreed.

If the substitute looks the same, do tests really need repeating?

At minimum, colour approval is needed because a new yarn usually takes dye differently. For a change of fibre or yarn count, pilling, shrinkage and dimensional stability are re-tested because they depend on fibre length, twist and spinning route, not on appearance. A yarn that looks the same on the table can behave differently after five washes.

Who normally pays when substitution causes re-sampling or re-testing?

It depends on why the change happened. If the mill discontinued a yarn the factory had committed to supply, the cost is usually the factory's to absorb or negotiate. If the buyer's own requirements changed, or the buyer chose the substitute, the buyer normally carries the rework. Agreeing this before the substitution starts avoids the argument later.
