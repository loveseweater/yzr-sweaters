Knitting Tension and Loop Length: Why Samples Differ From Bulk

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Quick answer

Knitting tension is how tightly the machine pulls each loop, and loop length is the resulting size of that stitch. They are the factory's main control over a sweater's width, length, fabric weight and hand feel — and they are set per machine, per yarn and per style. When the same style is knitted on a different machine, in a different yarn lot, or by a different operator, the tension can drift, which is why bulk can measure differently from the sample both sides approved.

When a buyer finds that the bulk shipment measures slightly off the approved sample — a centimetre narrow here, a touch heavier there — the instinct is to look for a defect. Usually there is none. The knitting tension moved, and tension is the least visible variable in knitwear production and one of the most consequential.

What are tension and loop length?

On a flat-bed knitting machine, yarn is fed to the needles at a controlled rate and formed into loops. Loop length is the amount of yarn consumed in a single stitch, and tension is the machine setting that determines it. A longer loop, produced at looser tension, gives a bigger, more open stitch; a shorter loop gives a smaller, denser one.

The two terms get used loosely and are sometimes treated as the same thing, but the distinction is useful. Tension is the setting the technician controls on the machine; loop length is the measurable physical result. Two machines can be set to the same nominal tension and still produce different loop lengths if the yarn or the machine condition differs, which is why tension settings do not transfer neatly between machines.

The result is expressed as stitch density — the number of courses (rows) and wales (columns) per unit of measurement. Stitch density connects the machine setting to the finished fabric, and it is what a knitting technician works towards. Our notes on knit gauge explain how gauge relates to this.

What does tension control in the finished garment?

Four properties move together when tension changes, and they move in the same direction — which is why a small tension error produces several visible symptoms at once.

  • Width and length. Looser tension produces a wider, longer panel from the same number of stitches and rows, because each stitch occupies more space. A knitter aiming for a target measurement adjusts tension and stitch count together, and a drift shows up as a garment that measures off in both directions.
  • Fabric weight. Looser tension uses more yarn per stitch, so a looser-knit sweater of the same dimensions weighs more. This matters commercially because yarn is priced by weight — a tension drift is a cost drift, and our notes on yarn consumption explain how the figure is estimated.
  • Hand feel. Tighter tension gives a firmer, denser fabric; looser tension gives a softer, more fluid one. Two sweaters in identical yarn can feel distinctly different purely because of tension, a point our notes on hand feel and micron make from the yarn side.
  • Opacity and cover factor. Looser knitting leaves more open space between stitches, so a light-coloured knit can become more revealing than intended — a common surprise on fine-gauge viscose and cotton blends.

Tension also interacts with the structure being knitted. Ribbing behaves differently from plain jersey; a cable or jacquard structure changes how the yarn feeds and how the fabric relaxes. Our notes on knit patterns cover the structural side.

Why do samples and bulk differ?

This is the question tension actually explains, and there are four recurring causes.

  • A different machine. Sample rooms and bulk floors are often different areas with different machines. A style sampled on one and bulk-knitted on another can shift unless the technician matches loop length deliberately rather than copying the tension number.
  • A different yarn lot. Yarn from a new dye lot or a new spin can behave differently — slightly different friction, slightly different bulk — and a setting that suited the sample yarn can drift on the bulk yarn. Our notes on yarn substitution explain why a yarn change is a specification decision.
  • Relaxation. Knitted fabric changes dimension after it comes off the machine, as the loops settle and internal tension releases. The window between knitting and finishing matters, and so does the finishing itself — our notes on garment dyeing and finishing cover the stages where the fabric is relaxed and set.
  • Operator and shift variation. Tension is set by a person. On a long run across multiple machines and shifts, small differences accumulate unless the measurement is checked during production rather than only at the end.

This is the mechanism behind a large share of "the bulk is slightly off" complaints, and it is why the production control point is a measurable one. Checking a knitted panel against the agreed stitch density during production catches a drift while it is still cheap to correct; the same problem found at final inspection is a shipment decision.

How does tension relate to shrinkage?

Directly. Fabric knitted at loose tension has more room to move when it is washed and relaxed, so it generally shrinks more than tightly knitted fabric in the same yarn. That single fact explains why two suppliers can both pre-shrink their knitwear and still deliver different shrinkage performance — the starting tension differs, and the finishing process is compensating for different amounts of movement.

It also explains why shrinkage and tension should be specified together. Asking for a maximum shrinkage figure without any reference to fabric density leaves the factory room to meet the number in more than one way, and those ways have different consequences for hand feel and weight. Our notes on controlling shrinkage cover the mechanisms.

What should a buyer do about it?

  • Approve a measured sample, not just a visual one. The specification that carries forward is the measurement and fabric weight, recorded on the approved sample. Our notes on measuring a sweater explain how the figures are taken so both sides read the same numbers.
  • Record the gauge and stitch density alongside the measurements. Gauge alone does not pin down the fabric; density per centimetre is the figure that can be checked on a knitted panel.
  • Ask how production tension will be matched to the sample. A supplier with a real answer will talk about matching loop length and checking panels in production, not about copying a machine setting.
  • Allow a stated tolerance rather than demanding an exact match. Knitted fabric has a realistic measuring tolerance, and requiring zero tolerance guarantees disputes. Our notes on quality standards documents cover how to write the bands in.
  • If bulk measures outside tolerance, check the weight as well as the dimensions. A garment that is wider and heavier than the sample points at tension; one that is wider and lighter points at yarn or structure.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the bulk garment measure slightly differently from the sample I approved?

Most often because the knitting tension moved between the sample and the bulk run. Samples and bulk are frequently knitted on different machines, in different yarn lots and by different operators, and each of those changes shifts the loop length unless it is actively matched. The finishing process then relaxes the fabric from a different starting point, so the finished dimensions differ.

Can a supplier simply match the sample exactly?

Within a tolerance, yes, and a competent factory will match loop length deliberately and check panels during production. But knitted fabric is elastic and moves during relaxation and finishing, so a realistic tolerance band is always involved. Demanding an exact match in every dimension is not achievable and creates disputes rather than better goods.

Does tighter knitting make a better sweater?

Not automatically. Tighter knitting gives a firmer, denser fabric with better shape retention and less shrinkage, which suits some styles. Looser knitting gives a softer, more fluid hand and more warmth for the weight, which suits others. What matters is that the tension suits the design and the target price rather than drifting by accident.
