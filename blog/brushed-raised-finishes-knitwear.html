Brushed and Raised Finishes on Knitwear: Specifying the Hand Feel

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Brushing (also called raising or napping) is a mechanical finishing step where tiny wire rollers tease fibre ends out of the knit surface to build a soft nap. It only works on spun yarns with enough loose fibre — filament and tightly twisted yarns will not brush. Every extra pass buys softness and pays for it in pilling, so the finish is a dial, not a switch: specify nap intensity, weight before and after, wash-test behaviour and pilling grade in the tech pack, or you will get the mill's default and find out at retail.

Brushed knitwear sells a feeling. A customer picks up a brushed cardigan, squeezes it, and decides in two seconds. But on the buying side the finish is oddly invisible — we regularly receive tech packs with pages of stitch detail and a single line reading "soft hand," as if the nap were a mood rather than a manufacturing process. We see this a lot, and it costs real money: brushed orders approved on photo samples arrive with the nap everyone imagined, then pill in week two. Here is how the finish actually works and how to specify it so that doesn't happen.

What brushing physically does

The knit comes off the machine with its surface basically flat — whatever loft the yarn has is inside the fabric, not on it. A brushing machine runs the panel or garment over rollers covered in fine wire teeth that catch fibre ends and pull them upright, a few millimetres at a time, usually over several light passes rather than one heavy one. The result is a layer of raised fibre — the nap — that traps air and reads as softness to the hand. A second step, often light steaming or a tumble with moisture, settles the nap and fixes it. It is the same family of process that turns plain woven cotton into flannel, and like flannel, the input yarn matters more than the machine settings.

This is why brushing sits at the end of the dyeing and finishing route, after knitting and washing but typically before final pressing: the sequence exists so the nap is built on the garment's final dimensions, not squeezed flat again by later processes.

Which yarns brush well — and which refuse to

Brushing needs loose staple fibre on the surface, which rules out a lot of the yarn cupboard. The honest ranking, from our floor:

  • Spun acrylic is the brusher's favourite. Plenty of accessible fibre ends, uniform staple, and the nap stands up and stays up. It is how you get the "cashmere feel at acrylic money" cardigan that dominates autumn retail. See our acrylic sourcing guide for the fibre grades behind it.
  • Wool and wool blends brush beautifully — lambswool and the shorter-shorn grades give a dense, low nap that reads premium. The tradeoff is cost and a nap that flattens with wear, recovering with steam.
  • Cotton brushes modestly. The nap is shorter and dustier, more flannel-adjacent; it works for casual programmes but never reaches acrylic loft.
  • Filament yarns will not brush at all. Continuous smooth polyester or nylon has no ends to raise. If a supplier promises a "brushed" 100% filament polyester knit, what you will actually receive is a chemical softener or a brushed-look slub — a different product with different durability. Start with spun polyester if the price point is synthetic.
  • Angora and mohair look brushed without touching the machine — the halo is the fibre itself. Worth knowing before you pay for a finish the yarn already provides; our angora guide covers the animal-welfare paperwork that route requires.

The tradeoff nobody escapes: softness vs pilling

Every fibre end you raise becomes available to tangle. That is the physics behind the brushed-knit paradox: the softest cardigan on the rail is statistically the most likely to pill by the third wash. This is not a quality failure — it is the finish working exactly as designed. What separates good brushed programmes from bad ones is how the tradeoff is managed:

Anti-pill yarn grades. Acrylic in particular is available in engineered anti-pill variants whose fibre ends are modified to break off instead of anchoring pills. They brush slightly less loftily and cost a little more; for anything that will actually be worn rather than photographed, they are usually the right call — the reasoning is laid out in our pilling prevention guide.

Pass count control. One brushing pass gives a light nap that pills slowly; four passes give cloud-softness that pills immediately unless the yarn is anti-pill. A competent mill finishes to a target, not to "maximum soft" — and can dial back if your wash test fails.

Nap length discipline. Short dense nap survives washing; long fluffy nap impresses in the showroom and mats in the machine. For machine-washable brushed programmes, specify short nap explicitly.

How to actually specify it in the tech pack

"Soft hand" is not a specification; it is a wish. Four lines turn it into one. First, a physical reference — a bought garment whose nap you want matched, sent alongside the tech pack, because nap is nearly impossible to communicate in words. Second, weight before and after finishing: brushing adds grams of standing fibre and removes some through fibre loss in the process, and a garment-weight target post-finish is the only number that catches an over- or under-brushed bulk. Third, which surfaces are brushed — both sides, face only, or panel-specific (brushed body, unbrushed rib trims is a common premium combination, and mixed trims need the trim card to say so). Fourth, the wash and pilling requirement: brushed knits must be wash-tested after finishing, because a nap that looks identical on the dry rail can behave completely differently after one wash cycle. Our sample wash test procedure applies exactly as written, just with higher stakes.

If your programme also involves prickle or micron concerns on wool, note that brushing partially masks surface roughness — a finish cannot rescue a genuinely coarse fibre, but it does change what your hand-feel panel perceives, so test the finish on the actual bulk yarn, not a finer development yarn.

QC on brushed production: what to check at inspection

  • Nap uniformity under raking light. The single most telling check: lay panels flat and view at a low angle. Brushing irregularity shows as stripes or patches that are invisible straight-on. Build it into your inline checks as well, not only at final pre-shipment inspection.
  • Weight per garment against the post-finish target. Under-weight means under-brushed; over-weight plus a loose nap can mean over-brushed fibre that will shed and pill.
  • The tape test on trim edges. Ribbed cuffs and collars brush unevenly at their edges — either specified unbrushed or checked for balding.
  • One washed sample per dye lot. Non-negotiable for brushed orders: the nap can compact, curl or pill differently by lot, and the dry rail will not tell you.
  • Colour check after brushing. A raised nap changes how light scatters off the surface — brushed panels read lighter and dustier than the same colour unbrushed. Compare against a brushed reference, not the lab dip alone, or the whole order shades off-tone.

What the finish costs you

Brushing is inexpensive per piece and moderate in time — expect the finishing route to add a few days to the order, more if dye lots must be finished sequentially on shared machines. The real costs hide downstream: extra wash-test samples, a slightly higher seconds rate (brushing exposes fabric faults that flat panels hide — see our fabric defects guide for what surfaces), and packaging, since nap compresses in vacuum-tight polybags and needs recovery time or looser packing, a factor in shipping preparation. Budget for those, and the finish stays a margin-maker rather than a returns generator.

Frequently asked questions

Can any knitwear be brushed?

No, and this is the most common mistaken assumption in briefs we receive. Brushing needs loose staple fibre ends, so spun acrylic, wool, cotton and spun polyester brush well, while filament yarns — the smooth continuous polyester or nylon used for trims and fine gauge styles — cannot be brushed at all. Very tightly twisted or high-twist yarns also resist brushing and give only a minimal nap. Choose a brushable yarn first, then specify the finish.

Does brushing make knitwear pill more?

It raises the pilling risk, yes — every fibre end lifted out of the surface is available to tangle into a pill. The controllable part is the yarn and pass count: anti-pill acrylic grades, short dense nap and fewer brushing passes give most of the softness with much less pilling. Wash-testing one sample per dye lot after finishing is the reliable way to catch a problem before it ships.

How much does brushed finishing add to price and lead time?

The finishing itself is a small per-piece cost and adds only a few days, since panels pass through brushing machines quickly. The real schedule items are the development loop — reference matching, nap sampling and wash testing before bulk approval — and sequencing dye lots through shared finishing equipment. Budget one extra development round and a few days of finishing time, not weeks.
