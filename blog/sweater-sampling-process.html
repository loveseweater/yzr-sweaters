The Sweater Sampling Process: From Idea to Approved Sample

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Sampling is where a knitwear order is won or lost. It is the stage where your idea meets real yarn, real machines and a real price — and where most delays are created long before bulk production starts. Knowing what happens between sending a reference and receiving an approved sample helps you plan the calendar honestly and avoid paying for the same lesson twice.

What the factory needs from you

A complete tech pack shortens sample development more than anything else. At minimum it should carry a flat sketch or reference photo, a measurement spec by size, the yarn or blend you want, the target gauge, construction details, trims, colour references and care instructions.

  • If you have a tech pack — send it as one file, with the measurement chart in a spreadsheet rather than embedded in a PDF image.
  • If you only have a reference garment — send the physical piece if you can. A sweater in hand tells the factory more about gauge, weight and hand feel than twenty photos.
  • If you only have an idea — send a photo, tell us the market and target retail price, and be clear about what matters most: softness, warmth, structure or cost.
  • Always state your target quantity and delivery window — it changes which yarns and machines make sense.

How a knitwear sample is actually built

  • Yarn selection — matching your colour to a stock yarn, or dyeing a lab dip. Custom-dyed yarns are more accurate but add lead time.
  • Swatch and panel knitting — structure, gauge and stitch are knitted as panels, then checked for width, weight and appearance.
  • Linking and seaming — shoulder, side seams, neckline and armholes, where fit problems usually originate.
  • Trims — collars, plackets, buttons, zips, main labels and care labels.
  • Finishing — washing or softening, mending, pressing and shaping. This is where hand feel is decided.
  • Measurement and QC — measured flat against your spec, then photographed for your review.

The sampling rounds, and what each one is for

Knitwear normally needs two to three rounds. Skipping one rarely saves time, because the issue usually reappears in bulk.

  • Development sample (first sample) — proves silhouette, gauge and general look. Expect hand feel and small details to still be approximate.
  • Revision sample — your corrections applied: measurements, colour, collar shape, stitch density.
  • Pre-production (PP) sample — made in bulk yarn, on the bulk machine, at bulk settings. This is the piece you seal and sign off, and the standard your final inspection is measured against.
  • Salesman or photo sample — a clean, presentation-ready piece for your line sheet or e-commerce shoot.

Sampling cost, and whether it is credited back

Sample fees cover materials and development time, so a hand-knitted or heavily engineered piece costs more than a simple stock-yarn crew neck. Custom knitwear suppliers commonly credit sampling fees against the bulk order once it is placed — but confirm three things before sampling begins:

  • The sample price, itemised by style, and what is included.
  • Whether the fee is refundable or credited against bulk, and at what order value or MOQ.
  • Whether revision rounds are charged again, and how many are included.

How long it takes

A stock-yarn style with a clear tech pack moves quickly; custom-dyed yarn, intarsia or new trim sourcing takes longer. Rather than asking for a single number, ask for a schedule with dates for yarn arrival, sample completion and courier dispatch — then work backwards from your in-store date and add a buffer for holidays and shipping.

Giving feedback that gets samples right

Most "bad samples" are really unclear briefs. These habits cut rounds:

  • Measure it flat and send numbers. "Chest is 2 cm narrow" beats "fits small".
  • Photograph in daylight, both flat and on a person, so the factory sees drape as well as shape.
  • Consolidate all comments into one numbered list. Drip-feeding points over three emails is how contradictory instructions happen.
  • Rank the points as must-fix or preferred, so the team knows where to spend the effort.
  • For hand feel, compare. Send a reference garment or name the direction — softer, denser, drier, less hairy.

Common sampling problems and what they mean

  • Colour off-target — usually lighting or a different dye lot. Approve shades under daylight against a retained standard.
  • Measurements outside tolerance — often relaxation after washing. Ask whether the sample was measured after finishing, not straight off the machine.
  • Hand feel wrong — yarn count, twist and washing all move the hand. Ask which can be changed without changing the price.
  • Weight or thickness different — check the yarn count and stitch density, not only the gauge.
  • Trims not as specified — confirm button size, zip type and label wording in writing before the PP sample.
