Choosing a Knitwear Sourcing Country: China, Vietnam, Bangladesh, Turkey Compared

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Sweaters are not T-shirts. Knitwear depends on a flat-knitting machine park, technicians who can program shaping, and a local yarn market — and those three things are distributed very unevenly across sourcing countries. China and Turkey lead on capability and speed, Vietnam is strong and filling fast, Bangladesh is excellent at volume but thin on fancy knitwear, and India is fibre-specific. Choose on capability first, then on duty, then on FOB.

Every sourcing comparison we read ranks countries by labour cost, which is close to useless for knitwear. A sweater's cost structure is yarn plus machine time plus finishing, and its quality ceiling is set by the machines and the people who program them. A country can be cheap and still be the wrong answer if it cannot knit your gauge, cannot buy your yarn in small lots, or cannot hold your shade across three colourways. Here is how the main origins actually differ for a knitwear buyer.

Why knitwear does not follow the apparel playbook

Three characteristics make sweaters a different sourcing problem:

  • Shaping is done on the machine, not by cutting. Fully fashioned panels are knitted to shape, so the factory needs computerised flat machines and programmers who understand how a garment is built. A factory that is excellent at jersey T-shirts may have no sweater capability at all.
  • The yarn is the product. Wool, cashmere blends, fancy yarns and engineered fibres all have to be sourced, and the countries with deep yarn markets can offer small dye lots and fast colour development. The countries without them end up importing yarn anyway — often from China.
  • Finishing decides the hand feel. Washing, pressing and linking are skilled operations. The difference between a sweater that looks expensive and one that looks cheap is usually made here, not in the knitting.

China (Dongguan and the Pearl River Delta)

  • Capability: the widest in the world. A buyer can get 3GG chunky through to 16GG fine gauge, intarsia and jacquard, fully fashioned and seamless, hand-linked or cup-seamed, usually within one supplier network. Our overview of China's knitwear hubs covers the regional specialisations.
  • Yarn ecosystem: this is the underrated advantage. Worsted and woollen spinners, fancy yarn specialists and dyers are within a short distance, which is why small-lot custom colours and unusual yarns are genuinely available here. See yarn minimums and colour choice.
  • Speed and flexibility: development samples in days, flexible MOQ in a way that most competing origins will not match at 100 to 300 pieces.
  • Constraints: US Section 301 tariffs on Chinese-origin goods, UFLPA traceability obligations where cotton is involved, and rising labour cost. Peak-season capacity (roughly June to October for winter goods) genuinely tightens, which is why booking matters.
  • The failure mode you inherit: the cheap end of the market. There are excellent factories and there are trading operations with no production control, and the difference is not visible in a quotation. Vet the factory, not the country.

Vietnam

  • Capability: grown quickly and now solid for mainstream gauges and standard constructions. Complex intarsia, unusual gauges and hand finishing are less available than in China.
  • Duty treatment: the main reason buyers move here. Vietnam's free trade agreements — including the EU–Vietnam FTA — give preferential access that China does not have, and it avoids US Section 301 tariffs.
  • Yarn supply: still largely imported. Fabric and yarn often come from China or Taiwan, which means the yarn choice is narrower and the lead time has a hidden dependency.
  • Constraints: MOQs tend to be higher, development is slower, and capacity is heavily booked by large brands. A small or mid-sized buyer may find themselves at the back of the queue.
  • The failure mode you inherit: waiting. Longer development cycles and less flexibility for changes after sampling.

Bangladesh

  • Capability: genuinely world-class at high-volume circular knitting — jersey, fleece, basics. Sweater-specific flat-knit capability exists but is thinner, and the fancy end is limited.
  • Duty treatment: duty-free access to the EU under Everything But Arms, which is a real advantage for EU-bound programmes.
  • Constraints: long transit times, port congestion risk, high MOQs, and a compliance and audit load that has improved a great deal but still absorbs management time.
  • The failure mode you inherit: a mismatch between what you asked for and what the factory's machine park can do. Before placing a sweater programme here, confirm the gauge range and the shaping capability in writing.

Turkey

  • Capability: strong, and genuinely good at mid-to-high-end knitwear. Turkey has a real knitwear industry rather than a cut-and-sew industry that also makes sweaters.
  • Speed to Europe: the standout advantage — a couple of weeks rather than a month or more on the water, which changes how much inventory you need to carry and how late you can commit.
  • Duty treatment: the EU–Turkey customs union removes duty on industrial goods, which is significant for EU buyers.
  • Constraints: cost is meaningfully above China, quotes are usually in EUR or USD rather than lira because of currency instability, and capacity is finite. Fine for a fast, premium, mid-volume programme; not the answer for a value line.
  • The failure mode you inherit: paying a premium and then finding the yarn choice narrower than you expected, because parts of the yarn supply are imported.

India

  • Capability: strongest where its fibre base is — cotton knitwear, hand-knitted and hand-embellished product, and cotton-rich blends. Wool and fancy yarn knitwear are available but not the country's centre of gravity.
  • Cost: competitive at the yarn end because the cotton is local.
  • Constraints: consistency across units, infrastructure variability and longer transit to both Europe and North America. Cotton traceability documentation needs the same care as anywhere else.
  • The failure mode you inherit: unit-to-unit variation. If you go here, invest more in inspection than you would in Dongguan.

Portugal, Italy and nearshoring

Worth a mention because more buyers are asking. Portugal is genuinely good at knitwear, works in small runs, and is close to European customers — at a price that is a multiple of Asian production. It makes sense for premium small runs, for replenishment of fast-moving styles, and for brands whose story depends on European manufacture. It does not make sense as a replacement for a volume programme. The same logic is covered in our thinking on supply continuity.

A decision framework that actually works

Work through these in order, and the answer usually narrows to one country:

  • What is the product? If it needs an unusual gauge, intarsia, hand-linked finishing or a custom fancy yarn, your realistic options shrink to China or Turkey. If it is a standard crew neck in stock yarn, most origins can do it.
  • What is the volume and MOQ? Under a few hundred pieces per style per colour, flexibility matters more than labour rate — and that points back to China. Our notes on low-MOQ knitwear explain what makes small runs viable.
  • Where does it sell? Duty and trade-agreement access can be worth more than a few cents of FOB. Calculate landed cost per unit, not FOB — the discipline described in reading a quotation and comparing price factors.
  • How late can you commit? If you need to hold a decision until close to the season, transit time is a commercial variable, not a logistics detail. Turkey and domestic production win here.
  • What compliance burden can you carry? Traceability documentation, audits and testing have a real cost. Confirm what your programme needs before you choose the origin — see certifications explained.

The case for dual sourcing

Most mature buyers we work with end up with a primary and a secondary origin rather than a single one. Typically that means a capable, flexible primary for development and complex styles, and a second source for volume repeats once the style is proven. It costs something in duplicated sampling, and it buys you resilience against tariff changes, capacity crunches and freight shocks. Frequently asked questions

Is Vietnam always cheaper than China for knitwear?

No. On labour-intensive basics Vietnam can be competitive, but for sweaters the yarn often has to be imported and the development cycle is longer, which erodes the advantage. Add higher MOQs and slower sampling and many mid-sized buyers find the total cost of getting a style to market is not lower at all — the duty saving is the real reason to go there.

Which country is best for low-MOQ knitwear?

China, and specifically the Dongguan cluster, because flexible minimums depend on a local yarn market that will sell small dye lots and on factories willing to run short machine time. Turkey is second for European buyers who can carry the higher unit cost. Bangladesh and Vietnam are generally structured around larger programmes.

Can Bangladesh make a proper fully-fashioned sweater?

Some units can, but sweater capability is not the country's core strength — high-volume circular knit is. If you are placing shaped, fully-fashioned knitwear there, confirm the gauge range, the machine park and the shaping capability in writing before you commit, and inspect more heavily than you would elsewhere.

Should I move production out of China because of tariffs?

Only after you have costed the whole move, not just the duty line. Compare landed cost including freight and transit time, add the development cost of re-sampling in a new factory, and weigh the capability gap. For standard high-volume styles the arithmetic often favours a move; for development-led or complex knitwear it usually does not.
