Inline Inspection in Knitwear: Catching Defects Before They Multiply

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Inline inspection means checking the garment while it is being made — first pieces off each knitting machine, seams after linking, the first wash results before the bulk is washed — instead of discovering everything at final inspection. In knitwear it matters more than in almost any other garment category, because a wrong tension or gauge setting does not damage one piece, it damages every piece that machine makes until someone notices. A pre-shipment inspection tells you the lot is bad; an inline check tells you while the machines can still be fixed.

A final inspection is an audit; inline inspection is process control. We have stood on both sides of it — running inline QC on our own production floors in Dongguan and hosting third-party inspectors for buyers — and the pattern never changes: orders with inline checks almost never fail final inspection, and orders that fail final inspection almost never had one. Here is how the practice actually works on a knitwear floor.

Why knitwear punishes late inspection specifically

Cut-and-sew garment faults are usually local — a crooked seam on one unit. Knitwear faults are systemic. The sweater is shaped on the machine, so a setting error propagates: a tension dial half a turn off gives every panel from that machine the wrong loop length, which changes the panel weight, which changes the garment measurement after washing. By the time 3,000 pieces are knitted, linked, washed and pressed, the fault is baked into all of them, and the remediation conversation is the one in our guide to failed inspections and rework. The whole logic of inline inspection is to catch the first five pieces, not the last three thousand. The underlying mechanics — why loop length drives everything — are covered in our piece on knitting tension and loop length.

The three checkpoints that earn their keep

You do not need inspectors at every table. Three checkpoints catch the overwhelming majority of knitwear problems:

  • First-off pieces, every machine, every colour change. The first panel off each machine gets weighed on a gram scale and measured flat against the spec. This is the single highest-value check in knitwear production — a machine knitting heavy at first-off is knitting heavy all shift. Weight drift between machines of the same style is also the earliest warning of shade and hand-feel inconsistency later.
  • Inline at 20–40% completion. Pull finished-linked garments from the line mid-production: check linking seams (missed stitches, looped-off edges), measurement against the size spec, and — critically — whether the measurement has drifted from the first-off pieces. Drift means a machine setting has moved or an operator is interpreting the spec differently, and there are still 60% of goods to save.
  • First wash and finishing results. Before the bulk goes into wash, one full garment from each knitting machine goes through the complete wet process, is dried, conditioned and measured. This predicts shrinkage and relaxation before it exists at scale. Skipping this check and going straight to bulk wash is how brands end up with an entire order half a size small.

A fourth check earns its keep on multi-colour orders: shade tracking between dye lots, comparing yarn cones against the approved lab dip as each lot arrives — the practical end of colour consistency management.

Who should run it

Three realistic options, and the honest trade-offs. Factory inline QC is standard at any competent mill, including ours — the question to ask a supplier is not "do you do inline QC" but "show me the records": a good one produces per-machine, per-day weight and measurement logs without flinching. Third-party inline inspection (often sold as DPI, during-production inspection) costs roughly USD 300–400 per man-day and buys independence — worth it on large first orders, new suppliers or styles with tricky specifications. Your own merchandiser or QA during a visit is free-ish and effective, but only covers the days they are physically there. Most experienced buyers use factory records plus third-party inline on the orders that genuinely scare them, then a pre-shipment inspection at the end under AQL sampling as the formal gate.

What good inline records look like

If a supplier's inline QC is real, it leaves a paper trail: first-off panel weights logged per machine, measurement readings against tolerance bands, defect counts by category and by operator, and corrective actions with names and times against them ("machine 14, tension adjusted 14:20, re-check passed 14:45"). Vague summaries — "all good" — are not records. Buyers who ask for inline reports at 20% and again at 60% completion get two things: early warning, and a very clear signal about how the factory actually runs its floor. That signal matters as much as the data, and it is part of the broader quality standards conversation you should have before production starts.

Writing it into the order

Inline inspection only happens reliably when it is in the purchase order: what gets checked (first-off weight, inline measurement, wash test), when the buyer receives reports (dates or completion percentages), and what triggers a stop — for example, first-off weight outside tolerance stops that machine until re-approved. Factories respect buyers who specify this, because a stop at 5% completion costs the factory an afternoon, while a failed final inspection costs everyone a vessel and a season.

Frequently asked questions

Is inline inspection the same as a DUPRO inspection?

DUPRO — during-production inspection — is the third-party version of inline inspection: an inspector visits when 20–40% of goods are finished and samples against your spec. It is useful, but it is one visit at one moment. Factory inline QC runs continuously, every machine, every shift. The strongest programmes combine the two: continuous factory records, plus one independent DUPRO visit on high-stakes orders as verification.

Does inline inspection replace the pre-shipment inspection?

No. Inline inspection controls the process; the pre-shipment inspection is the formal acceptance decision on the finished lot under AQL sampling. Skipping the final inspection because inline was clean removes your last gate — packaging, labelling and final measurement are only visible on finished, packed goods. Think of inline as making the final inspection boring, which is the goal.

What does inline inspection cost the buyer?

Factory-run inline QC is normally included in the price — it is part of running a proper knitwear floor. Third-party DPI adds roughly USD 300–400 per man-day at the factory, which on a 2,000–3,000 piece order is a few cents per unit. Set that against reworking or sorting a failed lot — or missing a delivery window — and the arithmetic is not close.
