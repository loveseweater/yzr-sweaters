Product Liability Insurance for Knitwear Brands: What It Covers and Why Your Factory's Policy Doesn't Help

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Product liability insurance covers claims that your product injured someone or damaged property — for apparel, think flammability, choking hazards, metal fragments and chemical skin reactions. If you import knitwear under your brand, the claim lands on you first, and a Chinese factory's insurance (when it exists at all) usually doesn't respond to a lawsuit in your market. You need your own policy; typical small-brand cover is USD 1–2 million per occurrence, priced on your turnover and product risk.

"Don't worry, the factory is insured" is a sentence we hear from new brands, and it deserves a hard look. Product liability is one of those topics that feels abstract until a customer emails a photo of a rash, or a marketplace suspends your listing and asks for a certificate of insurance. This guide covers what the insurance actually does, what apparel claims really look like, and the uncomfortable truth about relying on your supplier's cover.

What product liability claims look like in knitwear

Knitwear is a low-risk category compared with, say, electronics — but not a zero-risk one. The claim patterns that actually occur:

  • Chemical and skin reactions. Dyes, finishes or formaldehyde residues causing dermatitis. This is why restricted-substances testing exists — see RSL testing — and why the paperwork matters as much as the test.
  • Metal contamination. A broken needle fragment left in a garment is a genuine injury risk and a genuine claim; needle detection is your first defence and your first piece of evidence.
  • Choking and attachment hazards on baby and kids' knits — buttons, pompoms and embellishments that detach. Children's product rules are strictest here; see children's knitwear compliance.
  • Flammability. Less common in everyday sweaters than in sleepwear, but sleepwear-adjacent loungewear edges into regulated territory.
  • Drawstring and cord injuries on children's hooded styles — the reason cords on kids' upper-body garments are restricted outright in the US and EU.

Why the claim lands on you, not the factory

In the US and EU, anyone in the supply chain can be sued — and in practice the plaintiff's lawyers start with whoever is reachable and solvent in their jurisdiction: the brand whose label is on the garment. You may have a contractual right to recover from your supplier, but that right is only as good as the supplier's assets and willingness, across a border, under a different legal system. Which brings us to the insurance question.

The uncomfortable truth about your factory's insurance

Some Chinese factories carry product liability insurance; many carry none; and a large share of those that do hold domestic policies written for the Chinese market, with limits and jurisdiction clauses that simply don't engage when a consumer in Ohio or Hamburg files a claim. "We're insured" may be entirely true and entirely useless to you. If a supplier's insurance is part of your risk plan, ask for the certificate and read three things: the insurer, the territorial and jurisdiction limits, and whether your market's sales are covered. Most of the time, the honest conclusion is that you need your own policy.

What a policy costs and what it covers

For a small knitwear brand or importer, standard product liability cover is typically USD 1 million per occurrence / USD 2 million aggregate, and premiums are priced mainly on your annual turnover and product category — apparel sits in a low-to-moderate risk band, so cover is usually affordable relative to revenue. Marketplaces and larger retailers increasingly demand a certificate before listing or ranging your product, which settles the "is it required?" question commercially even where the law doesn't. Optional extensions worth knowing about: recall insurance (covers the logistics and notification cost of pulling a defective product) and worldwide jurisdiction including the US, which must be stated explicitly.

How to lower both the premium and the risk

  • Keep your testing records. Lab reports, RSL certificates and needle-detection logs are both prevention and defence — insurers ask for them, and courts reward them.
  • Get labels right. Fibre content and care labels are legal documents; see US fibre content labelling and care labelling rules. Mislabelling converts a quality issue into a compliance one.
  • Use contracts that work. Indemnity and insurance clauses in your purchase contract give you a recovery path; our supply contract guide covers the essentials.
  • Log incidents. A dated record of complaints and resolutions is gold in both underwriting and litigation.

One clarification that saves confusion: product liability is not cargo insurance (goods lost or damaged in transit) and not a warranty against quality complaints — it responds to injury and property damage caused by the product. You may need all three; they do different jobs.

Frequently asked questions

Is product liability insurance legally required for selling knitwear?

Rarely by statute for ordinary adult apparel — but commercially, often yes. Major marketplaces and retail chains require a certificate of insurance as a listing or ranging condition, and some jurisdictions' rules for children's products make operating without cover reckless even where it isn't strictly mandated. Treat it as a cost of selling under your own label.

Does product liability insurance cover quality complaints and returns?

No. It covers claims for bodily injury or property damage caused by the product — not pilling, sizing issues or a customer who simply doesn't like the fit. Those are commercial quality matters, handled through your inspection and returns processes; insurance is for the claim that arrives with a lawyer attached.

How much cover should a small knitwear brand carry?

USD 1 million per occurrence with USD 2 million aggregate is the common starting point and satisfies most marketplace and retailer requirements. Brands selling children's products, or with growing US exposure, often step up from there. Price it on your actual turnover and category with a broker who knows apparel — the premium is usually modest next to the revenue it protects.
