Selling Knitwear in the UK Market: Layering, Retailers and Rules

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Quick answer

The UK is a layering market rather than a deep-winter market: mild, wet and changeable weather keeps knitwear on the shop floor almost all year, so fine-gauge cotton and blends sell alongside wool. Orders typically run in two phases, autumn/winter and spring/summer. Because the UK is now a separate customs territory from the EU, a UK delivery and an EU delivery cannot share one customs entry.

Ask a British buyer why knitwear sells in July and the answer is usually the weather. Mild, damp and famously unpredictable conditions make a light knit useful for most of the year, which is why the UK does not follow the Continental pattern of one short, sharp winter season. That difference shapes the calendar, the yarn choices and the way a UK range is built.

Why does the UK buy knitwear differently?

The UK climate is maritime and mild. Winters are rarely severe enough to make heavy chunky knits the default, and summers are rarely hot enough to stop knitwear selling. What the weather does reward is layering: a fine-gauge merino or cotton-blend knit worn over a shirt, under a jacket, or on its own on a cool morning. That keeps knitwear in the assortment for spring and summer as well as autumn and winter.

The practical consequence is that a UK buyer usually needs two or three delivery windows a year, not one. Autumn/winter knitwear is ordered in roughly February to May and delivered across July to October; spring/summer knitwear is ordered from about August and delivered from February to April. A transitional period in March to May and September to November is where mid-weight knits earn their keep, which is the logic behind our notes on transitional season knitwear.

Christmas matters more in the UK than in most markets, because knitwear is a classic gift. Gifting ranges need to be on the floor before the late-November discounting period rather than arriving with it, and our knitwear buying calendar sets out where the ordering has to fall.

What do UK retailers expect from a supplier?

The UK retail structure has three distinct layers, and they buy very differently. Large multiple retailers and grocery-owned fashion chains run own-label programmes with their own buying and technical teams; they care about compliance documentation, consistency between lots and reliable delivery. Below them is a large and active independent sector — boutiques, menswear independents and online specialists — that buys in smaller quantities and cares more about hand feel, uniqueness and the flexibility to reorder.

Language matters in a small way. What a US buyer calls a sweater is a jumper to most British buyers, and knitwear is the umbrella that covers jumpers, cardigans and knitted tops. Womenswear sizes are UK numbers, and menswear is frequently sold by chest measurement in inches, which is worth checking before a size chart is issued. Our notes on sizing and grading cover how to build a market-correct chart.

On compliance, most UK buyers will ask for the same social and product safety evidence you already hold for Europe — a BSCI or SEDEX audit, Oeko-Tex for harmful substances, and restricted substances documentation. Value-tier programmes are relentlessly price-competitive; the independent and premium tiers pay for a better hand feel and a more interesting stitch. Colour is generally conservative in the core, so a navy, charcoal or oatmeal base with one or two seasonal colours still describes most successful UK ranges.

What has Brexit changed for knitwear imports?

The central change is that the UK is a separate customs territory from the EU. A UK delivery needs its own customs entry and cannot be folded into an EU shipment. Goods made in China and imported into the UK are subject to the UK Global Tariff, and EU preferential arrangements do not apply to Chinese-origin goods. If an order is to be split between the UK and the EU, plan two entries rather than assuming the goods can be redistributed freely once landed.

Documentation is largely what an experienced importer expects: commercial invoice, packing list, HS code and country of origin, with the importer of record handling UK import VAT and duty. The choice of Incoterms has become a more visible conversation — larger buyers tend to work FOB with their own forwarder — and our Incoterms guide explains where responsibility transfers in each case.

Northern Ireland is a special case worth flagging early, because movements from Great Britain to Northern Ireland follow different rules from movements into the EU. Rates and procedures are best confirmed with the buyer's customs broker; the planning point for a supplier is simply to know which of the two markets the goods are entering. Note also that a sweater knitted in China does not become a UK-origin product by being stored or shipped from the UK.

Which sizing, labelling and safety rules apply in the UK?

Chemical compliance runs through UK REACH, which is a separate regime from EU REACH even though the restricted substances lists are currently closely aligned. In practice buyers ask for test reports covering restricted substances such as certain azo colourants, and the documentation has to name the correct regulation for the destination. Children's nightwear is a UK-specific trap: garments intended for sleep are subject to flammability requirements, and products marketed as loungewear can fall inside them depending on design and fabric. If a childrenswear style could be read as sleepwear, have the testing house confirm which regime applies before the order is placed — the same discipline our children's knitwear safety notes describe.

Sizing is the quieter issue. A UK 12 is not a US 8 on paper in the way many buyers assume, and British consumers on average prefer a slightly longer body length and a less closely fitted shape than some Continental markets. Reusing a US chart with the size numbers relabelled is the mistake that shows up as returns.

How should you plan a UK knitwear range?

  • Work two buying windows a year. Autumn/winter orders from February to May, spring/summer orders from August, with Christmas gifting stock landing by late October. Missing the Black Friday period costs a UK buyer a large share of the season.
  • Weight the yarn range towards mid-weight. Lambswool and merino blends in the 7 to 12 gauge band carry the core, with fine-gauge cotton and cotton blends for spring and summer. Heavy chunky knits are a smaller share of the UK market than in Nordic or German ranges.
  • Use the heritage story, because it sells. Cable, Aran and textured stitches read as authentically British to British consumers, and a supplier who can knit them well is worth keeping. Our notes on knit patterns cover the structures involved.
  • Send a UK-specific size chart with the sample, along with a care and composition label mock-up. Buyers who receive a complete pack move faster.
  • Check the port and the terms. UK sea freight usually enters through Felixstowe, Southampton or London Gateway, and transit from South China is commonly around thirty to forty-five days port to port, with peaks in the run-up to the fourth quarter.

Frequently asked questions

Is knitwear a winter-only category in the UK?

No. The UK climate is mild and changeable rather than extreme, and layering is a genuine habit, so lightweight and mid-weight knits sell through spring and summer as well as winter. Most UK buyers work two knitting seasons a year, with a transitional period in between where mid-weight knits do particularly well.

Does a UK order need different compliance from an EU order?

Broadly aligned but not identical, and they are separate legal regimes. UK REACH and EU REACH operate independently even though the restricted substances lists are currently close, so documentation should name the correct regulation for the destination. Children's sleepwear also has UK-specific flammability rules that can catch garments marketed as loungewear.

Can a UK delivery serve EU customers?

Physically yes, if the business has a distribution setup for it, but customs-wise an EU-bound sale needs its own entry. The UK and the EU are separate customs territories, and goods made in China do not gain EU preferential treatment by being shipped from a UK warehouse.
