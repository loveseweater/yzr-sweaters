Hank Dyeing vs Package Dyeing: How Your Sweater Yarn Actually Gets Its Colour

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Most buyers specify a colour and a fibre and stop there, but the physical form the yarn is dyed in — loose hanks (skeins), wound packages (cones) or loose fibre before spinning — changes shade uniformity, hand feel, minimum order quantity and lead time. Hank dyeing keeps the most loft and suits wool and cashmere blends on small runs; package dyeing is cheaper and more uniform at volume but needs a well-built cone. Ask which method your mill plans to use before you approve a lab dip, because approving a hank-dyed swatch and then running bulk on package-dyed yarn is a reliable way to lose the colour.

When a colour comes back slightly flat, or a shade drifts between the approved sample and the third carton, we usually start by asking the mill a boring question: how was the yarn dyed? The answer is often hank for the sample and package for the bulk. Both are perfectly good methods, but they are not interchangeable — here is what actually differs, and why it shows up in your margin.

Three forms, three different processes

Yarn has to be in some physical form before it goes near a dyebath, and that form decides how the liquor reaches the fibre:

  • Hank or skein dyeing. The yarn is reeled into loose skeins, hung on rods and dipped or circulated in a dyebath with almost no tension on the yarn. It is the gentlest route, it keeps bulk and loft, and it can run very small lots — a few kilos of one shade is possible. The cost is handling: every skein has to be wound back onto a cone before knitting.
  • Package dyeing. Yarn is wound onto a perforated cone or tube, loaded into a pressure vessel, and dye liquor is pumped through the package from the inside out. It is efficient and economical at volume, but penetration depends on how evenly and how densely the cone was wound — our notes on winding and waxing cover that step. A hard-wound package gives you a pale core and a dark outer layer.
  • Fibre, top or dope dyeing. Colour goes in before the yarn is spun — at the loose-fibre or sliver stage, or into the polymer for man-made fibres. This is how melanges and heathers are made, and it gives excellent uniformity and fastness, but you are committing to a blend and a large minimum; see fancy yarns and colour effects.

There is a fourth route that bypasses yarn dyeing altogether: dyeing the knitted panel or the finished garment. That is a different commercial decision, covered in yarn-dyed vs piece-dyed knitwear and garment dyeing and washing.

What the method changes for you

  • Hand feel. Hank-dyed wool keeps more loft and softness; package-dyed yarn is slightly compressed. On a brushed or lofty style the difference is visible, not just measurable — relevant to brushed finishes and hand feel and micron.
  • Shade uniformity. Package dyeing can produce inside-outside differences when the cone density is wrong; hank dyeing can vary skein to skein if circulation is poor. Both show up downstream as barre or joins in the panel — see knitwear fabric defects and colour consistency.
  • Minimums and lead time. A package-dyeing machine is loaded by the batch. If the smallest vessel is 100 kg, the whole batch has to be that shade, and a 300-piece order cannot absorb it. Hank dyeing runs much smaller lots, or you use a stock-dyed colour — the arithmetic is in yarn minimums and colour choice.
  • Colourfastness. Dyestuff class matters more than method, but penetration differences show up in rubbing and washing fastness on deep navy, black and bright red; check against the colourfastness guide.

Why the sample and the bulk disagree

The classic case: the development sample was knitted from hank-dyed skeins left over from another order, bulk was package-dyed from a fresh lot, and nobody thought to mention it. Dyestuff uptake differs by fibre, count, twist and method, so the swatch is not the same colour any more. A workable approval chain is a lab dip on the right substrate, then a knitted swatch from the actual production yarn and method, then a shade band with light and dark tolerances as described in the colour card and checked under more than one light source for metamerism. Keep a sealed reference sample for the dispute that never quite happens — gold seal samples explains the habit. If the order runs short of yarn mid-production, a top-up dye lot is where a second shade enters the cartons.

What to put in the tech pack

  • State the dyeing method, not just the fibre and a Pantone reference.
  • Ask for the dyelot size and whether the whole order can come from one lot.
  • Require the approval swatch to be knitted from the production yarn and production method.
  • Ask for a shade band and a check under D65 and store lighting.
  • If a reorder is planned, ask whether yarn can be reserved — see yarn stock planning and first order to reorder.

Where the water goes

Dyeing is the wet part of the process. Package dyeing generally uses less liquor per kilo than hank dyeing, and both are subject to effluent rules that buyers increasingly ask about — our dyeing water and effluent guide covers what a mill can actually document. If a lower-impact shade matters to your range, choosing a stock colour or a dope-dyed fibre often beats commissioning a fresh custom dyelot on both cost and water.

Frequently asked questions

Is hank-dyed yarn better quality than package-dyed?

Not better, just different. Hank dyeing is gentler and keeps more loft, which shows on soft wool and cashmere blends. Package dyeing is more uniform and economical at volume. Judge the knitted swatch rather than the method name.

Why does a custom colour have a minimum quantity?

Because a package-dyeing machine is loaded by the batch. If the smallest vessel is 100 kg, the whole batch has to be that shade and a small order cannot absorb it. Hank dyeing can run much smaller lots, or you can pick from stock-dyed colours.

Can I approve a lab dip on one yarn and switch to another for bulk?

You can, but do not expect the same shade. Dyestuff uptake differs by fibre, count and even by dyeing method. Approve the colour on the exact production yarn and method, then keep a sealed reference sample for comparison.
