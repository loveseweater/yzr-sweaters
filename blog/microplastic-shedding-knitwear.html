Microfibre Shedding in Knitwear: What Brands Can Actually Control

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

Microfibre shedding is the release of tiny fibre fragments from a fabric, and synthetic knitwear — polyester, acrylic, nylon — sheds most heavily during washing because those fibres are plastic-based and do not biodegrade. The amount released is driven mainly by fibre length, yarn twist, fabric density and washing conditions. Brands control the first three at the sourcing stage; washing conditions can only be influenced through care guidance.

Polyester, acrylic and nylon knitwear releases tiny fibre fragments during wear and washing, and how much escapes is shaped mainly by decisions taken at the yarn and fabric stage. Brands cannot control how customers wash, but they control almost everything upstream — and that is where the useful work happens.

What is microfibre shedding?

Microfibres are extremely fine fibres — in textiles, typically finer than about one decitex, the mass in grams of 10,000 metres of fibre. Microfibre shedding is their release from a fabric during wear, washing and drying: a knit is thousands of interlocking loops holding fibre ends in place, and friction works the least secure ones free.

Natural fibres shed too, but wool and cotton fragments biodegrade, so attention has settled on polyester, acrylic, nylon and other plastic-based fibres. Shedding is not pilling: pilling is fibre tangling into bobbles still attached to the garment. The surface side is covered in our guide to preventing sweater pilling.

What determines how much a knitwear style sheds?

Five variables explain most of the difference between a low-shedding knit and a heavy shedder, and all five appear in the yarn specification.

Fibre length (staple length)

Staple length is the length of the individual fibres spun into a yarn. Short staples give twist less surface to grip, so they work loose and escape; longer staples and continuous filament yarns are held far more securely. Our sweater yarn guide compares the two.

Yarn twist and structure

Twist binds fibres into a yarn. Low-twist yarns feel soft but hold their fibres loosely; a firmer twist, or a two-fold construction that traps fibre ends inside the yarn, keeps more fibre mass where it belongs. Ask the spinner what twist a yarn is delivered at.

Fabric density and structure

A tight, dense knit exposes fewer loose fibre ends to friction than an open structure. Ribs present more ends, and brushed or raised finishes — fibres deliberately pulled to the surface — increase shedding sharply. Our explanation of sweater fabric weight (GSM) shows how density is written into a tech pack.

Fibre type and blend ratio

Polyester, acrylic and nylon are the fibres most associated with persistent release, and even a small percentage of elastane can loosen a structure. Ask for the full composition, not the dominant fibre.

Washing conditions

Higher temperatures, longer cycles, aggressive agitation and harsh detergent chemistry all raise release. Most shedding happens in the drum, so small changes here multiply across a garment's lifetime — but they sit in the customer's hands, not the factory's.

What can brands control before production?

You cannot redesign the washing machine, but you can influence every upstream variable.

  • Specify longer staple or filament yarns — the highest-leverage decision, and one that shows up immediately in the yarn specification.
  • Agree a minimum twist level with the yarn supplier and write it into the spec so reorders match.
  • Avoid brushed and heavily textured surfaces unless the design genuinely needs them.
  • Choose denser structures — tighter gauge, higher cover — for the same silhouette.
  • Consider fibre substitution where performance allows; our overview of recycled and organic knitwear fibres covers the options.
  • Ask about yarn-level anti-shedding treatments where the spinner offers them.

None of these choices is free: long-staple yarns cost more, firmer twists change hand feel, denser knits weigh more. Make the trade-off deliberately, at specification stage, with your sustainability claim in view.

What can be done at the washing stage?

Most release happens in the drum, so consumer habits matter more than any factory process. Useful guidance stays specific: wash at lower temperature, run shorter and gentler cycles, wash less often, use a laundry bag or inline fibre filter, and avoid tumble drying. Repeat these points on the care label and product page — not because they eliminate shedding, but because they reduce the rate. General care advice sits in our sweater care and fabric guide.

Why does this matter to your brand now?

Consumer awareness of microplastic pollution has grown steadily, and several markets are reviewing how textiles and washing machines should respond. Direction is still developing, so treat specific thresholds cautiously and confirm the position in each target market with a qualified adviser. The practical outcome is simple: a low-shedding specification is becoming a claim you can support with evidence.

How do you raise the topic with a supplier?

Ask concrete questions and listen for concrete answers.

  • What is the staple length of this yarn, or is it a filament?
  • At what twist level is it delivered, and can that be specified?
  • Will this construction be brushed or raised in finishing?
  • Can you provide yarn composition and washing test results for comparable styles?
  • What would you change to reduce shedding, and what would it cost?

A supplier who understands the issue will talk about fibre length and yarn structure and will name the trade-offs. One who answers only with a certification list is not engaging.

Frequently asked questions

Does a tighter knit really release less fibre?

Generally yes, because a denser structure traps more fibre ends inside the yarn and presents fewer loose ends to friction. The effect is strongest when density is combined with longer fibres and a firmer twist. Density alone will not rescue a short-staple, loosely twisted yarn.

Are anti-shedding finishes available for any yarn?

Not universally. Yarn-level treatments exist, but availability varies by spinner and fibre type, and the technology is still developing. Treat any such finish as an option to verify case by case, and ask for test evidence rather than accepting a marketing description.

Is microfibre shedding from clothing regulated yet?

There is no single global rule. Several markets are reviewing textile and washing-machine measures, and requirements differ by region and product category. Confirm the position in your target markets with a qualified adviser before making claims, and focus on specifications you can verify today.
