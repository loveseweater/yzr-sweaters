Yarn-Dyed vs Piece-Dyed Knitwear: Which Dyeing Route Should You Choose?

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

Yarn dyeing colours the yarn before knitting, so the colour sits in the fibre, fastness on deep shades is usually better, and multi-colour, striped or jacquard styles are possible — at the cost of higher minimums and longer lead times. Piece dyeing (garment dyeing) knits first in natural yarn and colours the sweater afterwards, which lowers minimums and shortens the front end for single-colour orders. Choose by order size, colour complexity and how tight your delivery date is.

Yarn-dyed and piece-dyed knitwear reach the same colour by different routes, and the route you choose changes your minimum order quantity, lead time, colour accuracy and how the finished sweater behaves in wear. Here is how to judge which one fits your order.

What are yarn dyeing and piece dyeing?

Both routes put colour on a sweater; the difference is when. Yarn dyeing colours the yarn before knitting, piece dyeing colours the garment or knitted fabric afterwards. Almost every commercial trade-off follows from that timing.

Yarn dyeing (yarn-dyed)

Yarn dyeing means the yarn is dyed — in hank or package form — before a single stitch is knitted, so the shade is inside the fibre from the start. Because every yarn carries its own colour, stripes, colour-blocking and jacquard patterns (patterns knitted in from different coloured yarns) are simply a matter of loading the right yarns on the machine.

Piece dyeing and garment dyeing (piece-dyed)

Piece dyeing means the goods are knitted first in undyed natural yarn and coloured afterwards. In flat-knit sweater production this normally means garment dyeing: the finished sweater is dyed whole. The term can also cover dyeing knitted panels in cut-and-sew knitwear, but the logic is the same — colour goes in late, after the shape exists. This is the commercial route, not the wet process itself; for the process mechanics, see garment dyeing, washing and finishing.

What are the commercial strengths of yarn-dyed knitwear?

  • Colour sits in the fibre. Deep and vivid shades usually give the best colour fastness — the dyed material's resistance to losing colour in washing, rubbing and light — because the dye penetrates the yarn rather than sitting on a garment surface.
  • Multicolour works. Stripes, jacquard and colour-blocking are practical only on this route.
  • Repeat orders match more easily, because colour is controlled in the yarn lot and can be checked before knitting starts.
  • The trade-offs: coloured yarn must be bought ahead, minimums and lead times are longer, and dyed yarn cannot be re-dyed.

What are the commercial strengths of piece-dyed knitwear?

  • Low minimums and quick tests. Knitting from natural yarn lets you run a small trial quantity in a new colour before committing to a programme.
  • A shorter front end for simple orders, because no dyed yarn has to be ordered before knitting starts.
  • Late colour decisions. Undyed pieces can be held and dyed when a colourway proves itself, which suits fast colour top-ups.
  • Effects yarn dyeing cannot copy — washed and garment-dyed looks with deliberate depth variation across the surface.
  • The trade-offs: uniform colour is harder to control on a whole garment; deep and vivid shades are the most demanding; fastness must be tested rather than assumed; and the wet process adds shrinkage and distortion risk.

Which dyeing route should you choose?

Use the order profile, not preference, to decide.

  • Small quantity, single colour, testing a style → piece dyeing keeps it viable.
  • Multi-colour, stripes or jacquard → yarn dyeing, the only real route.
  • Extreme fastness demands — deep shades, kidswear, garments washed often → yarn dyeing.
  • Colour must repeat against a signed reference → yarn dyeing gives tighter base-colour control.
  • Late colour additions or top-ups → piece dyeing, because undyed production can be coloured late.
  • A tight schedule on a small single-colour run → piece dyeing shortens the front end.

How do you approve colour on each route?

A lab dip is a small dye sample prepared to your reference colour for approval before bulk, and both routes use one — but on different substrates. On yarn-dyed orders, approve the lab dip on yarn, keep a sealed yarn standard, then check the first knitted panels against it. On garment-dyed orders, approve the colour on a knitted and dyed swatch: a yarn sample cannot show how the colour will read in the finished knit. Approve under controlled light, hold a signed standard on both sides, then follow our sweater sampling process guide.

Does the fibre blend change the choice?

It can decide it. A blend spins two or more fibres into one yarn — wool with nylon, wool with viscose, cotton with acrylic — and different fibres absorb dye at different rates, so one fibre may take the bath more deeply than another. Yarn dyeing lets the mill colour and check the blend before knitting; in garment dyeing the whole sweater passes through one bath, so uneven uptake shows as a two-tone cast. Wool blends also dislike aggressive hot wet processing, making temperature control critical on garment-dyed wool.

What problems should buyers watch for?

  • Batch shade variation — keep one dye lot per colourway and signed retention samples, and compare every delivery with the sealed standard.
  • Crocking on dark shades — crocking is colour transfer through rubbing; test fastness on the finished garment, not the yarn.
  • Shrinkage after garment dyeing — agree dimensional tolerances before bulk and verify them on the dyed, washed sample.
  • Repeat-order drift — define an acceptable deviation from the standard, and reserve dyed yarn for re-orders on yarn-dyed programmes.

Frequently asked questions

Which dyeing route is cheaper for a small first order?

Neither route is automatically cheaper. Yarn dyeing carries the cost of buying dyed yarn in minimum dye-lot quantities, while garment dyeing adds a wet process and extra handling to finished garments. On a small single-colour order, piece dyeing usually produces the lower total because you are not committed to a minimum yarn lot; on a large multi-colour programme, yarn dyeing is the only route and its cost is spread across volume. The reliable answer comes from quoting both on your actual quantity and colour count.

Is piece-dyed knitwear lower quality than yarn-dyed?

No. Piece dyeing is a different route, not a lower grade, and well-made garment-dyed sweaters are common in premium ranges. What changes is the control you need: piece-dyed orders call for tighter written agreements on shade tolerance, dimensional change and fastness, because all three are decided after the garment is knitted.

Can one collection use both dyeing routes?

Yes, and many do. Striped and jacquard styles in a range go on the yarn-dyed route while plain single-colour styles go on the garment-dyed route. Keep separate standards and tolerances for each route, because the two will not deliver identical shade behaviour under the same colour name.
