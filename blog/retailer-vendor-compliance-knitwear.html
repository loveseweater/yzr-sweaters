Retailer Vendor Compliance for Knitwear: Routing Guides, Labels and Chargebacks

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

A retailer's vendor compliance manual — usually called the routing guide — dictates ticketing, packaging, carton marks, advance shipping notices, delivery windows and carrier rules. Breaches are not discussed, they are deducted from your invoice as chargebacks. Get the current routing guide before the tech pack is final, cost compliance into the unit price, and make the factory responsible for every requirement it controls.

Landing a department store or a large chain feels like the moment a knitwear brand has made it. Then the vendor manual arrives: sixty pages of label placement, carton weight limits and EDI requirements, with a deduction schedule at the back. We see brands cost the garment carefully and forget the compliance entirely — which turns a healthy wholesale margin into a series of small, unexplained deductions.

What a routing guide actually covers

It is the retailer's rulebook for how goods must arrive. Expect sections on:

  • Ticketing and identification. Exact ticket format, where it is attached, which GS1/GTIN numbers are allowed, and how a barcode must scan. Our GTIN and barcode guide covers the numbering side; the routing guide adds placement and print quality.
  • Packaging. Polybag type, warnings, hanger or flat-pack, whether units must be individually bagged, and what a set looks like. For marketplace fulfilment the logic is similar — see Amazon FBA prep for knitwear.
  • Carton marks and contents. Carton label format, maximum weight and dimensions, whether mixed-SKU cartons are allowed, and the content documentation each carton needs. See shipping documents for the wider paperwork set.
  • ASN / EDI and appointment booking. An advance shipping notice (typically an EDI 856) must be transmitted in a set window before the truck arrives, with a delivery appointment booked against the retailer's dock schedule.
  • Delivery window and carrier. A narrow "in-stock" window, often with the retailer's nominated carrier and Incoterm/DDP responsibilities spelled out — check these against your Incoterms rather than assuming they match.
  • Version control. Manuals are updated, usually annually. Working from last year's PDF is a classic way to fail.

Where knitwear orders actually fail

Some of these are generic, but knitwear adds its own failure modes:

  • Ticket attachment on a soft surface. A knitted garment has no firm place for a sticker. Retailers usually specify a ticket on the polybag or a specific hangtag position; a sticker applied directly to knit fabric peels, damages the surface, or scans badly.
  • Flat pack versus GOH. Some retailers require garments on hangers, others forbid them. If the manual says hanging, that is a completely different packing and freight plan — see GOH shipping.
  • Compression and creasing. Overstuffed cartons leave press marks on ribs and brushed finishes, which arrive as "damaged" before anyone scans a label. Our packing guide covers the carton discipline.
  • Colour and size ratio drift. The purchase order says 1:2:2:1; the cartons say something else. Count accuracy against the PO is a compliance item, not a courtesy — plan it with size ratio planning.
  • Late or unbooked delivery. Missing the dock window by a day can mean a refused delivery and a chargeback, even when the goods are perfect.

Chargebacks: how the money actually leaves

A chargeback is a deduction the retailer takes against your invoice when a shipment breaches the manual — commonly a fixed amount per carton plus a percentage of the purchase order cost, with separate penalties for late ASN, unscannable tickets, mislabelled cartons or short shipments. The amounts vary widely by retailer, so read your own manual rather than relying on anyone else's numbers. Two things are consistent: the deduction almost always exceeds what compliance would have cost, and disputing it requires contemporaneous photos, carton lists and ASN confirmations. Repeat breaches hit your vendor scorecard, which affects future orders and terms far more than any single deduction.

Build compliance into the sourcing, not the shipping

  • Send the routing guide with the tech pack. Label artwork, polybag spec and carton rules belong in the tech pack from day one, so the factory quotes the real work.
  • Quote compliance separately. Ticketing, individual bagging, set assembly and carton marking are labour. If they're buried in a "free" unit price, they'll be rushed at packing.
  • Assign ownership explicitly. The factory normally handles ticketing and cartons; the brand or its 3PL usually handles EDI/ASN because it needs the retailer's system access. Write down who books the carrier and who transmits the ASN — see contract essentials.
  • Approve retail packaging on the pre-production sample. One fully packed, ticketed, cartoned unit, photographed and signed off, before bulk. Then verify again at pre-shipment inspection.
  • Keep one person on version control. Somebody must own "which revision of the manual applies to this PO".

And be honest about whether the channel is worth it. For many small knitwear brands, independent boutiques and curated wholesale platforms are more profitable per unit once you cost the compliance overhead — our wholesale showroom guide covers that route.

Frequently asked questions

Can a factory handle ticketing and ASN for us?

Ticketing, polybagging, carton marks and packing lists, yes — those are physical operations at the factory. ASN/EDI usually cannot be done there, because it requires access to the retailer's system and a precise transmission window. That normally stays with the brand or its 3PL. Put the split in writing; "the factory will handle compliance" is how this gets missed.

How big is a typical chargeback?

It varies by retailer and breach type — commonly a fixed amount per carton or event, plus a percentage of the PO value, with separate penalties for late ASN or late delivery. Read your own manual's deduction schedule rather than quoting anyone else's. The consistent finding is that the deduction costs more than doing it right at the factory.

Should a small brand sell to a major retailer?

Only after costing the overhead. Labelling, EDI, appointment logistics, deduction risk and longer payment terms can consume the margin on a small first order. Independent boutiques and curated wholesale platforms often give a better net return per unit, with far less administrative load. Start with the channel you can serve perfectly.
