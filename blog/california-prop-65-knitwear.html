California Prop 65 and Knitwear: Warnings, Testing and Real Risk

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Prop 65 is California's right-to-know law, not a federal ban: if a product exposes Californians to a listed chemical above safe-harbour levels, you either reformulate, or provide a clear and reasonable warning — and enforcement is driven largely by private plaintiffs who share the penalties. For knitwear, the real-world triggers are almost never the knit fabric: they are lead in metal trims, phthalates in prints and coatings, and formaldehyde from certain finishes. Test the trims and prints as well as the fabric, keep the reports, and the risk stops being scary.

A US buyer emails us, six weeks before shipment, a forwarded chain from their California retailer: "Prop 65 compliant?" — and no definition of what compliant means. We see this a lot, and the panic is usually misplaced. Prop 65 is one of the most misunderstood pieces of consumer regulation in the apparel trade: it is not a safety standard with pass/fail certificates, it is a warning law enforced by lawsuits. This guide explains what it actually demands, where knitwear realistically trips it, and what to put in your factory order so the question stops arriving by forwarded email.

What Prop 65 actually is

The Safe Drinking Water and Toxic Enforcement Act of 1986 — "Prop 65" — requires California to maintain a list of chemicals known to cause cancer or reproductive harm, now numbering roughly nine hundred. Two duties follow for businesses selling into California: do not discharge listed chemicals into drinking water, and do not knowingly and intentionally expose people to them without "a clear and reasonable warning." Exposures below the state's published safe-harbour levels are exempt; so is exposure that poses no significant risk, demonstrated with your own scientific evidence.

The feature that makes it unlike European RSL regimes is enforcement. Beyond state prosecutors, any private citizen can sue an alleged violator and collect a share of the penalties, which are assessed per violation per day. That bounty mechanism created an industry of specialised plaintiffs' firms that test retail products, and it is why an obscure Californian state law effectively sets a de facto national standard — brands that ship anywhere in the US mostly engineer to the California rule because freight does not reliably sort itself by destination state. It also means "Prop 65 compliance" has no certificate: there is only a product that is clean enough not to warn, backed by test evidence, or a product that carries a warning label.

Where knitwear actually trips it

Plain knitted fabric in wool, cotton, acrylic or polyester almost never carries a listed chemical at meaningful exposure. The risk concentrates in the components and finishes layered onto it — the parts of the garment that change most often and get specified least:

  • Lead in metal trims. Zips, buttons, rivets, snap fasteners and metal badges can carry lead in the alloy or plating. Skin-contact lead carries a very low safe-harbour exposure threshold, so even small quantities matter; cheap hardware from unvetted sources is the classic offender.
  • Phthalates in prints and coatings. Plastisol screen prints, puff prints, PVC patches and coated trims are the usual carriers of listed phthalate plasticisers. If your programme uses placement printing or any soft plastic component, that is where your testing budget belongs — see our printing guide for the chemistry of what sits on the fabric.
  • Formaldehyde in finishes. Certain resin finishes and some dye carriers can leave formaldehyde, a listed chemical with a long enforcement history in textiles. Easy-care and anti-shrink finishes are the higher-risk routes; a standard sweater finishing bath is not.
  • Dye and accessory edge cases. Certain azo dye precursors, PFAS in water-repellent finishes, and cadmium in some pigments appear on the list and in occasional apparel settlements. These are the tail of the distribution — real, but far behind trims, prints and formaldehyde in frequency.

Childrenswear raises the stakes for two compounding reasons: children's exposure assumptions are stricter, and separate federal and state rules already apply, so anything in the kids' range should be running the fuller compliance programme in our children's knitwear safety guide anyway.

The warning label, and when you actually need one

A compliant warning names the listed chemical and is displayed "clearly and reasonably" — for consumer products, typically on the product or its immediate packaging. The familiar framed warning text is the safe-harbour version. But the decision logic matters more than the artwork: the duty is triggered by exposure above the level that the state has determined poses no significant risk, and if nobody in the chain "knowingly and intentionally" causes an exposure above that level, no warning is needed. That is why brands test: a laboratory result below the safe-harbour level is the evidence that no warning is required. Warning labels do exist on garments — usually on mass-market pieces with PVC prints where reformulation was uneconomic — but a brand positioned anywhere near quality is better served cleaning the product than labelling it.

Testing: what to buy, and what it costs

Efficient Prop 65 testing is targeted, not blanket. Test each component that can plausibly carry a listed chemical — the trims, prints and finishes above — and the fabric only where a finish has been applied. Specify the analytical method and the list of analytes in the test request, and require results reported against the safe-harbour levels for skin contact, not merely a generic pass. A focused panel for a knitwear programme typically runs to low hundreds of US dollars per component at an accredited third-party lab — the same labs and largely the same analytical methods used for the RSL testing your retailer may already require, which is why the two workstreams should be specified together: one order of tests, two compliance problems solved. Pull samples from bulk production, not development samples, and file the reports against purchase order numbers so they match the batch that shipped.

Who is liable when it goes wrong

The Act reaches "any person in the course of doing business" causing the exposure — in practice, the whole chain from manufacturer to retailer, with the deepest-pocket and most-visible defendants carrying the practical exposure. For an importing brand, the realistic scenario is not a state action but a private plaintiffs' letter after their testing finds a listed chemical in your retail product: settlement demand, penalty exposure per day per violation, attorney fees, and usually a reformulation or warning commitment going forward. Defense costs outpace the settlement. The documents that decide whether that letter becomes a nuisance or a crisis are the same three every time: your specification to the factory, the bulk test report, and the paper trail connecting them to the shipped batch. This is the same documentation discipline our supply contract guide builds for quality disputes generally, and the same reason to hold retained reference samples from the shipped lot.

Writing it into your factory order

The practical fix is four lines in the purchase paperwork. First, a Prop 65 clause in the supply agreement: the supplier warrants the product, including all trims and prints, will not expose consumers to listed chemicals above safe-harbour levels, and carries the rework cost if bulk testing fails. Second, a trim sourcing restriction: metal hardware and PVC/plastisol components only from named, tested sources — our trims guide covers how to lock this in the trim card. Third, a testing plan: which components get tested, at which lab, at bulk stage, paid for by whom. Fourth, record retention: test reports and batch mapping filed against your PO, so a query five years later is an afternoon's retrieval rather than an archaeology project.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a Prop 65 warning label on every sweater I sell in the US?

No. The warning duty applies only where a listed chemical is present at an exposure above the safe-harbour level, and typical knitwear without metal trims, PVC prints or resin finishes falls well below. Most brands satisfy the law by testing the risk components and keeping the reports — no label needed. Labels appear on garments where reformulation was uneconomic, which is a positioning decision most knitwear brands avoid.

Is Prop 65 a federal law or a quality standard?

Neither. It is a California state right-to-know law enforced largely through private lawsuits, with penalties assessed per violation per day. There is no "Prop 65 certificate" that can be issued — only laboratory evidence that exposures are below safe-harbour levels, plus a supply-chain paper trail. Its reach is national in practice because brands engineering to one US standard ship the same product everywhere.

What does Prop 65 testing cost and who should pay for it?

A targeted panel — lead on metal trims, phthalates on prints and coatings, formaldehyde where finishes are used — typically costs a few hundred US dollars per component at an accredited lab, far less than blanket screening. Convention puts first-bulk testing in the buyer's budget and retest-after-failure in the supplier's; whichever split you choose, write it into the purchase agreement together with the specification itself, so a failure has a defined cost owner rather than a negotiation.
