Vacuum and Compression Packing for Knitwear: Cutting Cube Without Crushing the Hand

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Compression saves freight because knitwear is billed on volume, not weight. The risk is that loft — the air held in a woollen-spun, brushed or chunky yarn — is part of the product, and a vacuum bag can flatten it into hard creases that steaming will not fully lift. Dense, fine-gauge cotton, acrylic and viscose knits tolerate it well; chunky wool, brushed or raised finishes, cashmere blends and heavy rib do not. Test on a real sample, then specify the compression ratio, bag, fold pattern and recovery check in the packing spec.

We see this a lot with buyers who spot a "compressed packing" line on a freight saving and assume it is free efficiency. It is not free. Knitwear is the one garment category where the air inside the fabric is part of what the customer is buying, and squeezing that air out to save a few CBM can change the hand, the look and the number of returns. Here is where compression genuinely pays and where we push back.

Why cube, not weight, decides the bill

Poly-bagged sweaters are light for their volume, so sea freight is charged on CBM, and a container cubes out long before it weighs out. That is the whole commercial case for compression: fewer cubic metres, lower freight, and on LCL a smaller bill entirely. Our container planning guide and the LCL crossover maths show how carton dimensions drive the number.

The same logic applies in reverse for air: chargeable weight uses a volumetric calculation, so a compressed carton can help there too, though air freight usually means the order is urgent enough that garment condition matters more than the saving. Compression is fundamentally a sea-freight, high-cube decision.

What compression actually does to a knit

A knitted fabric is a structure of loops holding air. Some of that volume is the yarn's own crimp and loft, some is the space between loops, and a good part of the hand feel is the fabric pushing back when you squeeze it. A vacuum bag removes the air and presses the loops flat against each other for weeks. Dense, tightly knitted structures spring back; lofty ones can stay flattened, and a hard fold line pressed in under vacuum behaves like a crease that was heat-set rather than one that happened in a suitcase.

This is why the decision belongs with the yarn and structure, not the warehouse. Our notes on stretch and recovery and hand feel and fibre fineness explain the same property from the specification side: two sweaters of identical GSM can respond to compression completely differently.

Which knits tolerate it, and which do not

There is no universal rule, but these bands hold up well enough to brief against:

  • Usually fine: fine-gauge flat jersey in cotton, acrylic, viscose or blends; smooth, dense structures with little surface loft. The garment is already compact, so there is little to lose.
  • Test first: mid-gauge wool blends, standard rib and interlock, anything with a defined surface pattern where a fold line would be visible.
  • Avoid or exclude: chunky and low-gauge knits, brushed or raised finishes, chenille, cashmere and cashmere blends, and heavy cable or sculpted structures. These are the styles where the loft is the product, and a fine cashmere that arrives flat is a complaint no discount fixes.

Also exclude anything with a stiff trim, a structured collar or an embellishment that could be pressed into the fabric face. A sequin or a heavy button leaves a permanent imprint under vacuum.

Creases, recovery and the moisture question

Three failure modes show up after a compressed transit. First, hard fold lines that survive unpacking and light steaming — our pressing and finishing guide explains why some creases are set by pressure and time rather than heat alone. Second, incomplete loft recovery, where the garment looks thin even after 48 hours of airing. Third, moisture: a sealed, tightly compressed bag holds whatever humidity was in the carton, and a long sea crossing through warm and cold zones is exactly the condition behind mould and mildew in shipped knitwear.

If compression is used, the bag needs to be properly dried and sealed, the carton needs desiccant and a sound transit-tested carton, and the goods need airing time at destination before they go on a hanger. Our fulfilment and storage note covers that receiving step, which buyers routinely leave out of the plan.

How to specify it so it stays a controlled process

Compression should never be a packing-room decision made the day before the container arrives. Write it into the packing specification with measurable values:

  • Which styles are in and out — by style number, not by a general instruction.
  • Compression ratio or target carton CBM, and the bag type and valve used.
  • Fold pattern and where folds fall, so a crease is not placed across a patterned front panel.
  • Maximum time under compression before unpacking, especially for summer transits.
  • A recovery test on a real sample: unpack, air for 24 to 48 hours, light steam, then compare loft, hand and creasing against the gold seal sample. A wash test on the same sample tells you whether any flattening is permanent.

Keep the approved compressed-and-recovered sample as the reference, exactly as you would an approved colour standard. Without it, "it looked fine before packing" is the only evidence anyone has.

When it is not worth it

For small orders, air freight, luxury programmes or anything already shipping garments on hanger, the saving rarely justifies the risk. GOH is the opposite philosophy — pay for volume to avoid folding at all — and it is the right answer for structured or high-value knits. In between, the honest calculation is simple: compare the CBM saving against the cost of one extra return or one downgraded wholesale claim, using the same landed cost discipline you apply to the freight itself.

Our position at the factory is unglamorous: compress the dense, everyday styles where the cube is genuinely wasted, and leave the lofty ones alone. That split captures most of the freight saving without putting the product at risk.

Frequently asked questions

Will sweaters recover their loft after vacuum packing?

Dense, fine-gauge knits in cotton, acrylic or viscose usually recover after 24 to 48 hours of airing with light steam. Chunky, brushed, raised, chenille and cashmere styles often do not fully recover, and hard fold lines pressed in under vacuum for a long transit can be permanent. The only reliable answer is a recovery test on a real sample: compress it, hold it, unpack it, air it and compare against the approved sample before you commit the order.

Does compression damage the fibres permanently?

The fibre itself is rarely damaged; what changes is the fabric structure and the finish. Loops pressed flat can stay flattened, brushed surfaces can mat, and a fold line held under pressure can behave like a set crease. Trims and embellishments can also leave imprints on the fabric face. That is why the exclusion list should be written by style number and why the approved sample needs to be a compressed-and-recovered one.

Is vacuum packing the same decision as garments on hanger?

They are opposite ends of the same trade-off. Vacuum packing minimises volume by folding and squeezing the garment, while garments on hanger deliberately uses more container space to avoid folding altogether. Choose compression for dense, everyday styles where cube is wasted, and GOH for structured, high-value or crease-sensitive knits where the product condition is worth more than the freight saving.
