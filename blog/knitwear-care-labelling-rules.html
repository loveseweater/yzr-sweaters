Knitwear Care Labels and Composition Labelling: Rules for the US, EU and UK

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

A knitwear care label is a legal document, not a design choice. Sweaters sold at retail generally must carry an accurate fibre composition, a country of origin and care instructions, and the EU and UK also require a responsible operator's name and address. The US, EU and UK each set their own rules, so one label design rarely covers all three markets — and a labelling failure can hold a shipment, force relabelling or trigger a withdrawal.

Buyers usually treat the care label as a printing job: send artwork, approve the proof, move on. In reality it is the one part of a sweater regulated by law in nearly every market where it sells, and a labelling error is one of the few defects a discount cannot fix.

Why are care labels a compliance issue, not a design choice?

A care label is a consumer-protection document. Customs, marketplace compliance teams and retail QC inspect it, usually late — at the port or after the listing goes live. By then every garment is cartoned, so corrections mean opening polybags, replacing labels and repacking. Responsibility usually falls on the importer placing the goods on the market.

What must appear on a knitwear care label?

Requirements differ by market, but four elements recur.

  • Fibre composition — fibres as percentages by weight, in descending order, using legally recognised fibre names.
  • Country of origin — where the garment was made.
  • Care instructions — how to wash, dry, bleach, iron and professionally clean it.
  • Operator identity — in the EU and UK, the name and address of the manufacturer or the importer established in that market.

Details change periodically, so confirm the current requirement per destination with your lab or customs broker.

What do US labelling rules require?

The US regulates apparel labelling mainly through the Federal Trade Commission (FTC).

  • Fibre content. The Textile Fiber Products Identification Act (TFPIA) generally covers most apparel; wool sweaters typically fall under the Wool Products Labeling Act (WPLA). Both require approved fibre names and percentages.
  • Country of origin. Imported garments must generally be marked so the final purchaser can identify where they were made.
  • Care instructions. The FTC's Care Labeling Rule typically requires an instruction that will not harm the garment if followed.
  • "Made in USA". Strict: an unqualified claim is generally reserved for goods made almost entirely in the US, so imported yarn breaks it.

Confirm the current position with your testing lab or customs broker.

What do EU labelling rules require?

Fibre composition rules are harmonised across the EU: the textile fibre names regulation (Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011) sets out which names may be used and how composition is marked.

  • Approved fibre names only, as percentages by weight.
  • Language. Labelling must generally be in the official language of the member state where the product is sold, so an English-only label fails in France or Germany.
  • Responsible operator. The manufacturer's or EU importer's name and address must appear on the label.
  • Care information is widely expressed with GINETEX symbols.

Ecodesign and digital product passport measures are expected to add declaration duties, so watch the EU's emerging sustainability requirements for knitwear.

Is UK labelling identical to the EU's after Brexit?

Substantially similar but administered separately. The UK retained its own textile labelling regulations on the same framework, requiring fibre names, composition and a UK responsible operator identity. An EU label may not carry the correct UK operator details, so treat the UK as a separate version and confirm the current rule.

How are care symbols read?

Two symbol families cover most of the world: the GINETEX/ISO system (ISO 3758) used widely in Europe, and the ASTM guide in the US. Both read left to right: wash, bleach, dry, iron, professional care.

  • Wash — tub. A number inside is the maximum temperature; a bar means a milder cycle; a hand means hand wash.
  • Bleach — triangle. A cross means do not bleach.
  • Dry — square. A circle means tumble drying, with dots for heat; lines mean flat drying.
  • Iron — iron shape. One, two or three dots for low, medium and high heat.
  • Professional care — circle. P means dry cleaning with listed solvents, F a petroleum-solvent process, W wet cleaning.

Symbols must describe what the garment can survive: a tumble-dry symbol on wool is a returns problem in the label.

Where do knitwear labels most often go wrong?

  • Composition copied from an earlier style, so it no longer matches the yarn — only a lab fibre analysis checks this; see sweater lab testing.
  • Rounded blend percentages, or naming a trace fibre as if it were significant.
  • English only on goods sold in an EU market requiring the local language.
  • Fibre names used loosely. "Lambswool" means wool from a first shearing, not a substitute for the regulated name "wool"; cashmere and mohair may only be named when the fibre meets the definition.
  • Symbols that contradict the fabric — a tumble-dry symbol on wool that felts.
  • Missing origin or operator address, and labels that fade too early.

How should buyers control labelling compliance?

  • Write the exact label text into the tech pack, one version per market. Labels are ordered as trims on the same lead time — see our trims and labels guide.
  • Take composition from the yarn supplier's records.
  • Test before bulk. A fibre composition analysis and a wash test evidence the content and the printed instructions.
  • Approve a labelled sample in the final language, carrying your own name and address as importer.

Frequently asked questions

Does every sweater need a care label, even if it is dry clean only?

Generally yes. Markets that require care information usually require it regardless of how the garment is cleaned, so a dry-clean-only sweater still needs a label telling the consumer what to do — in that case, a professional-care symbol or wording rather than a blank label. Some markets also require the instruction to cover at least one safe cleaning method.

Can one label design serve the US, EU and UK at the same time?

Sometimes, but it is rarely the cheapest route. The three markets differ on required fibre names, language, operator identity and origin marking, so a single label often ends up carrying more text than each market needs, or missing something one of them requires. Most buyers run one label version per destination and keep the artwork on file.

Who is responsible if the fibre composition on the label is wrong?

The party placing the product on the market — normally the brand or importer — carries the responsibility, even if a supplier drafted the wording. In practice the supplier should be able to document the composition from yarn records and lab testing. Keep those records, because if a composition claim is challenged, the paperwork is what settles it.
