Shipping Sweaters by Sea: Container, LCL and Packing Essentials

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Knitwear is light and bulky, and that single fact shapes every decision in sea freight. A container of sweaters generally weighs far less than the same container filled with denim or footwear, so you pay for space rather than weight. How FCL, LCL, carton planning and container choice interact is what turns a freight quote into a predictable landed cost.

Why sweater freight is priced on volume

Carriers price general cargo on whichever is greater: actual weight or a volumetric figure derived from carton dimensions. For garments the volumetric figure usually wins, which is why knitwear is described as light cargo. Shaving grams off a garment barely moves your freight cost; removing air from the carton does. Cube is what you buy at sea.

FCL or LCL: how buyers choose

  • FCL generally wins as volume grows. Once a shipment approaches roughly two-thirds of a container's usable capacity, all-in FCL cost often comes close to LCL while giving you a sealed box and fewer handling points.
  • LCL suits testing and topping up — first orders, new colourways and replenishment between main shipments, because you are not paying for space you do not fill.
  • LCL carries fixed charges. Consolidation, terminal handling and documentation fees apply per shipment rather than per cubic metre, so on small volumes they can dominate the total.
  • LCL means more touches at consolidation, the terminal and destination, so crush and moisture risk tend to run higher.

Container types and what a knitwear order fills

Three types cover most garment shipments. The volumes below are industry-typical and close enough for planning, though exact dimensions vary by carrier — confirm them on your booking.

  • 20GP — around 33 cubic metres of internal volume.
  • 40GP — around 67 cubic metres; the standard workhorse for larger apparel shipments.
  • 40HQ — around 76 cubic metres thanks to extra internal height. For knitwear this is often the default, because the added height brings cube without much weight.

Sweaters are compressible, so a 40HQ normally cubes out long before it nears its weight limit. Carton dimensions, not garment weight, are what to optimise.

Loading and packing points that matter

  • Consistent carton sizes stack tightly; mixed sizes create voids you still pay for.
  • Board grade must hold a tall column through weeks of vibration — the bottom layer carries the load.
  • Floor load or palletise. Floor loading maximises cube; pallets speed handling but consume height.
  • Compression control. Over-stacking soft cargo can press hard fold lines that customers read as damage; sensible stack heights and corner boards help more than extra strapping.
  • Moisture plan. Containers can sweat internally on long routes; desiccant, liners and dry cartons at loading are the usual defences.
  • Marks and labels must match the packing list exactly — shipper, consignee, carton number, size and colour assortment, port of discharge.
  • Wood packaging generally falls under ISPM 15 rules; pallets and dunnage should be heat-treated or fumigated and stamped.

Documents that travel with the cargo

  • Bill of lading — carrier's receipt, contract of carriage, and in negotiable form a document of title.
  • Packing list — carton count, contents, weights and dimensions.
  • Commercial invoice — value, trade terms and HS codes, which drive duty assessment.
  • Certificate of origin — where a trade agreement or your buyer requires it.
  • Compliance records — Oeko-Tex certificates, BSCI / SEDEX reports or an inspection report.

Requirements differ by destination and change over time. Treat this as general orientation and confirm the document set with your forwarder and a destination customs broker.

Sea, air or express

Sea is the default for knitwear: lowest cost per unit at volume. Air freight suits urgent replenishment or high-value fine-gauge pieces where the margin absorbs the premium. Express courier works for samples and small test drops but gets expensive fast, and it is charged on volumetric weight too, so a bulky sweater carton pays heavily. A practical rule: if the cost of arriving late exceeds the air premium, fly it; if not, wait for the vessel.

Delays and damage: the usual causes

  • Document problems — an invoice or packing list that disagrees with the bill of lading, wrong HS classification or a missing certificate. Inconsistency tends to trigger holds.
  • Missed cut-offs — cargo arriving after the documentation or gate cut-off rarely waits politely for the next sailing; it rolls.
  • Peak-season space — bookings are frequently rolled ahead of major retail seasons. Book early and track the booking, not only production.
  • Crush and water damage — over-stacking, weak cartons, LCL re-handling, container sweat or a worn door seal.

Most of it is pre-emptable: agree carton spec and stack plan, photograph cartons and the container at loading, keep documents consistent, and leave margin for a roll.
