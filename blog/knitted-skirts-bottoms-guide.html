Knitted Skirts and Bottoms: An Overlooked Category for Buyers

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

Knitted skirts and knitted bottoms are usually the smallest part of a knitwear range and one of the most profitable per unit, because they extend the same yarn and colour palette into a second product and encourage a co-ord purchase. The catch is construction: a knitted skirt must recover its shape after sitting, and a knitted trouser must not sag — so fabric weight, elasticity and lining decisions matter far more than they do on a sweater.

Most knitwear ranges are built from the top down: sweaters, cardigans, maybe a dress. Bottoms are an afterthought, if they appear at all — and yet they are one of the easiest ways to increase the average order value. A skirt knitted from the same yarn as a sweater creates an obvious outfit, and the same development work serves two products. What stops brands is usually not demand but doubt about whether a knitted bottom will behave: will it bag at the seat, sag at the hem, or lose its shape by lunchtime?

Why is this category under-used?

Two reasons, mostly. The first is that buyers worry a knitted bottom will look and feel like loungewear rather than a daywear piece — which is true when it is made from the wrong fabric, and false when the weight and structure are chosen properly. The second is that knitted bottoms expose construction quality that a loose sweater conceals: a skirt shows every curve, and a sagging seat is immediately visible.

The brands that do this well treat the bottom as its own product with its own requirements, rather than as an accessory to the sweater. That means accepting that it may need a different yarn, a firmer gauge and a different weight than the top it is merchandised with — even if the colour matches exactly.

What are the main knitted bottom styles?

  • Pencil and straight skirts. The closest to a woven skirt silhouette. They need enough body and elasticity to hold the line; a soft, drapey yarn will cling and show every seam.
  • A-line and swing skirts. More forgiving, flattering and easier to produce, because the fabric is not under tension. A good first knitted bottom for a new range.
  • Midi and maxi lengths. Weight matters enormously here — a heavy skirt stretches under its own mass, so these need either a firmer knit or a lighter yarn at greater density.
  • Knit trousers and wide-leg styles. Widely sold, but the hardest to get right. They need a dense, elastic structure and often a drawcord or fitted waistband to stay up.
  • Co-ords and twin sets. A top and bottom in the same yarn and colour, sold together. The strongest commercial argument for the category, since the set prices higher than the two pieces would separately.

What construction issues should a buyer expect?

  • Bagging and recovery. The single biggest issue. Ribbed or elasticated structures recover better than loose plain knit; a skirt with poor recovery will be shapeless by the end of a working day. Test recovery by stretching the sample and checking whether it returns.
  • Waistband construction. Whether the waistband uses elastic, ribbing, a drawcord or a wide knit band changes both comfort and durability — and how much the skirt slides. Expect discussion on this point; it is not a detail to leave to the factory.
  • Lining and opacity. Fine-gauge knits and lighter colours can be semi-transparent when worn, so the sample must be assessed on a body in daylight, not flat on a table. Some styles need a lining or a denser knit rather than an added underlayer.
  • Length stability. A knitted skirt can settle longer after hanging or wearing. Confirm the measurement point (relaxed versus hung) and how much length change is acceptable — the same relaxed-measurement principle covered in our guide to shrinkage control.
  • Movement at the hem and slit. Slits and vents relieve a tight skirt but also concentrate stress; the hem finish needs to be secure, which is where linking quality shows.

How should a buyer specify a knitted bottom?

A skirt or trouser needs a slightly different specification from a sweater. The points worth stating explicitly:

  • Fabric weight and gauge — heavier and slightly firmer than the matching top, usually. Matching the sweater exactly is often a mistake.
  • Target stretch and recovery — how much the garment should stretch and what it should return to, agreed in numbers rather than described as "firm".
  • Waistband type and elastic specification — including whether the elastic must be replaceable or fully encased.
  • Lining or opacity requirement, with a clear instruction on how it will be checked.
  • Measurement chart including length points, with the relaxed method defined.
  • Fit sample on a body, not just flat measurements. A knitted bottom that measures correctly can still wear badly — our notes on reviewing a sample cover how to test this properly.

How does this fit into a range?

The commercial logic is straightforward: a bottom shares the yarn, palette and development already sunk into the knitwear range, so the incremental cost of adding one is modest compared with launching a new category from scratch. It also lifts order value, since a co-ord sells as a set.

A sensible starting point is one or two styles rather than a full range — an A-line midi and a straight or wide-leg trouser, in the season's strongest neutral — and letting sell-through data decide whether to expand. Our notes on range planning cover how to structure the mix, and on replenishment how to reorder once a style proves itself.

One caution on merchandising: knitted bottoms are usually bought as part of an outfit, so the styling and photography need to show them worn with the matching top. Sold without that context, they read as an unfamiliar category and sell slowly — which is often misread as weak demand rather than weak presentation. Frequently asked questions

Should a knitted skirt use the same yarn as the matching sweater?

Usually the same yarn in the same colour, but not necessarily the same gauge or weight. A skirt needs more body and better recovery than a loose sweater, so it is often knitted slightly denser or heavier. Matching the colour exactly while adjusting the structure is the normal approach.

What stops a knitted skirt from losing its shape?

Structure and recovery. Ribbed or elasticated structures hold their shape far better than a loose plain knit, and waistband construction decides whether the skirt stays in place. Ask for the recovery to be specified as a requirement and checked on a fit sample rather than assumed from the fabric description.

Are knitted trousers difficult to produce?

They are the hardest style in this category. A trouser carries more weight and more movement than a skirt, so it needs a dense, elastic structure and a waistband that reliably holds. If you are new to knitted bottoms, starting with a skirt or a co-ord is a lower-risk route to learning how the fabric behaves.
