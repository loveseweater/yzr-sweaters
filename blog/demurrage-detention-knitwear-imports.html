Demurrage and Detention: The Port Charges Nobody Budgeted For

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Demurrage is charged when a full container sits inside the port terminal beyond its free time. Detention is charged when the empty container is not returned to the carrier within its free time after being collected. Both are daily, usually tiered, and billed to the party named on the bill of lading — which under FOB is the buyer. Most cases we see are not caused by the factory but by late documents, a customs exam, or a warehouse that could not take delivery.

A buyer once sent us a USD 1,800 charge for a container of cotton sweaters that had been produced and shipped on time. Nothing had gone wrong at our end, and nothing had gone wrong at sea — the consignee's warehouse had no delivery slot for nine days. We see this a lot with first-time importers, because the freight quote lists the ocean rate and the origin charges, while the daily charges at destination live in a tariff nobody reads. This is the part of landed cost that only shows up after the invoice.

Two clocks, two charges

Rates are per container per day and commonly escalate in tiers — a lower rate for days 1–5, a higher one for days 6–10, higher again after that. Something in the range of USD 75–150 per container per day is normal on many lanes, more for reefer or oversized equipment, so ten days of delay can quietly cost more than the inland haulage. Ask your forwarder for the written free-time terms and the tariff before the vessel sails, not after the charge lands. A good forwarder will give you both without being asked.

Why knitwear shipments get caught

  • Paperwork that arrives late. The original bill of lading still in transit, a telex release not issued, or a mismatch between the commercial invoice and the packing list. Cargo cannot be released without the right document, and the demurrage clock does not care why — see shipping documents and customs clearance.
  • Customs exams and compliance holds. Apparel shipments can be pulled for fibre-content, origin or forced-labour documentation checks. Our UFLPA guide covers the paperwork that keeps a US shipment moving; a hold of a week is a demurrage bill of a week.
  • No delivery slot at the warehouse. The most common cause we see. Peak-season congestion at the receiving warehouse or a 3PL that requires a booked appointment turns a smooth arrival into nine days of storage.
  • Chassis, driver or depot shortage. The container is collected but the empty cannot be returned, which is detention rather than demurrage.
  • Rolled cargo at origin. If the container was rolled to a later sailing after it had already been gated in, free time at the load port can expire before it ever leaves.

LCL shipments have their own version of this problem: a consolidated container held because one other shipper's documents are missing delays everyone's goods. Our LCL guide explains why the saving on a small order can evaporate at the destination.

Who pays, and what insurance covers

The carrier bills whoever is named as consignee or notify party on the bill of lading, and the Incoterm decides whether that is you or your supplier. Under FOB or EXW — the normal terms for our orders — destination charges are the buyer's responsibility, even if the delay was caused by a document the factory sent late. Under DDP the seller carries them, which is one reason DDP quotes look expensive. Decide who owns the free-time risk in the supply contract, and make sure the consignee details on the booking are exactly right; a wrong consignee means a correction fee and another day on the clock.

Standard cargo insurance covers physical loss or damage to the goods, not a delay charge. Do not assume a demurrage invoice is claimable. Some policies and some carriers will consider a claim when the delay was caused by a covered peril, but routine congestion and late paperwork are not.

How to keep the clock from running

  • Pre-clear before arrival. Send the commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading draft and certificate of origin to your broker several days before the vessel berths. Most developed markets allow entry filing in advance.
  • Get free time in writing. Ask for the number of free days at both ends, whether demurrage and detention are combined, and the daily tariff. Request an extension before the free time expires — it is much cheaper to ask on day 3 than to appeal on day 9.
  • Book the delivery slot early. Confirm the warehouse appointment, the truck and the empty-return depot while the vessel is still at sea. If your 3PL cannot receive on the arrival date, that is a planning problem, not a freight problem.
  • Use a telex release when appropriate. Waiting for paper originals to arrive by courier is an avoidable week — the mechanism is explained in our documents guide.
  • Keep a delay log. Record the discharge date, release date, collection date and empty-return date. Disputed charges are much easier to challenge with dates than with an email thread.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between demurrage and detention?

Demurrage is charged when a full container stays inside the terminal beyond its free time. Detention is charged when the empty container is not returned to the carrier's depot within the allowed free time after collection. One is about the cargo sitting at the port, the other about the carrier's equipment sitting with you.

Who pays demurrage on an FOB knitwear shipment?

The buyer. Under FOB, EXW and similar terms, destination-side charges — including demurrage, detention, storage and clearance — belong to the importer, and the carrier bills the consignee named on the bill of lading. Check the Incoterm and the consignee details before the goods ship, because a correction at destination costs both a fee and a day.

Will cargo insurance cover demurrage charges?

Usually not. Standard cargo insurance covers physical loss of or damage to the goods, not delay-related charges. Congestion, late documents and a missing warehouse appointment are commercial delays. Ask your broker in writing if a specific cause is contested, but do not budget on the assumption that the charge is recoverable.
