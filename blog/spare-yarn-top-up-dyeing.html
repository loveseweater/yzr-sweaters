Spare Yarn and Top-Up Dyeing for Knitwear Brands

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Spare yarn is a small quantity of each colourway held back from production and kept by the brand or the factory. It does three jobs: it lets a customer's snag be repaired in a matching yarn, it makes a top-up dye possible when a reorder is too small to justify a full dye lot, and it gives the brand a physical reference if a colour is later questioned. The quantity is small — a few hundred grams per colourway is often enough — and the cost of not having it is disproportionate.

A customer snags a sleeve. The brand offers a repair, which is good service — and then discovers that the yarn cannot be bought in that exact colour any more, because the mill rotated the shade or the dye lot is gone. What should have been a five-minute mending job becomes either a replacement garment or a disappointed customer. Spare yarn is the cheapest insurance in a knitwear programme, and it is almost always skipped.

What is spare yarn for?

Three distinct uses, and each justifies a different amount.

Repair. A snag, a dropped stitch or a small hole can be mended invisibly if the right yarn is available. Our notes on repair services cover the programme side; the yarn is the prerequisite. Without it, a repair becomes a replacement.

Top-up dyeing. When a reorder is smaller than the dyehouse minimum, a small quantity of undyed yarn held from the original lot can be dyed to top up a shortfall without running a full lot. Our notes on yarn minimums explain why this matters on small repeats.

Colour reference. A retained hank in the original lot is the physical answer if a customer or retailer questions a shade six months later. Our notes on colour consistency explain why a lot-matched reference beats a code.

How much should be kept?

The honest answer is that it depends on the programme, but the quantities are smaller than buyers expect.

  • For repair: a few hundred grams per colourway covers a meaningful number of small repairs. A single sleeve repair uses a few metres of yarn, so even 200 grams is a large reserve.
  • For top-up dyeing: enough to cover the gap between a plausible reorder and the dyehouse minimum. That is a judgement about likely repeat volume, so it comes from the sales plan rather than from a formula.
  • For reference: a single hank or cone is enough, provided it is stored properly and labelled with the lot.

The practical approach is to set aside a fixed small percentage of the yarn order — often expressed as a wastage allowance above what production consumes — and treat it as a separate line rather than as part of production. Our notes on yarn consumption explain how the production figure is derived, and yarn stock planning covers holding decisions.

Who should hold it?

The brand holds it if it offers its own repairs, sells spares, or expects to make top-up decisions quickly. The advantage is control; the disadvantage is that the brand has to store and track it.

The factory holds it if the brand has an ongoing relationship and the factory can top up a reorder directly. This is often more practical, because the factory already has the space and the lot records — but it depends on the relationship surviving, and on the factory still having the yarn when needed.

The reliable structure for a small brand is a combination: the factory keeps the bulk of the reserve against future orders, and the brand keeps a small repair quantity of its best-selling colourways. That way the repair kit is under the brand's control, and the top-up reserve sits where it can actually be used.

What goes wrong with spare yarn?

It is not labelled. A cone of oatmeal with no lot number is nearly useless six months later, because matching it to a specific production run becomes guesswork. Label every unit with the colour reference, the yarn specification and the lot.

It is stored badly. Wool absorbs moisture, attracts insects and yellows in poor conditions. Our notes on yellowing in storage and moth damage cover the two failure modes; a bag of yarn in a damp stockroom will not be usable when it is needed.

It is used for the wrong thing. Production staff topping up a bulk run from the repair reserve is a common failure — the reserve quietly disappears into production and is gone when a customer needs it. It has to be marked and treated as held stock.

Nobody knows it exists. A reserve that the customer service team does not know about does not get used. It belongs in the product record, not only in a warehouse bin.

Is top-up dyeing actually possible?

Sometimes, and it depends on three things being true at once.

The undyed yarn has to still exist in the same count and blend, usually greige or a base shade held from the original order.

The dyer has to accept a small lot. Many will not run a vessel below a minimum load at any price, because the constraint is equipment rather than economics. Where a small lot is possible it usually carries a surcharge. Our notes on yarn minimums cover the mechanism.

The resulting shade has to match closely enough. A top-up dye is a new dye lot, so it will sit inside a shade band rather than match exactly. That is fine for a repair or a mending reserve, and less fine for a reorder that will be merchandised next to the original. Our notes on working to Pantone and lab dips cover the approval route.

Where all three hold, a top-up is a genuinely useful option. Where the first fails — the base yarn is gone — the option does not exist, which is why the decision to hold greige has to be made at the original order.

How should a brand set this up?

  • Agree a spare-yarn quantity per colourway in the purchase order, as a separate line with its own price. Our notes on supply contracts cover where it belongs.
  • Decide who holds it, and write it down. Ambiguous custody means nobody has it when it is needed.
  • Label every unit with colour reference, yarn specification and lot number.
  • Store it properly — dry, sealed, pest-controlled — and treat it as held stock rather than production inventory.
  • Record it in the product file so customer service and the repair process know it exists. Our notes on the care card cover the customer-facing repair route.
  • Ask about greige retention at the original order if a top-up dye is plausible — that decision cannot be made retroactively.

Frequently asked questions

How much spare yarn should a brand keep?

Less than most buyers assume. A few hundred grams per colourway covers a large number of small repairs, because a single sleeve repair uses only a few metres. The larger question is the top-up reserve, which depends on likely repeat volume and on whether the dyer will accept a small lot. Both are set at the original order, not afterwards.

Can I re-dye yarn to match an old colour?

Only if the undyed base yarn still exists in the same count and blend, the dyer will run a small lot, and you accept that the result will be a new dye lot sitting inside a shade band rather than an exact match. All three conditions have to hold, and the first is the one that cannot be fixed later — if the base yarn is gone, no top-up is possible.

Should the factory or the brand hold the spare yarn?

Often both, in different proportions. The factory is best placed to hold a top-up reserve because it has the space and the lot records, while the brand should hold a small repair quantity of its best-selling colourways so that repair does not depend on the supplier relationship surviving. What matters most is that custody is written down and the yarn is labelled.
