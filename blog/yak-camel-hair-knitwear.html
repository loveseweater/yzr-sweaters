Yak and Camel Hair Knitwear: Fibres Beyond Merino

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Yak down and camel hair are the two fibres most often pitched as "cashmere alternatives" to buyers who want a story without a cashmere price. Both are genuine speciality fibres with real properties — yak is warmer for its weight than merino and comes in natural browns and greys, camel hair is soft, light and warm with a distinctive natural tan. Both are scarce, both have limited colour flexibility, and both need a fibre-savvy spinner rather than a general mill.

Once a brand has worked through merino, lambswool and cashmere, the next question is what else exists. Yak and camel hair are the two answers that come up, usually with the same vague brief: softer than merino, cheaper than cashmere, and with a story. The brief is right about the story and partly right about the properties. It is wrong about the price and the colour range, and those two facts decide whether the fibre is viable.

What is yak down, and why is it not just "yak wool"?

A yak has a coarse outer coat and a fine down undercoat. The outer guard hair is long and strong, used for rope and coarse textiles; the down is the soft insulating layer grown for winter. Knitwear uses the down, combed out in spring, so "yak wool" in marketing almost always means yak down.

The down is fine — typically in the same range as good cashmere rather than merino — and measurably warmer for its weight than wool at comparable thickness because of how it traps air. Our notes on how knitwear keeps you warm explain that mechanism.

The colour is the other distinctive property. Yak down grows naturally in browns, greys and a near-black, so undyed garments come in warm neutrals a dyed merino cannot imitate convincingly. That is both an asset for a natural-colour story and a constraint for a brand needing a specific Pantone reference.

What is camel hair, and where does it come from?

Camel hair is the soft undercoat of the Bactrian camel, collected during the spring moult. Like yak, it has a coarse outer coat and a fine undercoat, and only the undercoat goes into knitwear. The fibre is soft, light, warm for its weight and naturally tan to light brown, which is why the colour name "camel" exists in the first place.

It is genuinely scarce. Annual global supply is small compared with wool or cashmere, most of it comes from a limited number of regions in Central Asia and China, and the dehairing process — separating the fine undercoat from the guard hair — is a significant part of the cost. Any quote for camel hair at close to cashmere prices is worth questioning on grade and actual percentage.

Both fibres are covered by the same regulatory framework as wool for labelling and composition, which our notes on care and composition labelling set out. Naming a fibre correctly on a label is a legal requirement, and "cashmere blend" containing yak is not a labelling grey area.

How do they compare with merino and cashmere?

  • Warmth for weight. Yak and camel hair are both warmer than merino at the same fabric weight, and closer to cashmere. That makes them attractive for lightweight winter knits where bulk is unwanted.
  • Hand feel. Yak down is soft with a slight dry handle; camel hair is softer and more supple than its coarseness might suggest. Neither feels like merino — merino has a crimp and loft that these fibres lack, which our notes on hand feel and micron explain in fibre terms.
  • Drape. Both drape more heavily than merino, which suits relaxed silhouettes and less structured styles. A crisp tailored shape is better served by a wool with more body.
  • Durability. Yak and camel hair are strong fibres, but their lack of crimp means they can felt and pill differently from wool. Blending with a small proportion of wool or a synthetic is common for exactly this reason, and our notes on preventing pilling cover the mechanism.
  • Price. Both sit between good merino and cashmere, with camel hair usually dearer because of scarcity and dehairing. Our notes on cashmere grades explain the grading logic that applies to speciality fibres generally.

Our notes on alpaca and mohair cover the two fibres buyers usually compare against, and the sweater yarn guide places all of them in the wider fibre landscape.

What are the practical constraints?

Colour range. This is the constraint that catches buyers most often. Undyed yak and camel hair are beautiful and limited; dyeing them is possible but the natural base tone shifts every colour, so a bleached white or a bright shade behaves differently from the same dye on wool.

Supply and minimums. Both fibres are scarce and come through specialist traders. Yarn minimums are higher than for standard wool counts, and the availability of a specific count in a specific colour can be genuinely limited — the constraint our notes on yarn minimums describe in general terms.

Spinning and price. These fibres need a spinner experienced with short, fine down fibres; a mill that handles wool well may not handle yak down well, and the yarn quality shows in the fabric. Scarce fibres also move in price more sharply than commodity wool, so a programme costed early in the year may not hold — our notes on price volatility explain the pattern.

How should a buyer specify them?

  • Name the fibre precisely. "Yak down", "camel hair", and the exact percentage in the blend. A vague "speciality fibre blend" on a spec sheet is unverifiable and a labelling risk.
  • State whether the colour is natural or dyed. The two are different products with different colour behaviour, and a buyer who assumed natural will be surprised by a dye lot.
  • Ask for the dehaired fibre content, not just the blend. A yarn described as camel hair that is half guard hair will feel very different from a properly dehaired one.
  • Approve on a knitted swatch. These fibres behave differently in the knit than in the yarn. Our notes on sample types explain where a knitdown fits.
  • Test pilling and shrinkage, and plan colourway count against availability — four colours in yak may not be achievable at a small order size.

Are they a good choice for your range?

Yes, in specific circumstances. Yak suits a brand with a natural-colour, low-dye aesthetic and a customer who values warmth without bulk. Camel hair suits a premium positioning where the fibre story carries weight and the price point can absorb it. Both suit relaxed, drapey silhouettes better than structured tailoring.

They are a poor choice when the range needs a tight colour match across many styles, when the order is small enough that fibre minimums bind, or when the target price is closer to merino than to cashmere. In those cases a wool-cashmere or wool-alpaca blend delivers much of the feel with far more flexibility — the substitution logic our notes on yarn substitution describe.

Frequently asked questions

Is yak wool warmer than merino?

Yes, for the same fabric weight. Yak down is a finer fibre that traps air efficiently, so a yak knit at a given thickness insulates more than merino at that thickness. It is closer to cashmere in warmth than to wool, which is why it suits lightweight winter knits where bulk is not wanted.

Can yak and camel hair be dyed?

They can, but the natural base colour influences the result. Yak down comes in browns, greys and near-black, and camel hair is naturally tan, so a dye applied over a dark natural base gives a different and usually deeper result than the same dye on bleached wool. Buyers wanting a precise colour match across a range should expect a compromise or a blend.

Why does camel hair cost so much?

Scarcity and processing. The global supply of camel undercoat is small, and separating the fine down from the coarse guard hair is a costly dehairing step that a significant share of the raw material does not survive. A quote that looks cheap for camel hair is worth questioning on grade and actual percentage in the blend.
