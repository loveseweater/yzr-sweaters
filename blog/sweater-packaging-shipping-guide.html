Sweater Packaging & Shipping: From Factory to Your Door

Views: 0

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

A knitwear order is not finished when the last stitch is sewn. How sweaters are folded, bagged, boxed and moved decides whether they arrive sellable, and it often decides whether your landed cost works. This guide walks through the packaging and shipping choices a buyer actually controls, from polybag to port, so you can plan the last mile of your order with fewer surprises.

Start with the packaging your market needs

Packaging serves two customers: your end buyer and your own warehouse. Decide the level of finish before production, because it affects packing time, cost and how the carton is planned.

  • Polybag (flat or hanger): the standard export packing. A clear polybag protects the garment, holds the fold and carries your barcode sticker. Low cost, high volume.
  • Hangtag and label: brand tag, care label and country-of-origin label. Simple, but required by many retailers and customs regimes.
  • Retail-ready gift box or sleeve: for direct-to-consumer and premium brands. Adds cost and bulk, but raises perceived value.
  • Eco options: recycled or biodegradable polybags, paper bands and FSC cartons for brands selling on sustainability.

If you sell on marketplaces, confirm whether the platform or retailer requires polybag suffocation warnings, barcodes or specific label placement — those details are cheap to add at packing time and expensive to fix later.

Folding and crease control

Knitwear creases differently from woven fabric. A sweater folded tightly and left compressed in a hot container can hold a hard fold line that customers read as damage. Good factories fold to a consistent template, keep bulk down without pressing hard creases, and use tissue or a light insert on fine or delicate knits. Cable and chunky styles tolerate folding better than fine-gauge cashmere, which benefits from lighter compression and flatter stacking.

Carton packing and carton planning

The carton is where small decisions add up. A sensible export packing plan covers:

  • Carton size and strength: matched to the folded garment so there is no wasted air, with a board grade strong enough to stack.
  • Pieces per carton and assortment: solid colour/size cartons or assorted cartons, depending on how you pick and ship.
  • Inner packing list: a label on the outside and a list inside so a carton can be identified without opening it.
  • Moisture protection: a liner or desiccant where humidity is a risk on long sea routes.

Good carton planning reduces dimensional weight, which is often the single biggest lever on freight cost. Ask for the packed carton dimensions and weights before you book freight, not after.

Choosing sea, air or express

Each mode fits a different stage of your business.

  • Sea freight: lowest cost per unit for bulk orders. Slower, and needs planning around production and sailing schedules. Best for planned replenishment and volume ranges.
  • Air freight: faster than sea at a higher cost, useful for mid-season fills and heavier orders that still need speed.
  • Express courier: fastest for samples, small batches and urgent top-ups. High per-kilo cost, so it suits low weight and high value.

A common pattern for new buyers is express or air for the first sample and initial trial order, then sea for repeat bulk. Sweaters are bulky for their weight, so volumetric weight matters — compare quoted chargeable weight, not just actual kilos.

Export documents and clearance

Clearance problems delay goods and cost money, so agree documents with your supplier early. A typical knitwear export set includes a commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading or airway bill, and a certificate of origin where a trade agreement or buyer requires one. Depending on the destination and your product, you may also need fibre-content and care labelling that meet local rules. Your forwarder or customs broker can confirm what applies to your market; the key is to have labels and documents correct at the factory, because reworking them after export is slow and costly.

Reducing damage and cost

Most transit damage traces back to packing, not handling. Keep creases out with a consistent fold, protect against moisture on long routes, use cartons rated for the stack height, and avoid over-packing cartons past their safe weight. On cost, the levers are dimensional weight, container fill, consolidation of your order into fewer shipments, and choosing the right mode for the order's urgency.
