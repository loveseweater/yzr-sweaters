Why Your FOB Knitwear Price Moves: Export Rebates, Invoices and the Bits Buyers Never See

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

China's export rebate refunds domestic VAT paid on goods that are exported. The rate is set by product classification and can be revised, so it is a variable inside a factory's FOB cost calculation — not a discount offered to the buyer. That is why a quote can shift between seasons, why factories prefer to handle export declaration themselves, and why a supplier asking you for precise HS and consignee data is not being difficult. Do not try to claim a share of the rebate; do fix the price basis and the policy-change clause in your purchase order.

A buyer sends us a quote from six months ago and asks why the same style is now a few cents different. Nothing about the yarn or the gauge changed. The answer is often on the export side of the ledger, in a mechanism most import teams never see because it sits entirely inside the supplier's country. Understanding it will not lower your price, but it will make a few otherwise puzzling behaviours make sense — and it will help you spot a quote that is too cheap for honest reasons.

What an export rebate is, and what it is not

When a Chinese manufacturer buys yarn, dyeing, trims and services, domestic VAT is included in those costs. When the finished goods are exported, the tax system refunds part of that domestic tax through an export rebate. The rate depends on the product's customs classification and is adjusted by policy from time to time; we are deliberately not quoting a number here, because the applicable rate for your style and the date of your shipment are things your supplier must confirm at the time of quoting.

Four things it is not:

  • Not a subsidy to the buyer. It is a refund of tax already paid upstream, and it belongs to the exporting entity.
  • Not a negotiation lever. Asking for a share of the rebate is asking a supplier to give away part of its cost recovery; it usually just gets folded back into the unit price somewhere else.
  • Not a discount on duty in your country. Your import duty is a separate calculation on your side — see our HS codes and duties guide.
  • Not guaranteed. Classification disputes, documentation gaps or policy changes can delay or reduce it, which is why a factory quotes on an expected recovery, not a certainty.

In simplified terms, an FOB price is built from tax-inclusive input costs plus conversion and overhead, less the rebate the exporter expects to recover, plus the local charges of getting the goods onto the vessel. Our quotation guide breaks the visible lines down; this is one of the invisible ones behind them.

Why it shows up in your quote

Three behaviours that buyers often read as awkwardness are actually about the rebate:

  • The factory prefers FOB to EXW. The rebate is claimed through an export declaration made by the exporting entity. If you buy EXW and arrange the export yourself, the question of who is the exporter of record — and who can claim the refund — becomes complicated. A supplier that handles the export declaration keeps the paperwork chain intact and can price accordingly. Our Incoterms guide explains the other half of that decision.
  • They care a lot about the HS code and the invoice wording. The same classification that sets your import duty also determines the rebate category at origin. A supplier pushing back on a loose description like knitted garments is protecting a paper trail, not being pedantic. The shipping document set has to tell one consistent story from factory to destination.
  • A quote has a short validity window. Between yarn prices, exchange rates and policy, a factory that holds a price for six months is taking on risk it cannot see. Our notes on yarn price volatility and currency risk cover the other two variables in that same window.

There is also a less comfortable version of this story. A supplier offering an unusually low FOB price may be quoting on the assumption that it will not carry a fully compliant documentation chain — sometimes described loosely as buying export documents from a third party. That can work right up until it does not, and the failure tends to surface as a delayed shipment or a document inconsistency at your end. A price worth having is one whose paperwork you would be happy to show a broker.

The paperwork chain behind it

Without going into Chinese tax procedure, the logic is straightforward and worth knowing because it explains what your supplier will ask you for. To claim a rebate, the exporter needs a matched chain: proper VAT invoices from its upstream suppliers, an export declaration in its own name with the correct classification, the commercial invoice and packing list, and the foreign-exchange receipt against that shipment. Gaps in that chain — a missing supplier invoice, a declaration that does not match the invoice, a payment routed to a different entity — can hold up the refund.

This is why payment routing matters more than most buyers expect. Our payment terms guide covers the commercial side; the relevant point here is that the entity on the invoice, the entity receiving the funds and the entity making the export declaration should line up. A mismatch is a paperwork problem for the factory and, potentially, a clearance problem for you. If a supplier asks you to pay a different company than the one quoting, ask why before you send anything.

What buyers should actually do

None of this requires you to become an expert in Chinese tax. Five habits cover it:

  • Fix the price basis in the purchase order. State the Incoterm, the currency, what is included and what is not, and the validity period of the quote. Our supply contract guide is the right place for the full version.
  • Add a policy-change clause. If a rebate or tax change alters the cost basis mid-order, the PO should say how that is handled — a re-quote, a shared adjustment, or a fixed price the supplier honours. Ambiguity here becomes an argument at shipment time.
  • Keep the classification consistent across documents. Invoice, packing list, declaration and your own import entry should describe the same goods at the same level of detail.
  • Keep the payment chain clean. Pay the contracting entity, to the account named in the contract, and get any change in writing.
  • Judge the price as a landed cost. A cent or two on FOB is meaningless next to freight, duty and packing quality — our landed cost guide and the price factors breakdown are the better lenses.

And be sceptical of a quote that is dramatically below the others without a clear reason in yarn, gauge or volume. In our experience the explanation is usually a thinner spec, a different fibre content, or a documentation shortcut that will cost someone later.

Frequently asked questions

Is the export rebate a discount I can negotiate into my price?

No. It is a refund of domestic tax already paid on the exporter's inputs, and it belongs to the exporting entity. Asking for a share of it usually results in the same unit price with the cost moved elsewhere. What you can negotiate is the FOB price itself, and what you should fix in writing is the price basis, currency, validity period and how a policy change is handled.

Why does my factory prefer FOB when EXW looks cheaper?

Because the rebate is claimed through an export declaration made by the exporting entity, and EXW can leave that chain unclear. When the factory handles the declaration, the invoice, packing list and declaration stay consistent and the supplier can price with a known recovery. Compare the two quotes as a landed cost — the EXW saving often disappears once local charges and the document chain are included.

Why does the factory need the HS code and exact consignee details so early?

The product classification affects the rebate category at origin as well as the duty at destination, and the export declaration has to match the commercial invoice and packing list. Precise data early prevents an amended document set near cut-off. If a supplier is vague about classification, that is worth questioning — it may signal a documentation shortcut rather than a small administrative detail.
