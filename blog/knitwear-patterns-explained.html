Knitwear Patterns Explained: Cable, Jacquard and Intarsia

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

Knit patterns fall into two families: relief structures that create texture by moving stitches — cable, rib, waffle, pointelle — and colour structures that put multiple yarns into one fabric, such as jacquard, intarsia and stripes. Both raise yarn consumption and machine time compared with a plain knit, and both must be described precisely enough that a sample comes back as intended rather than as the knitter's interpretation.

Two sweaters can share the same silhouette, yarn and gauge and still sell at very different prices, because the pattern decides how much yarn goes in and how long the machine runs. Pattern is also where development most often goes wrong: a reference photo shows a raised cable, the factory reads "cable knit", and the sample arrives with a flat twist that was cheaper to produce. This article covers what the main structures actually are and what a buyer has to nail down before sampling.

What is the difference between a knit structure and a knit pattern?

The terms get used loosely. Structure is how stitches are formed and interlooped — the mechanics of the fabric. Pattern is the visible design those mechanics produce. A single structure can yield many patterns, and one pattern is often built from several structures across different panels of the same garment.

For buying purposes, the practical split is between structures that move yarn and structures that add yarn. Moving yarn within one colour changes texture; adding yarns in different colours changes surface design and is where cost climbs fastest.

Which patterns only move stitches within one colour?

These are the relief structures. They knit with a single yarn at a time and create depth by displacing stitches.

  • Cable — groups of stitches crossed over each other at intervals, producing a raised braid. The width of the cable and how often it crosses both change yarn consumption, which is why "cable knit" alone is not a specification.
  • Rib — alternating columns of knit and purl. Used for cuffs, hems and neckbands because it recovers, and used across a whole body when the fabric needs to hold shape.
  • Waffle and basketweave — blocks of knit and purl arranged in a grid, giving a cell-like surface with more bulk than plain knit.
  • Pointelle — deliberate eyelet holes forming a fine open pattern. Lighter and more delicate, with a different yarn path than an openwork lace panel produced by dropped stitches.
  • Fisherman and Aran-style combinations — panels of cable, rib and texture combined across the body, often with a distinctive centre panel. These are among the heaviest yarn consumers because texture packs fabric into the same width.
  • Seed and moss stitch — small alternating texture giving a granular surface, useful when a design needs interest without added weight.

How do multi-colour structures differ?

Colour structures are a different production problem, because more than one yarn is active in the same fabric.

  • Jacquard — two or more colours knitted across the full width, with floats carried behind. Efficient for repeating motifs and all-over patterns, and the standard route for Fair Isle and geometric designs. Floats need managing, since long floats can catch on jewellery or fingers.
  • Intarsia — separate yarn blocks in defined areas, each with its own yarn supply, so large motifs sit cleanly with no floats behind them. Used for a single large graphic, a chest motif or a panel design. It is the most labour-intensive of the colour structures and the hardest to scale.
  • Stripes — simple colour changes by row or block. Easy to knit, but stripe sequence, width and the number of colours must be specified, and yarn changes affect the reverse side.
  • Colour blocking — large flat areas of different colours, usually built as panels rather than as a knitted pattern, which keeps production simpler than intarsia while reading as a designed garment.

Why do patterns drive cost and yarn consumption?

Three things change when a pattern goes beyond plain knit, and all three show up in the quote:

  • Yarn consumption rises. Crossed cables and textured panels pull yarn sideways into the fabric, so the same silhouette needs more yarn than a plain version. Our overview of yarn consumption explains how this is estimated before quoting.
  • Machine time increases. Patterns knit more slowly than plain fabric, and intarsia and complex jacquards slow further — reducing effective capacity, which matters most in peak season. Our notes on factory capacity cover why machine days are the real constraint.
  • Damage risk goes up. More structure means more places for dropped stitches, uneven tension or panel mismatch, so inspection expectations should be agreed rather than assumed.

There is also a boundary beyond which a design stops being a knitting question. All-over printing, embroidered motifs and applied patches sit outside the knit itself and follow different cost and durability rules — our guide to embroidery and embellishment covers where those routes make more sense than a knitted pattern.

What must a buyer specify about a pattern?

A reference photo is not enough. To get a sample back as intended, specify:

  • A close-range photograph of the intended stitch structure, plus a name if you have one.
  • Scale — how wide the cable crosses, how large the motif repeats, or how wide the stripes are, in centimetres rather than "large" or "small".
  • Placement — all over, centre panel only, sleeves only, or body and sleeves combined. Placement changes yarn consumption significantly.
  • Colour references for every colour in a multi-colour design, with the intended order and repeat.
  • The reverse side — whether floats are acceptable, how long they may be, and whether the inside will be lined or left exposed.
  • A physical reference garment where possible: nothing communicates a raised cable or a soft jacquard as quickly as holding the original.

Where do patterned styles go wrong, and how do you avoid it?

The recurring failures are predictable. A pattern described by name alone gets interpreted by whoever programs the machine. Scale is left to the factory, so the cable is much wider or narrower than the mood board suggested. Colourwork is designed without checking float limits. And a textured style is costed as if it were plain knit, so the first quote looks competitive and the second one does not.

The fix is the same in each case: put structure, scale, placement and colours in the tech pack, send a reference garment, and ask the supplier to confirm yarn consumption and machine time before you approve the sample rather than after.

What should you expect from a supplier on patterned styles?

A supplier experienced with patterned knitwear will ask about the structure before quoting, tell you when a motif needs intarsia rather than jacquard, flag when a design substantially raises yarn use, and send stitch-level photographs with the sample so you can compare structure rather than just look at the whole garment.

Frequently asked questions

Is cable knit always more expensive than plain knit?

Almost always, because crossing stitches uses more yarn per unit of fabric and slows the machine. How much more depends on the cable width and how frequently it crosses, which is why a pattern described only as "cable knit" cannot be costed reliably. Specify the structure and scale so the impact can be estimated properly.

Should I choose jacquard or intarsia for a large logo?

For a single large motif, intarsia usually gives the cleaner result, because each colour block is knitted from its own yarn supply and no floats are carried behind the design. Jacquard suits repeating motifs and all-over patterns where the repeat is small enough to manage floats. Confirm the intended scale before the structure is chosen.

Can a pattern be changed after sampling without affecting cost?

Usually not. Changing structure, scale, placement or colour count after sampling normally changes yarn consumption and machine time, which means the quote and often the lead time change too. Decide the pattern before sampling and treat post-sample pattern changes as a new development round.
