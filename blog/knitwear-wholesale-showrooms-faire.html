How Wholesale Showrooms and B2B Marketplaces Work for Knitwear

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

Brands sell knitwear to retailers through five main routes: an in-house sales team, independent sales agencies, multi-brand showrooms, seasonal trade shows and market weeks, and online B2B wholesale marketplaces such as Faire, Joor and NuORDER. Each route carries a different cost structure, reach and order size, and each one passes a different set of pressures down to the supplier that knits the goods. A supplier who knows which channel a brand sells through can plan samples, MOQ and replenishment lead times around it.

If you sell knitwear wholesale, your route to retailers decides what you need from your supplier: how many samples, in how many colours, how early, and how fast a repeat order can be filled. This guide explains how the main wholesale channels work, how a season runs from sample to delivery, and where the pressure lands on the factory that knits the goods.

What are the main wholesale channels for selling knitwear?

There is no single best route. Most brands run two or three channels at once, and each one places a different set of demands on the supply chain.

  • In-house sales team — full control of the pitch, the pricing and the retail relationship, but salaries, travel and samples sit on the brand's own balance sheet.
  • Sales agency — an outside firm representing several non-competing brands in a region or category for a commission. It widens coverage quickly because the buyers are already known, but the salesperson's attention is shared across labels.
  • Multi-brand showroom — a permanent sales space in a wholesale district such as New York, Paris, London or Milan where several brands show samples side by side. Retailers visit by appointment, usually during market weeks, and order after seeing the goods in person. For knitwear, where hand feel and colour decide the sale, this is often the main channel.
  • Online B2B wholesale marketplace — a platform such as Faire, Joor or NuORDER that verifies retail buyers and takes orders all year. Entry is easier and trial orders can be small, which suits boutiques; in return the brand pays platform commission and works with lower average order values.
  • Trade show or market week — a fixed seasonal window in which retailers see the whole line once and order before it closes. For the supplier it creates a hard deadline weeks earlier, when the full sample line must be ready.

How does a wholesale season run, from sample to delivery?

The pattern is the same in every channel; only the timing moves. The brand builds a sample line and a price list, puts samples live in a showroom or on a platform, and retailers order during the selling window. The brand then aggregates orders by style, colour and size, starts bulk production, and ships so the goods arrive inside the retailer's receiving window — before the selling season, not during it. That deadline comes from the retailer, not the brand, which is why our sweater buying calendar is worth reading before a delivery date is agreed.

What does a wholesale channel demand from the knitwear supplier?

This is where wholesale reality reaches the factory floor.

  • Samples in every colourway — a showroom or a listing needs a sample in each colour a retailer can order, not one reference piece. Three to six colourways means three to six samples, photographed and ready at the same time, so sampling capacity becomes the bottleneck.
  • Order quantities split into small lots — trial orders placed through a platform turn a brand's purchasing lumpy. The factory is then asked to knit below its natural minimum order quantity (MOQ, the smallest run it will accept for a given style and yarn), unless several orders are consolidated. Our guide to sweater MOQ explains what sets that floor.
  • Delivery windows that cannot move — retailers plan floorsets months ahead, so a late sweater is a missed season and a markdown. The buffer has to be built in at the start, because it cannot be found at the end.
  • Continuous replenishment — selling continues after the season opens. Best-sellers are reordered in small batches and expected back on shelf quickly, which suits a supplier who keeps yarn records, holds spare capacity and can restart a style without a fresh sampling round; see our notes on knitwear inventory replenishment.

How can a brand and its supplier work better together?

Most friction in wholesale knitwear comes from information arriving late. Five habits prevent most of it.

  • Share the wholesale calendar before sampling starts — showroom dates, show dates and retailer receiving windows.
  • Order the full sample set in one round, with colours, sizes and trims approved together rather than piecemeal.
  • Reserve replenishment capacity in advance and agree a realistic minimum for repeat orders.
  • Fix repeat terms in writing: the shortest workable lead time and the smallest repeat quantity.
  • Keep the yarn map current — mill-stock yarns replenish faster than yarns dyed to order.

How do platform commissions and retailer terms squeeze the price?

Every channel takes a cut before the supplier is paid. A showroom or agency earns commission, a marketplace charges for the sales it generates, and the retailer expects payment terms rather than cash on delivery. Those costs come out of the brand's margin, which is why purchase price, MOQ and payment terms are negotiated together. If you ask a supplier to hold capacity or keep a yarn on file, that is a commitment they carry — put it in the same conversation.

Frequently asked questions

Do retailers buy directly from a showroom?

They place orders there, but the contract is with the brand, not the showroom. The showroom displays samples and collects orders on the brand's behalf, usually for a commission. Delivery, invoicing and quality responsibility stay with the brand.

How early should wholesale samples be ready?

Before the selling window opens, which in practice means weeks ahead of the showroom appointment or the marketplace launch date. They must cover every colourway a retailer can order, because a colour missing from the line cannot be sold.

Why do B2B marketplace orders arrive in small quantities?

Because the platform is designed for retailers to open with a trial order. Volume builds as each retailer reorders what sold, so the total can be large but reaches the factory as repeat batches rather than one bulk order. Agree the minimum repeat quantity and lead time before the season starts.
