Knitwear Supply Risk: Dual Sourcing and Continuity

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Most knitwear buyers run a single supplier and discover their exposure only when that supplier fails. Dual sourcing means qualifying a second factory before you need it, which costs a sample round and some goodwill rather than a season; holding yarn is often a better hedge than a second factory, because it removes the constraint that cannot be replaced quickly. The realistic target is not zero risk but a plan that says what you would do in week one, not week six.

Supply disruption is usually discussed as an abstract risk until it happens: a factory loses its main customer's slot to a bigger account, a fire stops production, a key yarn goes out of stock, or the supplier simply stops answering. The buyers who survive those events are rarely the ones who predicted them — they are the ones who had a qualified alternative and some material already secured.

What actually goes wrong in knitwear supply?

Five failure modes, and they differ in how recoverable they are.

  • Capacity displacement. A larger customer books the slot you expected — common in peak season, and the reason our notes on capacity booking treat slot reservation as a separate commitment.
  • Yarn unavailability. A mill discontinues a shade or a count. This is the one that cannot be fixed by switching factory, because the constraint is upstream. Our notes on yarn substitution cover what happens when it occurs.
  • Sudden closure or insolvency. A factory stops trading, sometimes with your goods inside it. Rare, and catastrophic when it happens.
  • Quality failure at scale. The bulk does not match the sample and has to be remade, consuming the capacity you needed for the next order.
  • Logistics disruption. Port congestion, a missed vessel or a customs hold — recoverable, and the one buyers plan for most often.

The pattern worth noticing: three of the five are upstream of the sewing floor, and only one is genuinely about the factory.

What does dual sourcing actually involve?

Not splitting every order in half. Dual sourcing in knitwear usually means having a second qualified factory that can take work if the first fails, without necessarily producing anything in a normal season.

Qualification is the work. The second factory has to see your spec, sample a style, and demonstrate it can produce to your standard. That is a real cost — a sample round and some technical time — and it is the price of having an alternative.

A small trial order is better than a promise. Placing one modest order with the second source proves the relationship and gives you a production history to reference.

Not every style needs two sources. Qualifying on one or two core styles is usually enough, because a substitute factory that can make your core crew neck can probably make the variations.

Two sources create consistency questions. Two factories will not produce identical garments, so the specification has to be tight enough that both can hit it. Our notes on quality standards documents and the bill of materials are what make that possible.

Why is holding yarn often the better hedge?

Because yarn is the constraint that cannot be improvised. A second factory can be found and qualified in weeks; a discontinued shade or a specific count cannot be recreated at all.

Holding yarn — or arranging for the mill to hold it — protects against three failures at once. It secures the colour for a reorder even if the mill rotates its range, it lets a top-up dye happen when a reorder is below the dyeing minimum, and it means a switch to a second factory does not require re-sourcing the material. Our notes on spare yarn and top-up dyeing cover the mechanics.

The trade-off is capital tied up in material and the risk of holding stock for a style that does not sell. That is why the decision belongs to the styles with a realistic repeat, not to the whole range.

How should a continuity plan be structured?

A useful plan is short and specific. It answers four questions.

Which styles are critical? The ones whose absence would cost real revenue or a retail relationship. Most ranges have two or three.

What is the single point of failure for each? Usually a specific yarn, a specific machine type or a specific factory. Naming it is most of the work.

What is the fallback, and how long does it take to activate? A qualified second factory takes days to activate if it is already qualified, and months if it is not. That difference is the whole argument for qualifying early.

What would you do in week one? The practical actions: who calls whom, what is already secured, what has to be re-planned. Our notes on supplier vetting cover how to assess a candidate.

How does a trading company change the risk picture?

A single factory and a trading company backed by several partner factories are not the same risk, even when the price is identical. The factory has one machine hall, one workforce and one set of customer commitments; if it takes a larger account or loses a machine, the work does not go anywhere else. A trading company can move a style to another partner without the buyer running a new search.

That is not automatically better — a trading company adds a layer, and the buyer loses some direct visibility of the floor. But it does mean the redundancy question has a different shape: instead of asking whether the supplier has a second site, you are asking whether the partner network is genuinely capable of the same work. Our notes on trading company versus factory cover how to tell the difference between real multi-factory capability and a broker with no capacity of its own.

What should a buyer do this season?

  • Name your single points of failure, per critical style. Write them down rather than carrying them in your head.
  • Qualify one second source on your core style, even if you place no order yet.
  • Place one small trial order with the second source so the relationship is real.
  • Secure the yarn for your repeat styles, and confirm whether the mill will hold it. Our notes on yarn minimums explain why a bespoke shade is more exposed than a stock one.
  • Ask your current supplier what their own contingency is for a machine breakdown or a yarn shortage. A supplier with a real answer is a different risk from one who has never thought about it.
  • Keep the specification portable. A complete tech pack and bill of materials is what lets a second factory pick up a style quickly — and it is also what protects you if the first one disappears.

Frequently asked questions

Is dual sourcing worth it for a small brand?

It is usually worth qualifying a second source on one or two core styles rather than on everything. The cost is a sample round and some technical time, which is small against losing a season. Placing one modest trial order proves the relationship, so the alternative exists when you need it rather than only on paper.

Which is the bigger risk: the factory or the yarn?

The yarn is usually the harder constraint. A second factory can be found and qualified in weeks, whereas a discontinued shade or a specific count cannot be recreated at all. That is why holding yarn for repeat styles is often a better hedge than a second factory, and why the two protections address different failures.

How do I make my specification portable to a second factory?

A complete tech pack plus a bill of materials that names the yarn, trims and components by specifier and quantity. That package is what lets a second factory pick up a style quickly, and it is also the thing that protects you if the first supplier disappears with the details. Our notes on the tech pack and bill of materials cover what each must contain.
