Sweater Pilling: Why It Happens and How to Prevent It

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Pilling is the complaint that reaches a buyer's inbox long after the goods have shipped, and by then the fix is expensive. The good news is that pilling is largely decided before a single garment is knitted — in the yarn, the twist and the structure. This guide explains what causes pills, how different fibres behave, and how to agree a pilling standard with your factory so the risk is managed instead of argued about.

What pilling actually is

A pill is not a defect that appears from nowhere. Every yarn has loose fibre ends at its surface. Under friction, those ends work their way out of the structure, migrate to the surface, and tangle into a small ball. Static charge helps the process along, which is why dry winter air and synthetic layers tend to make it worse. Once formed, a pill is held in place by long fibres still anchored in the knit, and over time many break off or shed. That is why a sweater can look fuzzy in week one and clearer by week six. Pilling is surface abrasion, not a sign that the yarn is bad or the factory was careless.

The factors that drive pilling

  • Fibre length and fineness — short staple fibres have more loose ends per metre and escape the yarn more easily. Fine fibres also tangle more readily than coarse ones.
  • Yarn twist — low-twist yarns feel soft and lofty but hold their fibres loosely. Higher twist binds fibres into the yarn and is one of the most effective anti-pilling levers.
  • Ply and yarn construction — two-ply yarns generally pill less than singles, because the folding traps loose ends inside the structure.
  • Knit density and gauge — a loose, open knit lets fibres move and abrade freely; a tighter structure holds them in place.
  • Abrasion in wear — underarm, side seam, cuff and where a bag strap crosses the body are the classic pill zones.
  • Washing and drying — agitation, hot water and tumble drying all increase surface friction.

How different fibres behave

Fibre choice sets the baseline. Cashmere is the fibre buyers most associate with pilling, and the reputation is only partly fair: cashmere fibres are short and fine, so a low-grade, short-staple cashmere will pill noticeably, while longer-fibre grades in a well-twisted yarn tend to settle down after a few wears. Wool has natural crimp and scales that help fibres grip each other, so wool pills tend to form and then shed. Acrylic is strong and abrasion-resistant, but its fibres are smooth and slip out of the yarn easily, and acrylic pills tend to stay attached instead of breaking off — which is why a cheap acrylic sweater can look tired after a few washes. Blends behave according to their component fibres and the yarn engineering behind them; a wool-acrylic or cotton-acrylic blend can be engineered to pill less than either fibre used carelessly on its own.

What a factory can control

  • Longer-staple and combed yarn — combing removes the short fibres that become pills in the first place.
  • Higher twist and two-ply construction — agreed at yarn specification stage, not after sampling.
  • Tighter stitch density — a denser structure restricts fibre migration and also improves shape retention.
  • Anti-pilling finishing — resin or polymer-based treatments and enzyme washing smooth and seal the fibre surface, reducing the rate at which loose ends escape.
  • Brushing and shearing — where a raised surface is part of the design, controlled brushing produces a more even, less pill-prone nap.

Our Dongguan production partners work through these levers before sampling, because pilling traced back to a yarn decision is a specification change, while the same problem found at final inspection is a rework bill.

What buyers can ask for in the spec

The most useful thing a buyer can do is not to demand "no pilling" — nothing in knitwear delivers that — but to write a measurable standard into the order: name the test method and the minimum grade. Random tumble pilling (the pilling box) and Martindale abrasion testing are the two methods most often quoted in apparel specifications, with results expressed as a grade on a 1–5 scale rather than a percentage. Buyers commonly set a minimum of around 3–4 for wool and cashmere blends, depending on fibre and end use. Test both the yarn and the finished garment, since finishing can change the result.

Advice to pass on to your customers

  • Turn the garment inside out before washing and use a mesh bag for anything delicate.
  • Use a wool or delicate cycle at low temperature with a gentle detergent, and avoid biological powders that break down protein fibres.
  • Skip the tumble dryer — dry flat, reshaped, away from direct heat.
  • Rest the garment between wears so fibres recover, and de-pill with a fabric comb rather than pulling pills off by hand.

Shipping care instructions that say this plainly does more for your review scores than most marketing copy, because it frames mild pilling as normal fibre behaviour rather than a manufacturing fault.
