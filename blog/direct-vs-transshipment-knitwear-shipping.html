Direct vs Transshipment Sailings: What a Hub Port Really Adds to a Knitwear Order

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

A direct sailing keeps your container on one vessel from the load port to the discharge port. A transshipment routing unloads it at a hub — Singapore, Hong Kong, Busan, Colombo, Dubai — and puts it on a second vessel, sometimes a third. It is usually cheaper and often the only service available to smaller destinations, but it adds a connection window: if the onward vessel is full or late, your container waits for the next one. On a seasonal knitwear order that can be the difference between making a launch and missing it. Read the routing on the booking, not just the transit days on the quote.

Two forwarders quote the same lane. One says 28 days, the other 34, and the cheaper one is the 34-day one. Buyers often assume the difference is a slower ship. Usually it is a hub. We see this a lot on orders going to secondary ports in Europe, Latin America and Africa, where no direct call exists — here is what the extra stop actually costs.

What the routing line on a booking means

A booking names a load port, a discharge port and, in between, the vessels that carry the box. A direct service keeps the container on one vessel between the two, though that vessel may still call at several ports along the way — direct means no transshipment, not no stops. A transshipment routing discharges the container at a hub, yards it for a few days, then loads it onto a feeder or a second mother vessel. A through bill of lading covers both legs under one document, which simplifies the paperwork but does not guarantee the connection.

The quote usually hides the connection risk

  • Quoted transit is port-to-port on the schedule, not door-to-door. Hub dwell, customs clearance and inland haulage sit on top; the clearance walkthrough covers the destination end.
  • A missed connection means waiting for the next onward vessel — often a week, sometimes two in peak season, which is exactly when blank sailings and rolled containers hit hardest.
  • Extra days in a box are extra humidity exposure. Longer total transit raises the chance of container rain and mould on a hygroscopic fibre like wool; see mould and mildew in knitwear shipments.
  • Every lift is another handling. More moves means more stress on the cartons, which is why carton transit testing earns its keep on a long routed shipment.
  • Waiting has a price. If the onward leg is delayed, storage and demurrage or detention can land on the importer regardless of whose fault it was.

When transshipment is the right answer anyway

None of this makes a hub routing a mistake. For secondary destinations with no direct call, it is the only service that exists. LCL consolidation inherently involves a hub because the container is built and broken down at a freight station. For cost-sensitive basics with a flexible delivery window, the saving is real. For a launch-critical order, we often split it: the bulk sails, the critical sizes fly — see staggered and split shipments and air freight versus sea. On Europe-bound cargo, rail freight is sometimes a shorter single-leg answer than a routed sea service. One thing a hub never does is change origin: a container that sits in Singapore for four days is still China-origin, which matters for trade remedy duties.

How to compare two quotes properly

  1. Ask for the full routing: load port, hub, discharge port, vessel names, and whether the B/L is a through B/L.
  2. Ask for the connection frequency at the hub — weekly, or several sailings a week. That number tells you what a missed connection costs.
  3. Add hub dwell, clearance and inland haulage to get a real door date, not a port date.
  4. Price a one-week slip against the freight saving. On a winter range the slip usually wins.
  5. Get cut-off dates in writing and book before the cartons are sealed; production lead times and choosing a forwarder cover that sequence.

What to write into the order

State the required arrival date, whether a direct service is required, who carries the cost of a rolled container, and that the forwarder must confirm the connection before loading — supply contract essentials has the wording. Under FOB the buyer nominates the vessel and chooses the routing, so the decision is yours; under CIF or DDP the seller does, so ask rather than assume. Incoterms decide who owns the schedule risk, not just the freight bill.

Frequently asked questions

Does transshipment change the country of origin?

No. Origin follows where the garment was substantially transformed, not the ports it passed through. A container that sits in Singapore for four days is still China-origin. That matters for duty rates and for preference claims.

Is a through bill of lading a guarantee the container will make the connection?

No. A through B/L is a single document covering both legs, which simplifies the paperwork, but the carrier can still roll the container to the next vessel. Ask for the connection frequency at the hub instead.

Should I pay more for a direct sailing?

If the order is seasonal or tied to a launch date, usually yes — a one-week slip can cost more than the freight difference. For basics with a flexible delivery window, transshipment is often the sensible choice.
