Garment Dyeing, Washing and Finishing: What Happens After Knitting

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Two sweaters can leave the same machine with the same yarn and still feel like different products. What separates them is what happens after knitting: dyeing, washing, softening, anti-pilling and pressing. These steps decide hand feel, shrinkage and cost more than most buyers expect.

Yarn dyeing vs garment dyeing

Yarn dyeing colours the yarn before it is knitted. The colour is locked in before the machine starts, and the panel comes off already in its final colour. It is the standard route for most sweater programmes and gives the most predictable colour from piece to piece.

Garment dyeing knits first in natural or lightly tinted yarn, then dyes the finished piece. Because the garment is dyed after making up, colour penetrates an already-formed shape, which tends to produce a softer, slightly washed character rather than a flat, uniform surface.

What each route gives you

  • Yarn dyeing — colour accuracy. Lab dips are approved on yarn, so bulk colour tends to match closely and repeat orders are easier to control.
  • Yarn dyeing — minimums. Dyed yarn usually carries a mill minimum per colour, which often sets the practical MOQ for a colourway.
  • Garment dyeing — softness and character. The dye bath relaxes the knit, so garment-dyed pieces often feel softer, and slight variation between pieces is normal on casual, washed-effect ranges.
  • Garment dyeing — risk. The garment shrinks, twists and relaxes in the bath, so measurements must allow for shrinkage and colour is harder to hold across large lots.

In practice, most sweater programmes are yarn dyed; garment dyeing appears where the soft, relaxed character is the point.

Washing: where softness and shrinkage are set

Washing after knitting does more than clean the piece. It relaxes the loop structure, softens the yarn and stabilises the fabric so the garment shrinks in the factory rather than in your customer's machine.

  • Soft wash. A gentle wash with softener to relax the knit and improve hand feel — the most common finish on basic sweaters.
  • Enzyme wash. Cellulase enzymes act on cotton and cellulosic fibres, removing surface fuzz for a smoother handle and less pilling.
  • Stone wash. Abrasive treatment for a worn, vintage surface, most often on cotton and cotton-blend casual knits.
  • Silicone or softener finish. Leaves a slick, conditioned feel — effective, but it can wash out over time.

Wash intensity trades against stability: a heavier wash gives a softer hand but tends to increase shrinkage.

Anti-pilling: the finishing step buyers rarely ask about

Pilling is the small balls of fibre that form where abrasion pulls loose fibres to the surface. It is influenced by fibre length, yarn twist, stitch density and how the garment is worn — one of the most common quality complaints on knitwear.

  • Enzyme treatment removes surface fibre on cotton and cellulosic blends, reducing the material available to pill.
  • Singeing or cropping burns or shears protruding fibre for a cleaner surface.
  • Resin or polymer finishes bind surface fibres and improve abrasion resistance on wool blends.
  • Better raw material remains the strongest lever: longer-staple fibre with adequate twist pills less than short-staple, loosely twisted yarn.

Anti-pilling adds a process step and therefore cost. On wool and cashmere blends it is usually worth specifying, and a pilling test on the finished sample is the only way to know what a customer will see after a month of wear.

Brushing and milling: building a surface

  • Brushing raises fibre with rotating brushes for a fuzzy, softer-looking face — common on lambswool and mohair blends.
  • Milling or fulling works wool fibres together with moisture and agitation to create a denser, felted surface — the basis of a boiled-wool look.
  • Peaching or sanding gives a light, sueded surface on cotton and blends.

These finishes change appearance and hand feel considerably, and they affect weight and dimension too: a brushed sweater is warmer and softer but may shed more, while a milled sweater is denser but less elastic.

Drying, pressing and setting the measurements

After washing, garments are dried and pressed to their final measurements: the piece is steamed, shaped on a form or frame and allowed to relax to size. Too much steam or pressure can flatten cables or leave shine marks on wool.

Because most knits relax after washing, factories generally work to a washed measurement standard rather than the unwashed sample.

What finishing means for your costs and specifications

  • Extra steps add cost. Enzyme washes, anti-pilling, brushing and milling each add processing, and premium finishes can be a meaningful share of unit cost.
  • Shrinkage must be planned. Garment dyeing and heavy washing change dimensions, so fit is set after finishing, not before.
  • Hand feel is a specification. If softness matters, ask for a finish sample rather than leaving it to the factory's default.
  • Colour consistency varies with route. Yarn dyeing repeats more exactly; garment dyeing is harder to hold batch to batch.

Why the same style feels different between suppliers

When two quotes for the same sketch come back at very different prices, the gap is often not the yarn but the finishing. One factory may use a simple soft wash and standard pressing; another may garment dye, enzyme wash and anti-pill, yet both call the result the same product name.
