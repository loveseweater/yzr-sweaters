Knitwear Seams and Stitch Types: What Buyers Should Specify

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

A knitted garment's seams decide whether it stretches with the wearer or fights them. Linking joins stitches loop to loop and preserves stretch, which is why it is used on shoulders and armholes of fully fashioned knitwear. Overlock is fast and strong but does not stretch much, so it suits cut-and-sew panels and non-critical joins. Coverstitch gives a flat, stretchy hem finish. The buyer who specifies only the seam's appearance and not its stretch behaviour gets a garment that looks right and pulls at the armhole.

Buyers scrutinise yarn, gauge and colour and then leave the seams to the factory, which is understandable — seams are the least visible part of the specification and the hardest to describe. They are also where a knitted garment most often fails in wear, because a seam that does not stretch with the knit becomes the thing the wearer feels every time they lift an arm.

Why are knitted seams different from woven seams?

Because the fabric itself stretches, and a seam does not unless it is built to. In a woven garment the seam and the cloth behave alike, so a straight stitch works. In a knitted garment the cloth extends considerably under modest load, so a seam using an inextensible stitch becomes a rigid line across a moving surface.

The consequence appears as a shoulder seam that pops when the wearer reaches forward, an armhole that feels tight despite generous measurements, or a side seam that puckers after the first wash. None of these are pattern faults; they are seam-and-fabric mismatches.

This is also why seam choice interacts with construction route. Our notes on fully fashioned and cut-and-sew construction explain how the route determines which seams are available, and linking quality covers the craft step that most fully fashioned knitwear depends on.

Which seam types will a buyer actually meet?

  • Linking. Stitches are joined loop to loop on a linking machine, so the seam inherits the fabric's stretch and sits flat with almost no bulk. It is the standard for shoulders, armholes, necklines and cuffs on fully fashioned knitwear. It is also the slowest and most skill-dependent option, which is why linking labour is a real cost line. Our notes on linking quality cover the craft step that most fully fashioned knitwear depends on.
  • Overlock (serging). An edge is wrapped with thread to prevent fraying while joining panels. Fast, strong and inexpensive, with moderate stretch. It is the workhorse of cut-and-sew knitwear and fine for side seams, but it leaves a raised ridge and is not the best choice where the seam will be against skin.
  • Coverstitch. Two needle lines with a looper thread covering the raw edge, producing a flat, stretchy finish. Common on hems, cuffs and neckbands where a clean finish on the inside matters and the seam must extend with the fabric.
  • Flatlock. A stitch that joins two panels with no raised seam allowance, used where a flat join is wanted for comfort or for a sporty look. It has good stretch and is common in performance and seamless-adjacent constructions.
  • French seam. The raw edges are enclosed inside a folded seam, giving a clean finish on both sides. Rare in knitwear because the fold adds bulk and limits stretch, but it appears in lightweight, loose-fitting pieces.

A single garment usually uses several: linked shoulders and armholes, coverstitched hems, overlocked side seams. The specification question is not which single seam to choose but which seam belongs at each join.

How does seam choice change stretch and comfort?

Three properties move together, and they are why the join matters as much as the stitch.

Stretch. Linked and flatlock seams extend with the fabric; overlocked seams extend much less. On a join that crosses a moving part of the body — shoulder, armhole, crotch on a knitted dress — an inextensible seam restricts movement and eventually fails.

Bulk. A linked seam is nearly flat; an overlocked seam has a ridge; a French seam doubles the fabric. Bulk matters at the neckline, where it can make a collar sit away from the body, and at the armhole, where it can create a visible line under a fitted sleeve. Our notes on sweater necklines cover how the neckline finish affects how the garment sits.

Comfort against skin. A linked or coverstitched seam presents a smooth surface; an overlocked seam presents a ridge of thread that can be felt on a garment worn without a layer beneath. Our notes on hand feel cover the yarn side of the same comfort question.

How do seams affect cost and lead time?

Linking is the most expensive seam per unit of length, because it is slow and requires a skilled operator. This is the main reason a fully fashioned garment costs more than a cut-and-sew one at the same yarn and gauge — the assembly, not the knitting, carries the difference.

Overlocking is fast and cheap by comparison, which is part of why cut-and-sew dominates volume price points. Coverstitching sits between the two: faster than linking, but needing its own machine setting per fabric.

Lead time is affected by machine availability rather than by the seam itself. A factory with limited linking capacity becomes a bottleneck at peak season, which is why a supplier who can link in-house has an advantage on fully fashioned programmes. What should a buyer specify about seams?

  • Name the seam type at each join, not just "seam as per sample". A short list — linked shoulders and armholes, overlocked sides, coverstitched hem — removes most ambiguity.
  • State whether the seam must stretch. A join that crosses a moving part needs a stretch-capable seam. Saying so explicitly is faster than discovering it in a fit session.
  • Specify the seam allowance and finish where it will be visible or felt. Our notes on quality standards documents explain where those details belong.
  • Check seams in the fit session, not only the fit. Ask the wearer to raise their arms and reach forward. A popping shoulder seam is visible immediately and cannot be assessed on a flat sample.
  • Include seam strength and appearance in the inspection standard. Our notes on AQL inspection explain how defect categories are set, and seam defects usually belong in the major category.
  • Watch the interaction with trims. A zip or a heavy button puts load on the seam beside it, which our notes on cardigan closures cover.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my sweater's shoulder seam pop when I raise my arms?

Almost always because the seam does not stretch with the fabric. An overlocked or straight-stitched shoulder on a knitted garment restricts movement, and the thread eventually gives or the seam puckers. A linked seam on the shoulder inherits the knit's stretch and solves it. It is a construction issue, not a sizing issue.

Is an overlocked seam worse than a linked seam?

Not worse — different. Overlock is fast, strong and appropriate for cut-and-sew side seams. It is a poor choice where the join must stretch or where the seam sits against skin, because it has limited give and leaves a ridge. The right question is which seam belongs at each join, not which seam is best overall.

Can I specify seams without visiting the factory?

Yes, for the type and placement — a written join-by-join list is enough for that. What you cannot verify remotely is execution quality, which is where third-party inspection helps. Our notes on AQL inspection explain how seam defects are assessed against a defined standard.
