Booking Knitwear Capacity: Cut-off Dates and Yarn

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

A knitwear delivery date is protected by booking things in the right order: the production slot first, then the yarn, then the trims, then the inspection. Each has a cut-off date, and missing one moves the whole sequence rather than just that item. The factory's slot is the scarcest of the four in peak season, and yarn is the one that most often fails quietly because a bespoke colour needs a dyeing minimum you did not plan for.

Buyers tend to think of an order as a single commitment made on the day the purchase order is signed. In a knitwear factory it is a chain of separate reservations, each with its own deadline, and the delivery date is only as solid as the weakest one. Understanding the sequence explains why a confirmed order can still slip, and why some buyers reliably get their goods on time while others are always chasing.

What actually gets booked, and in what order?

Four reservations, and the order matters because each depends on the previous one being real.

  • The production slot. Machine time on flat-bed knitting machines, plus the linking capacity that follows. This is the constraint that sells out first in a peak season.
  • The yarn. Either a stock colour drawn from a mill's held range, or a bespoke shade that has to be dyed — and a dyed shade carries a minimum quantity and its own lead time.
  • The trims. Labels, hangtags, buttons and packaging, each from a different supplier with its own artwork proofing and production run.
  • The inspection and freight slot. A third-party inspection booking, and a vessel booking if the delivery window is tight.

Our notes on factory capacity and peak season cover how slots are allocated, and the knitwear buying calendar places the sequence against the retail calendar.

What is a cut-off date, and why does missing one matter?

A cut-off is the last date by which a decision or a purchase must be made for the delivery date to remain achievable. It is not a deadline for the factory's convenience — it is arithmetic. If the yarn needs six weeks to dye and knit, and the vessel leaves in ten weeks, then the yarn decision has a four-week window, not ten.

What makes cut-offs harsh is that they do not compress. A late yarn decision does not shorten the dyeing time; it pushes the knitting back, which pushes linking back, which pushes inspection back. The delivery date moves by the same number of days plus whatever buffer was consumed.

Three cut-offs matter most in practice. The colour confirmation cut-off for a bespoke shade, because dyeing cannot start without it. The trim artwork cut-off, because woven labels and printed hangtags have their own production runs. The lab dip cut-off, because a dip takes time to approve and a rejection restarts it. Our notes on lab dips and colour approval cover the approval loop, and trims and labels covers the trim lead times. Why is yarn the reservation that fails quietly?

Because the garment quantity can clear the factory's minimum while the yarn does not clear the dyehouse's. A buyer orders 300 sweaters in a bespoke burgundy, the factory accepts the order, and then discovers the minimum dye quantity for that shade is far more yarn than 300 sweaters will consume.

This is not a factory failure — it is a separate threshold the buyer was never told about. Our notes on yarn minimums explain the mechanism in full. The practical consequence for booking is that the yarn reservation has to be checked at the colour-selection stage, not at the production stage, because by then the choice is expensive to change.

The other quiet failure is a yarn that exists but is going out of stock. A mill's held range changes seasonally, and a shade confirmed in spring may not be available for a delivery ordered in autumn. Confirming availability before committing the colour is the control, which our notes on yarn stock planning cover.

How does peak season change the arithmetic?

Two effects compound. Machine slots become scarce, so the production reservation has to be made earlier than the same order would need in a quiet month. And the trims and dyeing suppliers get busy at the same time as the garment factory, because everyone in the chain is serving the same retail calendar.

The result is that a peak-season order needs its cut-offs pulled forward much more than proportionally. Our notes on holiday and novelty knitwear cover the short-window categories where this is most acute.

What happens when an order changes after booking?

It usually costs the slot, not just the change. Adding a colour or a size range after the yarn is booked means a new yarn commitment, and cutting a style after the machine time is reserved leaves capacity the factory may not be able to resell in a tight season.

The practical consequence is that changes are cheapest before the cut-offs and progressively more expensive after each one. Our notes on changing a knitwear order cover what can be adjusted at each stage and what cannot, and the buying calendar explains the seasonal shape that makes the timing matter.

There is also a Chinese New Year effect that catches buyers who plan on calendar weeks rather than working weeks. Production stops for a period around the holiday and ramps back up slowly afterwards, so a February delivery planned without accounting for it is usually a March delivery.

How should a buyer manage the sequence?

  • Ask for the cut-off dates in writing when the order is confirmed, one per item: colour, yarn, trims, artwork, inspection booking. A factory that can produce that list has a real production plan.
  • Reserve the slot before finalising the design. A slot reservation against a style still being developed is normal practice for repeat buyers and is what protects the delivery window.
  • Confirm yarn availability at colour selection, including whether the shade is stock or needs dyeing and what the minimum is.
  • Order trims with the bulk purchase order or earlier, and approve artwork before yarn dyeing starts so nothing waits on a label decision.
  • Build the buffer at the front, not the end. Contingency spent on early colour and trim decisions is far more effective than hoping the last two weeks absorb a slip.
  • Book inspection when the packing date is known, not when the goods are ready. Our notes on pre-shipment inspection explain the lead time.

Frequently asked questions

How far in advance should I book knitwear production capacity?

It depends on the season. In a quiet month a slot can be confirmed a few weeks ahead; in the run-up to autumn delivery, capacity sells out months in advance and repeat buyers reserve slots before the design is finalised. The reliable approach is to ask the factory when their slot allocation closes for your target delivery month, and plan backwards from that date.

What is the difference between a production slot and a yarn booking?

A production slot reserves machine and linking time at the factory. A yarn booking reserves the material, which for a bespoke colour means committing to a dyeing minimum that is often larger than the garment order needs. They are separate reservations with separate cut-off dates, and a confirmed slot does not guarantee the yarn exists.

Why does my order slip even though the factory accepted it?

Usually because a downstream cut-off was missed — a colour confirmed late, a trim artwork approved after dyeing started, or a yarn that turned out to need a dyeing minimum. Accepting an order reserves the slot but does not compress the lead times of dyeing, trim making or inspection. The delivery date holds only if each reservation in the chain is made on time.
