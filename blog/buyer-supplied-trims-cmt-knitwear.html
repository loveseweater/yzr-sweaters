When the Buyer Supplies the Yarn or Trims: How CMT Knitwear Orders Work

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

A CMT (cut, make, trim) order means the buyer supplies some or all of the materials and pays the factory for processing. On knitwear that usually means buyer-owned yarn plus factory-sourced or buyer-supplied labels and packaging. It works well when the yarn is genuinely special; it costs more than buyers expect once wastage, incoming inspection, storage, shortage replacement and leftover returns are priced in. Put all of those in writing before the yarn ships.

We get this request most often from brands that have found a yarn nobody locally stocks, or from groups with a central fabric team that wants the same fibre across several supplier countries. The logic is sound. What we see a lot, though, is the order placed as if the only change were a lower unit price — and then a difficult conversation three weeks later about who owns 34 kg of leftover cones and who pays for a second dye lot. This is the part the tech pack usually leaves out.

What CMT means on a sweater order

Three arrangements get called CMT, and they are not the same:

  • Full package. The factory buys yarn, knits panels, links, washes, finishes, trims, packs. One price, one party responsible for everything. Most of our orders look like this.
  • Pure CMT. The buyer supplies every input — yarn, labels, elastics, polybags, cartons — and the factory charges only for knitting, linking, finishing and packing. Common in domestic manufacturing, less common on export orders because of the customs work.
  • Partial free-issue. The buyer supplies one or two items (usually the yarn, or branded trims) and the factory sources the rest. This is the practical middle ground and the one we would recommend to most first-time buyers.

Knitwear is not cut-and-sew: there is no fabric roll to issue. Yarn arrives on cones, gets knitted into shaped panels, and any leftover is a dyed, partly used asset that is hard to redeploy. That single fact drives most of the friction. Our explanation of fully fashioned construction covers why consumption is calculated per panel rather than per metre.

Why buyers choose it — and when it backfires

Good reasons: a proprietary blend, a mill-certified traceable fibre, a group-wide colour standard, or a yarn you already own from a cancelled programme. In those cases free-issue protects something the factory cannot buy. Bad reasons are more common: the assumption that buying yarn directly removes a markup, or that a mill quoted price converts one-for-one into a cheaper sweater. It usually does not, because mills sell by conditioned weight and factories buy with a wastage buffer, credit terms and a replacement guarantee built in — see conditioned weight and moisture regain.

We have watched a buyer save roughly 8% on yarn and then lose it twice over: one week of production lost waiting for a replacement cone after a dye-lot shortage, and a short shipment because the delivered quantity was calculated on theoretical consumption with no allowance. If the yarn is ordinary — a standard 2/28 nm wool-acrylic, a stock cotton — let the factory buy it. You are paying for their yield risk, and that is worth something.

Ask for the calculated consumption in kilograms per dozen, then compare it with the yarn-per-sweater arithmetic and the bill of materials. If the supplier cannot show the calculation, they are guessing at the allowance.

Where liability sits when something goes wrong

Three failures account for nearly every dispute we have handled:

  • Yarn quality. Neps, slubs, uneven twist or a shade drift inside the delivered lot. The factory can reject the yarn on arrival, but it cannot be held responsible for a defect that was visible on the cone. Our yarn faults guide shows what an incoming check should catch.
  • Quantity short. Theoretical consumption never survives contact with a knitting floor. If you delivered exactly the calculated kilograms, a small shortfall stops the line. This is why we ask for a buffer and for a written rule on top-up dyeing.
  • Branded trims arriving late. Main labels, hangtags and polybags are often bought by the buyer's own packaging vendor, and they arrive after the garments are finished. The trim card approval should include a delivery date, not just a photograph.

Write the split into the supply contract: the factory is responsible for workmanship, measurements, finishing and packing to the approved sample; the buyer is responsible for fitness, quantity and timely arrival of supplied materials. Without that line, a rejected shipment becomes an argument about whose yarn it was.

The paperwork that makes it work

A free-issue order needs one page that both sides sign. Ours lists the yarn specification and mill lot number, delivered net weight and cone count, the agreed wastage percentage, the date the material must be on the floor, the incoming-check result, the consumption figure per dozen, the shortage-replacement rule, and what happens to leftovers. Add the branded trim schedule to the same page — labels, hangtags and packaging are covered in more detail in our trims guide. If you are comparing this with a standard OEM order, the difference is not the sewing; it is the ownership of the inputs.

Frequently asked questions

Should I supply my own yarn to a Chinese knitwear factory?

Only when the yarn is something the factory cannot source — a proprietary blend, a certified traceable fibre, or stock you already own. For standard qualities the factory's mill relationships, credit terms and replacement guarantee usually beat a direct mill price once wastage and delay are priced in. Ask for both quotes side by side.

Who pays for yarn wastage on a CMT order?

The buyer supplies the extra yarn. On fully fashioned knitwear we normally ask for 3–5% above calculated consumption, and more for multi-colour jacquard or stripe work. Agree the percentage in writing and reconcile actual consumption at the end of the run so leftovers can be returned or credited.

Can a factory work with buyer-supplied labels and packaging?

Yes, and it is the easiest form of free issue. Send the physical labels, hangtags and polybags to the factory before packing starts, with a trim card showing exactly where each item goes and the quantity per unit. Late trims are the most common cause of a finished order missing its ship date.
