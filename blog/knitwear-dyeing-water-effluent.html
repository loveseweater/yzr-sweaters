Water Use and Effluent in Knitwear Dyeing: What to Ask Your Mill

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Dyeing and washing a kilogram of knitwear typically consumes somewhere between 60 and 150 litres of water, and that water leaves the dyehouse as effluent that has to be treated before discharge. A competent mill can tell you its litres-per-kilogram figure, whether it runs its own treatment plant or shares a centralised one, and whether it works to ZDHC limits. If a supplier cannot answer any of that, it is outsourcing your compliance risk along with your dyeing.

Water is the least glamorous topic in a knitwear negotiation, and it is increasingly the one that decides whether your sustainability claims survive scrutiny. Buyers ask us about yarn content and certified fibres all the time — rarely about what happened at the dyeing and washing stage, where most of the water in a sweater's footprint is actually spent. Here's what the numbers look like, and the questions worth asking before you place the order.

Where the water actually goes

A sweater passes through wet processing at several points, and each one is a water event:

  • Yarn dyeing or piece dyeing. If your colour is dyed at the yarn stage, the dyehouse runs dye baths, rinses and soaping-off washes on cone or hank yarn. Piece dyeing (dyeing the knitted panel or garment) needs similar bath volumes with less liquor ratio discipline, since the material is already shaped.
  • Garment washing. Many of our styles get a relaxation or softener wash after linking — this is the familiar step described in our guide to garment washing and finishing. It is also where machine-washable wool treatments happen, and those chlorine-based processes carry their own effluent profile.
  • Sampling. Lab dips and sample dye lots are small, but per kilogram they are the most water-inefficient runs in the chain. Batching colour approvals, as we recommend in our lab dip guide, keeps a lid on this.

Solid, widely quoted industry figures put conventional piece dyeing at roughly 80–150 litres per kilogram of goods, while modern low-liquor-ratio equipment gets below 60. Weaving and finishing mills elsewhere in apparel can be far worse — knitwear's advantage is that it is a relatively short wet chain, and the best Dongguan dyehouses are genuinely efficient. But "relatively efficient" is not something you should take on faith.

What is in the effluent — and why buyers inherit it

Effluent from dyeing and washing carries residual dyestuff, detergents, salts, fixing agents and sometimes heavy-metal traces from certain dye classes. Three things matter to you as a buyer:

  • Chemical input discipline. The ZDHC Manufacturing Restricted Substances List has become the common language here. A mill that runs ZDHC-compliant chemistry and can show RSL test reports is managing what goes into the water before it ever leaves.
  • Treatment before discharge. In China, textile dyeing discharge falls under the national GB 4287 standard, which sets limits on COD, pH, suspended solids and more. Compliance means a working wastewater treatment plant — either on-site or a piped centralised plant — not a pipe to the river.
  • Your paperwork. Under the due-diligence expectations described in EU sustainability rules, a brand selling into Europe may be asked to substantiate environmental claims down to supplier level. "Our factory is certified" is no longer enough; the question is what the wet processors actually hold.

We see this a lot with smaller dyehouses that do good colour work but have thin documentation. The dyeing is fine; the paper trail is not. If your market requires evidence, that gap becomes your problem at the worst possible moment — mid-season, when a customer or platform asks for it.

Six questions that separate good mills from cheap ones

You do not need to be a chemist to vet a dyehouse. Ask these, in this order, and note not just the answers but how quickly they come:

  • Do you dye in-house or outsource? Outsourcing is normal in Dongguan, but the knitwear supplier — not you — should own the vetting of the dyehouse and be able to name it.
  • What is your water consumption per kilogram? A mill that tracks this knows the number. "I'll have to ask" usually means nobody has ever asked.
  • Where does your effluent go, and do you have discharge permits or treatment agreements? Look for a named treatment arrangement, and cross-check it during factory audits.
  • Do you work to ZDHC MRSL, and can you provide test reports for my dye chemistry?
  • Do you reuse water or recycle any streams? Cooling-water and final-rinse reuse is the most common and most credible answer.
  • Can dark shades be dyed with your low-impact chemistry? Deep blacks and navies are where mills cut corners; the answers reveal how they behave under pressure.

None of this needs to cost you more. The dyehouses that invest in low-liquor machinery usually win on repeatability and re-dye rates too — the water story and the quality story point the same direction, which is one reason we treat sustainability and manufacturing discipline as one subject rather than two.

Practical decisions that cut water without heroics

  • Choose stock-supported colours where the range allows. Fewer custom dye lots means fewer small-batch baths — and shorter lead times as a bonus.
  • Dye at yarn stage for melanges and piece-dye for solids only when you genuinely need it; every extra wet process is water, energy and risk.
  • Approve colour once, properly. Re-dyeing a rejected shade doubles that shade's water footprint instantly. This is the single most common waste we see, and it is a buyer decision, not a mill decision.
  • Ask for ZDHC-level chemistry as standard rather than as an upgrade. Most competent mills can comply; they just do not unless asked.

Frequently asked questions

How much water does dyeing one sweater actually use?

A rough working figure for conventional piece or garment dyeing is 60–150 litres per kilogram, so a 400-gram sweater represents roughly 25–60 litres of process water. Modern low-liquor-ratio equipment can bring that down substantially, and skipping an unnecessary re-dye saves more water than any equipment upgrade.

Is effluent compliance the factory's problem or mine?

Legally, the dyehouse's. Commercially, increasingly yours — EU due-diligence expectations and platform sustainability claims can reach down to supplier-level evidence. If you make environmental claims about your knitwear, you need suppliers who can substantiate their wet processing, which is why we recommend asking about treatment arrangements before your first order rather than after a customer complaint.

Does ZDHC compliance make dyeing more expensive?

At well-run mills, usually marginally or not at all — compliant chemistry is now the default offering of major dye suppliers. Where you do see cost impact is in very dark shades or unusual effects that historically relied on restricted chemistry. Ask early, because the workaround is a colour decision, not a price negotiation.
