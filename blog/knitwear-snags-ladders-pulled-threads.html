Snags, Ladders and Pulled Threads: The Knitwear Defects That Are Not Pilling

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

A snag is a loop pulled out of the fabric surface by a sharp edge; a ladder (or run) is that pulled yarn releasing a whole column of loops. Neither is pilling, which is fibre fuzz balling up on the surface. Knitted loops are structurally easier to catch than woven yarns, so prevention has to cover yarn choice, stitch density, finishing, polybagging and even the zips and jewellery the garment meets after it leaves the factory.

A buyer emails us a photo of one sweater with a loose thread across the chest and asks whether the whole batch is bad. We see this a lot, and the honest answer is nearly always: it depends on where the thread went and when. Most pilling complaints are about fibre and wear; snagging is about geometry — one loop caught on something. That makes it a design, packing and handling problem as much as a quality one, which is why it belongs in the spec rather than only in the final inspection.

What actually happens to the fabric

Knitted fabric is a chain of interlocking loops. Pull one loop and the yarn draws slack from its neighbours, leaving a raised float on the surface. If the yarn breaks or the structure is loose enough, the loops in that wale release one after another and you get a ladder running down the panel — the stocking-run effect. Fine-gauge single jersey, open lace structures, loose tension and long floats in jacquard or intarsia are the highest-risk constructions. Rib and cable structures hold a caught loop more locally, which is one reason a snag on a heavy rib cuff is often invisible by the next wear.

Three different defects get reported as one word, and inspectors should name them separately:

  • Pulled thread / float — yarn drawn out but intact. Usually repairable by working the slack back to the reverse with a fine hook.
  • Snag — a loop or group of loops visibly raised, sometimes with a small hole alongside it.
  • Ladder / run — a column of released loops. On knitwear this is a major defect and normally not repairable to retail standard.

Our fabric defects guide lists these alongside holes, dropped stitches and barre; the distinction matters because the remedy and the liability are different.

Where the damage happens

When a batch arrives with snags, the cause is rarely the knitting machine. In our experience the highest-risk points are:

  • Inside the carton. Rough carton edges, staples, or garments packed together with zips, hooks and metal trims from other styles. A single exposed zip in a mixed carton can mark a dozen units.
  • At finishing and packing. Wire hangers, burrs on pressing tables, untrimmed thread ends, and operators' jewellery. This is a factory discipline issue and it is the cheapest place to fix it.
  • At the customer. Rings, bracelets, handbag hardware, seatbelt buckles, velcro and pet claws. Careful brands warn about this on the care card, because a customer who snags a sleeve on a bracelet still leaves a one-star review.
  • In transit or on the shop floor. Garments re-hung on rough racks, or polybags torn open with a blade. Our packing guide covers the physical side.

That last group is why we ask buyers to photograph an unopened carton before a claim. If the damage is confined to units near the top of one carton, it is a packing or opening problem, not a production one — see how we handle complaints and returns.

How inspectors should grade it

Most AQL inspection plans treat a visible snag or a ladder as a major defect and a tiny loose float as a minor one, but the word "visible" does a lot of work. What we ask buyers to do is agree a photographic standard with the factory before bulk: one photo of an acceptable float, one of a rejectable snag, one of a ladder. Without it, two inspectors will grade the same garment differently, and the quality standards document will not save you at the final inspection.

What to specify to prevent it

  • Yarn and twist. Adequate twist and yarn strength resist a pull; very soft, low-twist, brushed yarns feel wonderful and snag easily. Our notes on ply and twist explain the trade-off.
  • Stitch density. A slightly tighter loop length costs a little yarn and dramatically reduces laddering. Do not let a cost-reduction exercise quietly loosen the tension — check tension and loop length against the approved sample.
  • Float length. Long floats on the reverse of jacquard styles catch fingers and jewellery. Cap them in the design brief.
  • Seams and fastenings. A seam that leaves a hard edge, or a zip with a sharp top stop, will do the damage itself. Check closures on the wear trial.
  • Packing. Individual polybags, smooth carton interiors, no staples, no mixed hardware, and a lint-and-hook check at final packing. Small accessories such as knit gloves and mittens need the same discipline at a smaller scale.

Frequently asked questions

Is a snag the same as pilling?

No. Pilling is loose fibre fuzz rolling into small balls on the surface after wear and friction. A snag is a loop physically pulled out by a sharp edge, and a ladder is a column of loops released from that pull. They have different causes and different fixes: pilling is about fibre and yarn quality, snagging is about structure, density and what the garment touches.

Can a pulled thread be repaired?

Often yes, if the yarn is intact. A fine hook or latch needle works the slack back through to the reverse, and a light press restores the surface. Never cut the thread — that converts a repairable float into a hole. A full ladder with released loops normally cannot be restored to retail standard.

Who is liable when snags appear after shipment?

It depends on where they are. Damage clustered in one carton or near the top points to packing or opening; random units across cartons point to production or finishing; damage reported after customer wear often comes from jewellery and hardware. Photograph the unopened carton and agree an accept/reject photo standard with the factory before bulk to make the claim resolvable.
