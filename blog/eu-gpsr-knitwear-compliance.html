EU GPSR for Knitwear: The Responsible Person Rule Explained

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

The EU's General Product Safety Regulation (GPSR) has applied to consumer products sold in the EU since 13 December 2024 — knitwear included. It requires an EU-established "responsible person" for every product, whose name and postal address must appear on the product or packaging, batch-level traceability, safety information in the language of each member state where you sell, and technical documentation held ready for market-surveillance authorities. If you import knitwear into the EU, you are almost certainly the responsible person — and the practical work lands in your factory order: labels, batch records and documents you must ask for in advance.

Most knitwear buyers have a compliance blind spot shaped like their risk register: chemical testing is covered by RSL work, labelling by fibre-content rules, sustainability by the EU's green agenda — and then a regulation arrives that is about none of those things and all of them at once. We started getting GPSR questions from European customers the season after it took effect, and the confusion is always the same: "does this apply to a plain sweater?" It does. This guide explains what the regulation actually demands, in the order you need to act on it, written from the factory side of the conversation.

What GPSR is, and who it actually binds

The GPSR — Regulation (EU) 2023/988 — replaced the old General Product Safety Directive and shifted European product-safety law from "reactive recall" toward "prove it before it ships." It covers consumer products placed on the EU market, including textiles and clothing, whether sold in shops or online. Unlike the fibre-name and labelling rules we cover in care and composition labelling, or the sustainability reporting stack in our EU sustainability rules guide, GPSR is product-safety legislation — but it reaches ordinary garments through traceability and documentation obligations that assume any product can become a safety issue: a drawstring, a flammable finish, a detachable trim a child could swallow.

The obligations apply along the whole supply chain, to manufacturers, importers, distributors, fulfilment-service providers and online marketplaces — with escalating duties depending on your role. For a buyer importing Chinese knitwear into the EU, the pivotal role is importer: the first economic operator established in the EU who places the product on the market. That is almost always you, not your factory, and not your freight forwarder.

The responsible person: the question everyone asks first

GPSR requires every product offered in the EU to have a responsible person established in the EU. For an imported sweater the options are: you as the importer; an authorised representative appointed by your manufacturer in the EU; or, for distance sales, the fulfilment-service provider in some configurations. In practice, most small and mid-size knitwear brands end up appointing themselves. What that means concretely:

  • Your company name and a EU postal address must be marked on the product, its packaging, an attached label or accompanying documents — alongside the manufacturer's details where you are not the manufacturer yourself.
  • You become the contact point for market-surveillance authorities: if a member state's authority has a question about a batch, it comes to you, and you need answers within days, not weeks.
  • Online listings must display the same information — a requirement that catches many brands who sell through their own site or marketplaces and never touched a physical label.

We see this a lot from the factory side: a customer discovers the label requirement after bulk is packed, then asks for re-labelling at the warehouse in Europe — slower and far more expensive than printing the correct labels at source. The fix is boring and effective: put the responsible-person line into the label and trim specification before sampling closes, exactly where your care symbols and fibre names already sit.

Traceability: batches, and why they matter now

GPSR expects products to be traceable through the supply chain. For knitwear the practical unit is the production batch or lot — the number your factory already uses internally for dye lots and production tracking, now surfaced onto labels or documents so that a problem found in retail can be traced back to a specific production run instead of "everything from that supplier, that year." Ask your factory to record which knitting, dyeing and finishing lots fed each shipped carton. Good factories run this level of lot control anyway for colour consistency; GPSR simply means the records travel with the goods, in a form you can retrieve under deadline.

Documentation: the file you hope you never open

The regulation expects the responsible person to hold technical documentation for the products they place on the market — a description of the product, the safety assessment reasoning, test reports and evidence of conformity with applicable rules, and information reasonably necessary to assess safety. For knitwear, the file is assembled from things a good buyer already produces: the tech pack (see our tech pack guide), the quality standards document agreed with the factory, lab test reports covering physical and chemical safety, and the RSL test results for restricted substances. What changes under GPSR is that these papers become an organized dossier with your name on it, kept up to date and producible on demand.

Safety information — instructions and warnings where relevant — must be in the language or languages of each member state where the product is sold. For a plain sweater this is usually satisfied by correct care labelling plus nothing else; for anything with functional risk (cords, kids' styles, novelty trims) check against our children's knitwear safety guide, because childrenswear carries stricter safety rules regardless of GPSR, and the two regimes compound.

What to ask your factory for, in plain terms

  • Label data at source. Add the responsible-person name and EU address, and the manufacturer's details, into the main label artwork before bulk — costs nothing at sampling, costs a re-work at destination.
  • Batch numbers on labels or cartons, with a factory-side record mapping each shipped batch to production lots.
  • Copies of test reports — chemical, physical, and any safety-relevant results — issued against your order references, not generic certificates.
  • A signed technical description of the product: composition, gauge, construction, trims, and any warnings — essentially your tech pack countersigned as a safety-relevant description.
  • Cooperation in writing on corrective action if a market authority query lands: who answers, in what time, with what records.

None of this is exotic. A factory that already runs gold-seal sample control, lot traceability and documented testing — the disciplines in our supplier scorecard guide — can produce all five items from existing systems. A factory that cannot is telling you something about how it will behave when an authority asks.

Enforcement: what failure looks like

GPSR is enforced by national market-surveillance authorities who can require corrective action, withdraw products, and coordinate through the EU's safety-gateway system; online marketplaces must act on flagged products too. The realistic trigger for knitwear is not a plain crew-neck — it is a product with a safety-adjacent feature or a complaint pattern. When it happens, the authorities' first request is the dossier and traceability records, and the second is a fix. Buyers with the file assembled answer in a day; buyers without it spend weeks reconstructing which factory, which batch, which test — which is how a minor issue becomes a recall.

Frequently asked questions

Does GPSR really apply to plain adult knitwear with no obvious safety risk?

Yes — it applies to all consumer products placed on the EU market, not only obviously risky ones. Plain adult sweaters usually satisfy the safety side through ordinary materials and construction, but the procedural duties still run: an EU responsible person, their name and address on the product or packaging, traceability, and documentation held ready. In practice the regulation costs a plain-sweater importer a label line and a filing discipline, not a testing programme.

Can my freight forwarder or fulfilment centre be the responsible person?

A freight forwarder that only transports goods is not established as an economic operator for this purpose. Fulfilment-service providers can carry responsible-person duties in specific distance-selling configurations, but that is a contractual and legal question for your EU advisor, not a default. In most import structures we see, the importer itself is the responsible person — plan for that unless your counsel says otherwise.

What exactly should I request from my Chinese factory for GPSR?

Five things: responsible-person and manufacturer details printed into the main label artwork at source; batch or lot numbers on labels or cartons with a lot-mapping record; test reports issued against your order references; a countersigned technical description of the product covering composition, construction and trims; and written commitment on response times if a market authority ever queries the batch. A factory with normal quality systems produces all five from records it already keeps.
