Section 301, Anti-Dumping and Countervailing Duties on Knitwear Imports

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

The HS code rate printed in a tariff lookup is rarely the whole duty bill on a China-origin sweater. Section 301 additional tariffs in the US, and anti-dumping or countervailing duty orders in some markets, sit on top of the normal rate and are charged on the same customs value. They change by HTS line, by origin and by review cycle, so the number your broker confirmed in January is not necessarily the number that applies when the vessel berths. Price them as a separate line in your landed cost, confirm them against your exact HTS code close to shipment, and put the assumption in writing in the purchase order.

We see this a lot with US buyers placing a first China order: they look up the duty rate for HS 6110, build a margin on it, then get an entry summary that is 7.5 or 25 points higher than expected. Nothing about the sweater changed — the extra came from a trade remedy that sits outside the ordinary tariff schedule. Here is how those layers work, and what to do about them before you commit to a retail price.

The ordinary rate is only the first layer

Every import has a base rate from the tariff schedule, set by the HTS subheading. For knitwear that is mostly Chapter 61 — 6110 for sweaters, pullovers and cardigans, 6111 for babies' garments, 6114 for other knitted apparel — and fibre content decides the line: wool or fine animal hair, cotton, man-made fibres, or other. Those rates are the ones a quick online lookup gives you, and they are the ones most suppliers quietly assume when they say duty is around ten percent. They are real, but they are the floor, not the ceiling.

Section 301: a list, not a rate

Section 301 tariffs are additional ad valorem duties applied to specific lists of China-origin HTS lines. Apparel and textile lines appeared across several lists at 7.5 or 25 percent, and exclusions have been granted, extended and allowed to lapse on their own schedule. Three things matter for a buyer:

  • It applies by HTS subheading and country of origin, not by supplier. Two factories shipping the same style get the same treatment; two styles with different fibre content may not.
  • It stacks. A 16 percent base rate plus a 7.5 percent Section 301 tariff is assessed at 23.5 percent of the customs value, not 7.5. Our HS code and duty guide covers how the classification is arrived at in the first place.
  • It can move while the goods are on the water. Exclusions expire, reviews conclude, and rates change. Verify close to sailing, not at the sampling stage.

Anti-dumping and countervailing duties are a different animal

An anti-dumping or countervailing duty order is not a general tariff. It is the result of a case against a named product from a named country, sometimes with company-specific rates, and it is collected as a cash deposit at entry with a final rate fixed after an administrative review. Rates in some textile cases have been high enough to end a product category overnight. Most mainstream sweater categories from China are not currently covered by a US order — but that is a fact to verify against your exact HTS line and product scope, not an assumption to build a margin on. Your broker can check the current case list in a few minutes; our clearance walkthrough explains where that check fits in the entry process.

Where the money actually lands

Legally, the importer of record pays — which for most buyers is you, not the factory. Commercially it depends on the Incoterm: under DDP the seller has priced the duty in and carries the change, under FOB or CIF you do. That distinction is worth being explicit about, because it is exactly where a rate change becomes an argument rather than a line item; see Incoterms for knitwear and the landed cost calculation. Low-value direct-to-consumer parcels used to sit outside most of this under de minimis rules, which have changed recently — our VAT, GST and de minimis guide covers that separately. If you re-export the goods, ask about duty drawback; do not assume it happens automatically.

How to price an order without getting surprised

  • Get the exact 10-digit HTS in writing for each style. Fibre composition moves the line, so an inaccurate composition statement is a classification problem as well as a labelling one — see US fibre content labelling.
  • Ask the broker for the full stack as of a date near sailing: base rate, Section 301 list and current rate, any AD/CVD case, any exclusion that applies.
  • Put the assumption in the PO. State the rate you priced on and who absorbs a change; supply contract essentials has the clause wording worth using.
  • Carry a contingency line. A few points of buffer in the landed cost is cheaper than repricing a whole season.
  • Never solve it with a description change or a detour. Origin follows where the garment was substantially transformed, not the port it sailed from. Rerouting through a third country without further processing does not change origin — it creates a misdeclaration risk. That also applies to transshipment routings, which are a logistics decision, not a duty one.

Frequently asked questions

Does Section 301 replace the normal duty rate?

No. It is an additional duty charged on top of the base rate for the same HTS line. A sweater at 16 percent MFN with a 7.5 percent Section 301 tariff is assessed at 23.5 percent of the customs value, not 7.5. Your broker should show both lines on the entry summary.

Who pays if the rate changes after I place the order?

Legally the importer of record, which is usually the buyer. Commercially it depends on the Incoterm: under DDP the seller has priced it in, under FOB or CIF you carry the change. Write the rate assumption and the change mechanism into the purchase order before you sign.

Can I avoid it by shipping through another country?

No. Origin is determined by where the garment was substantially transformed — for knitwear, generally where the panels were knitted and assembled — not by the port it sailed from. Routing goods through a third country without further processing does not change origin and creates a misdeclaration risk. Reroute for logistics reasons if it makes sense; do not do it for duty reasons.
