Knitwear Supplier Vetting: A 12-Point Checklist for Buyers

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Choosing a knitwear supplier is mostly a verification exercise. The best-looking catalogue and the fastest email reply tell you little about whether a factory can hold quality at volume, hit a delivery window, or survive an audit your own customer might later demand. This checklist has twelve points you can work through in a first video call, a sample request and — ideally — a factory visit. Use it to separate suppliers who can scale with you from those who will cost you a season.

1. Company credentials and legal identity

Confirm the legal entity name, business licence and registered address, and check that the entity you contract with is the one producing the goods. Many trading companies present themselves as factories; that is not automatically a problem, but you should know who owns production and who is accountable if something goes wrong. Ask for the licence and the bank account name — a mismatch with the trading name is a warning sign.

2. Certifications worth verifying

Certifications only matter if they are current and cover the site doing your work. Ask for certificate numbers and verify them independently.

  • BSCI — social compliance audit; check the rating and date, not just the logo.
  • SEDEX / SMETA — another social audit framework; confirm the audit is recent and the corrective actions closed.
  • Oeko-Tex Standard 100 — tests for harmful substances; confirm which product classes are covered.
  • ISO 9001 or similar — quality management; useful signal, but check the scope covers knitwear.

A supplier who shares certificates readily and points you to official verification portals is usually confident in them. One who sends a blurry image and changes the subject is not.

3. Willingness to accept audits and inspections

This is the most telling item on the list. Ask directly: can we visit the factory, can we send a third-party inspector, and will you allow unannounced audits? Suppliers who welcome verification — SGS, BV or your own QC team — are almost always the safer choice. A factory that refuses a visit or insists all contact goes through an intermediary is a serious risk flag regardless of price.

4. Sampling capability and communication

Good sampling previews bulk quality. Assess whether the yarn is to spec, the fit matches your measurements, trims and labels are correct, and the supplier explains deviations rather than hides them. Note turnaround time and how many rounds it takes to get a wearable sample.

5. Product line match

Ask what share of production is knitwear and which gauges and yarns they run routinely. A factory that knits fine-gauge merino and cashmere all year will struggle with a chunky hand-knit cable outside its wheelhouse. The right supplier is one whose everyday output matches your styles.

6. Responsiveness and English communication

Response quality during the enquiry stage predicts response quality in production. Look for clear answers to technical questions, replies within a stated window, and a named person who owns your account. If a simple spec question takes a week and three reminders, expect the same when chasing a shipment.

7. MOQ flexibility

MOQs vary by yarn, colour and style, so ask for the figure per style and per colourway rather than one headline number. A supplier offering a practical first order minimum — enough to test the market without overcommitting — is a better partner than one who quotes huge and negotiates down.

8. Quality control process

Ask where inspection happens and to what standard. A credible answer covers in-line checks during knitting and linking, a final inspection before packing, and a stated AQL sampling plan. Clarify who inspects, whether the report is shared, and whether you may send a third-party inspector. Suppliers who describe a process, not just a promise, are the ones to keep.

9. Delivery record and capacity

Request references for similar orders and ask about on-time delivery performance. Understand their calendar and peak seasons: knitwear factories are busiest ahead of autumn and winter, so a spring quote may look comfortable while a September window is tight. Ask how many lines they run and what happens if a yarn delivery slips.

10. Payment terms and commercial clarity

The quote should be transparent about price, what is included, currency, and payment schedule. Be cautious of a supplier who demands full payment upfront with no production milestones, and equally of one offering terms too generous to be real. A deposit plus balance against shipping documents is common; a supplier willing to align payment to agreed inspection milestones is signalling confidence.

11. Documentation and compliance ability

If you import, you need accurate paperwork: commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, and certificates of origin or compliance declarations where required. Ask what documents the supplier routinely issues and whether they support your market's requirements. Sloppy documentation delays customs clearance and creates avoidable cost.

12. Risk signals to watch

Some patterns should make you slow down, however attractive the quote.

  • A price far below the market — usually means substituted yarn, lighter weight or hidden costs later.
  • No physical address or refusal to visit — you cannot verify what you cannot see.
  • Vague answers on yarn and gauge — a sign the spec is not under control.
  • Samples that differ from bulk without explanation — the classic bait-and-switch risk.
  • Pressure to pay in full upfront — shifts all risk to you.
  • Reluctance to allow third-party inspection — the strongest single warning.

Written answers to the twelve points give you a comparable profile across suppliers, far more useful than gut feel. Score each one, then choose the supplier whose weaknesses you can actually manage.
