Selling Knitwear into Africa: South Africa, Nigeria and Kenya

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Africa is at least three different knitwear markets, not one. South Africa is a genuine winter market with formal retail and a European-style buying calendar; Nigeria is a vast, price-led market built on lightweight knits and informal retail; Kenya and the East African highlands sit in between, with cool evenings, a large school and uniform sweater trade, and thrift-market competition. Plan styles, gauges, price points and payment terms separately for each — and expect duties, ports and foreign-exchange realities to shape the program as much as the weather does.

Buyers eyeing Africa usually arrive with one of two wrong assumptions: that the whole continent is hot and needs no knitwear, or that it's one market with one playbook. Neither survives contact with the first order. After shipping to buyers across the continent for years — from Cape Town chains to Lagos wholesalers — here's the honest picture of what each region actually buys and what the operational edges look like.

South Africa: the real winter market

The highveld — Johannesburg, Pretoria, Bloemfontein — has genuinely cold, dry winters from June to August, with nights below freezing. Cape Town's winters are wet and cold. This is a real sweater market, not an accessory market:

  • Product mix: the same heavy gauges and wool-rich blends that work in northern Europe — cables, ribs, chunky cardigans — plus a layering tier for milder coastal provinces. Our southern hemisphere buying guide covers the calendar: SA autumn/winter orders are placed when Chinese factories are at their quietest, which is an advantage in capacity and price.
  • Retail structure: formalised chains and department groups with technical buying teams, showroom standards and compliance expectations closer to a European buyer's than a market trader's. Expect proper quality standards documents, test requirements and packaging specs.
  • Duty and regulation: South African clothing import duties are among the highest anywhere on apparel — historically in the 40%+ band on many knit categories, plus 15% VAT — and labelling must meet local content and care-labelling rules. Get the current tariff confirmed on your HS code before you model margins; our HS codes and duties primer explains how.
  • Logistics: Durban is the main container gateway, with Cape Town and Port Elizabeth secondary; port congestion and strikes have been known to add weeks, so a forwarder with Southern Africa experience is worth paying for.

Nigeria: volume, price and hustle

A market of over 200 million people where the knitwear reality is different: most of the country is hot year-round, so the sweater trade leans light and cheap rather than warm and premium.

  • Product mix: lightweight, low-gauge acrylic and cotton-blend knits — the "cool morning" layer for harmattan season (December–February, when dry Saharan air cools mornings), plus fashion knits for the youth market in Lagos and Abuja. Chunky merino programs are mostly not the trade; price per piece is.
  • Channels: a huge informal retail layer — market traders, wholesalers in places like Lagos's Balogun markets — alongside a small formal chain segment. Big wholesale buyers often work on smaller margins, faster turns, and larger colour-block simplicity. Basic styles from our value-tier programs fit this channel naturally.
  • The hard edges: duties are high, import processes can be slow and discretionary at the port of Lagos, demurrage charges run hot, and — the big one — foreign exchange. Naira availability has repeatedly delayed or reshaped payments. Credit terms to Nigerian buyers deserve real caution; our payment terms guidance applies with force here. Secure payment before shipment, or use instruments your bank actually stands behind.

Kenya and East Africa: highland knitwear

Nairobi sits at 1,800 metres; evenings and July mornings are genuinely cool. East Africa's knitwear demand has a shape of its own:

  • The school sweater economy. Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania and Rwanda have enormous school-uniform volume — V-neck pullovers in school colours, durable, mid-price, ordered in mass. It's the single biggest structured knitwear opportunity in the region, and it runs on dependable delivery for term-start dates more than on design novelty. Corporate and institutional uniform programs, covered in our uniform knitwear guide, follow similar logic.
  • Retail competition: imported second-hand knitwear ("mitumba") undercuts the entry price point — new garments compete on durability, branding and consistency, not on being cheapest. Blends that survive 30 washes beat the 50-cent thrift sweater over a school year.
  • Duties and logistics: East African Community tariffs on apparel run high, with used-clothing imports a live political issue and local textile-industry protection (including local-content pressures on government procurement) worth tracking. Mombasa is the gateway port, generally smoother than Lagos; inland trucking to Kampala and Kigali adds days, which our lead time planning should absorb.

Cross-cutting practicalities

  • Sizing and fit: South Africa grades closer to UK conventions; Nigerian trading sizes skew to local wholesale conventions; school-wear is spec-driven and unisex. Confirm grading per market — assuming US or EU blocks everywhere is a common first-order error.
  • Seasonal calendar: southern African winter is June–August; harmattan in West Africa is December–February; East African term-start knitwear peaks in December–January and May–June. That spread means a factory program can serve Africa almost year-round — one of the underrated reasons to build these relationships.
  • Compliance: fibre-content and care labelling rules differ by country and are enforced unevenly — unevenly doesn't mean never. Compliant labels and honest fibre declarations cost little and prevent the shipment-stopping problems that do occur.
  • Start smaller, ship righter: first programs into any African market should be conservative on volume, generous on quality, and structured on payment — the buyers who succeed here scale deliberately, market by market.

Frequently asked questions

Is there real demand for sweaters in Africa?

Yes, but concentrated and shaped by altitude and season. South Africa's highveld and the East African highlands have genuine winters and evenings; harmattan gives West Africa a cool-morning season. The demand skews practical — school wear, uniforms, durable everyday knits — more than fashion-led, though urban fashion demand is real in Johannesburg, Lagos, Nairobi and Cape Town.

What duty should I expect on knitwear imported into these markets?

High, across the board. South Africa's apparel tariffs have long sat in the 40%+ range on many knit categories plus VAT; Nigeria's import regime is punitive on apparel; the East African Community applies high common external tariffs as well. These are structural, not incidental — model them into your landed cost before pricing, and confirm the current rate for your specific HS classification with your broker, since they change.

How risky is selling to buyers in Nigeria on credit terms?

Treat open credit as high risk: foreign-exchange shortages have repeatedly delayed repatriation of payments even from well-intentioned buyers. The workable structures are payment before shipment, confirmed irrevocable letters of credit from solid banks, or starting with smaller orders to build history. A Nigerian buyer who understands this and pays reliably is a genuinely valuable long-term partner — the point is to let the payment record build trust, not the other way round.
