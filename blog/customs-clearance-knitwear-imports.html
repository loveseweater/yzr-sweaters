Customs Clearance for Knitwear: A Buyer's Walkthrough

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Customs clearance is a paperwork exam, not an interrogation. The cargo that clears quickly is the cargo whose documents all agree: commercial invoice, packing list and bill of lading describing the same goods, in the same words, with a fibre content that matches what is physically sewn into the garment. Most holds we see on knitwear come from one of three mismatches — description, value or fibre content — and all three are fixable before the vessel sails, and mostly unfixable afterwards.

Buyers often treat customs as the forwarder's problem until the day a container sits at the terminal accruing storage while someone asks for a "corrected invoice". We ship knitwear exports daily and see what clears and what stalls. This walkthrough is the version of customs we wish every first-time importer read before booking their first shipment.

What clearance actually is

Customs clearance is your government agreeing, three times over, that your cargo can enter: the entry or declaration (what the goods are, in tariff terms), the valuation (what they are worth for duty purposes), and the duty and tax payment. Everything else — exams, holds, requests for information — is the process of customs checking those three answers against your documents and, sometimes, against the physical goods. A customs broker files the entry on your behalf using documents your supplier provides. Nobody opens every container; the system runs on trust and consequences, which is why inconsistent paperwork gets punished and boring, consistent paperwork sails through.

The document set that does the work

Four documents carry almost the entire clearance, and they must agree with each other to the letter:

  • Commercial invoice. Seller, buyer, Incoterms, currency, per-piece and total value, and a description specific enough to classify — "men's 100% lambswool knitted crew neck sweater, HS 6110.11" clears; "sweater" invites a query. Our guide to the shipping document set lists every field that matters.
  • Packing list. Cartons, pieces per carton, net and gross weights, and per-style breakdown. If the packing list says 150 cartons and the terminal counts 149, the entry does not match the manifest and the file stalls.
  • Bill of lading or air waybill. The contract of carriage and the title document. Consignee and notify party details must match your broker's instructions exactly — a misspelled consignee is a maddeningly common cause of delays at destination.
  • Certificate of origin. States where the goods were made, which sets the duty rate and, in some trade lanes, unlocks preferential rates. China-origin knitwear into the US currently carries Section 301 tariffs on many apparel lines on top of the base rate — check the current schedule, because these numbers have moved repeatedly. Into markets with trade agreements (ASEAN, RCEP members), the right origin paperwork can cut duty materially, one of the levers in reducing import duty legally.

Classification and valuation: where knitwear gets special treatment

Knitwear classification is driven almost entirely by fibre content. Chapter 61 of the Harmonized System splits knitted garments by fibre — wool, cotton, man-made fibre — and then by garment type and gender. Two sweaters that look identical to a shopper can sit in different tariff lines with different duty rates because one is 51% wool and the other is 49%. This is also why the sewn-in label matters so much: if your lab-dip paperwork says 60% wool but the label says 55%, customs labs do check, and a fibre content discrepancy is one of the more expensive mistakes in apparel importing. US buyers should also make sure labelling follows the fibre content labelling rules before goods ship, not after they arrive.

Valuation has a wrinkle worth knowing: customs value is not one universal number. US entries are generally valued on the FOB price — international freight and insurance are excluded from the duty base — while EU and UK entries are valued on a CIF-style base that includes them. The practical consequence: the same sweater at the same FOB price pays duty on a bigger number in Rotterdam than in Los Angeles, which is worth remembering when you build your landed cost model.

Brokers, entries and the timeline

In the US, customs entries must be filed within a set window after arrival (15 days for the entry summary), but the work starts much earlier — Importer Security Filings must be submitted before the cargo is loaded in China, which means your broker needs the document set days before the vessel sails. Europe runs on a similar logic with its import declarations and security filings. The practical habit to build: when we send final documents, forward the whole pack to your broker the same day. The broker is either your forwarder's in-house team, an independent customs broker you appoint, or your own logistics staff — but whoever it is, they work from your supplier's paperwork, so the supplier's document quality is your clearance speed. Common holds on knitwear — and how they end

  • Document mismatches. Invoice says 3,000 pieces, packing list says 2,980 (someone forgot the six pieces pulled for inspection). Fixed with corrected documents, usually days, occasionally weeks.
  • Valuation queries. A declared unit price far below market triggers scrutiny. Declared value is not negotiable — the transaction value on file must be the price actually paid — but having your payment records match the invoice makes queries short.
  • Fibre content verification. Lab tests on samples from the container. If they match the label and the entry, it is a formality; if not, expect reclassification, back duties and penalties.
  • Random and targeted exams. X-ray, tailgate or intensive container exams happen to everyone eventually. They are nobody's fault, they cost a few hundred dollars plus a few days, and the only defence is a schedule with slack in it — one more reason not to plan deliveries to the exact day.
  • IP and trade compliance scans. Apparel with brand logos attracts attention. If your styles carry licensed marks, have the licence paperwork ready before anyone asks.

Frequently asked questions

How long does customs clearance take for a knitwear shipment?

With clean, pre-lodged documents, most entries release within a day or two of the cargo arriving — often before you have finished arguing about container haulage. Holds and exams stretch that to a week or more. The variable is almost always document quality, which is why suppliers who send complete packs early are worth their margin.

Do I need a customs broker, or can I clear goods myself?

Legally, in most markets you can file your own entries; practically, for an importer of a few knitwear shipments a year, a broker is cheap insurance. Classification, valuation and preferential origin rules are technical, mistakes are expensive retroactively, and brokers file hundreds of entries a week. What you should do yourself is understand the documents — you are still legally responsible for what is filed in your name.

Who pays when customs holds my cargo?

Storage and exam fees land on the importer of record almost always, regardless of whose error caused the hold — the Incoterms shift the cost of freight, not the cost of your own customs process. If the hold traces to a supplier's document error, a fair supplier will share the cost, and the claim is easier when the document requirements were written into the purchase order in the first place.
