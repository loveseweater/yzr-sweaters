Planning Knitwear Production Around Chinese New Year Shutdowns

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

The official Chinese New Year holiday is one to two weeks; the real production gap is closer to four. Workers drift home early in late January, return gradually through February, and quality dips at both edges as teams re-form. Rule of thumb: anything that must ship before the break should be finished, inspected and moving by two weeks before New Year's Eve; anything starting after it should assume the factory is fully stable from early March, not day one after the holiday.

Chinese New Year moves around the calendar — anywhere from late January to mid-February — and every year we watch the same scene: a buyer in late January discovering that "six-week lead time" and "CNY" do not fit in the same sentence. The holiday is not a surprise; it is the single most predictable disruption in our industry. Here's how we plan around it, and how you should too.

What actually happens in a knitwear factory

The public holiday on paper is 7–8 days. In practice, for a Dongguan knitwear factory:

  • Two weeks out: the workforce starts thinning as workers with long journeys book early trains. Output falls first in finishing and packing — the departments that lean hardest on returning workers.
  • The holiday itself: the factory is closed. Yarn stores, machines, everything sits. (One genuinely useful exception: this is when maintenance and machine overhauls get done — so it is not dead time for the factory, just for your order.)
  • Two weeks after: workers return in waves. Knitting restarts before linking, linking before finishing. A line that lists 40 people might run 28 for a week.
  • By early March: most factories are genuinely back to full strength — but now every delayed order in the region is competing for the same capacity, which is exactly why booking capacity ahead matters more than usual.

The quality edge effects are real and under-discussed. A machine restarted by a different operator than the one who stopped it, a linking line staffed with new hires, an inspection team relearning a style — the first garments either side of the break deserve your closest attention. When we run inline inspection, we weight it heavier in those weeks, and our buyers' pre-shipment inspections should too.

Working back from the deadline

Take the next holiday as a concrete example — Chinese New Year 2027 falls on 6 February, with the official break roughly the first week of February. If you work backwards:

  • Mid-December 2026: last comfortable booking for pre-holiday production. Yarn must be secured by now; custom-dyed colours placed earlier still. This is the point where honest factories tell you "this won't make it before the break" — believe them.
  • By mid-January 2027: garments should be off the machines and into linking and finishing. Anything still knitting on 20 January is gambling with the packing line.
  • By ~23 January 2027: goods inspected, cartoned, and booked with the forwarder. Pre-ocean freight space is tight for everyone in southern China at once — trucking slots and vessel space both need to be confirmed, not assumed, before your factory empties out.
  • First two weeks of February 2027: nothing moves. Couriers and ports keep running at reduced capacity, but don't build plans on factory output.
  • Late February into March: post-holiday production restarts. Quote the restart realistically — a style that normally runs six weeks should be planned at seven to eight if it straddles the return.

If that timeline looks aggressive, it is — which is the honest answer. Normal lead times already assume a stable factory; CNY weeks are the one period where padding the schedule is not pessimism, it is arithmetic.

Five strategies that work

  • Front-load your autumn/winter season. Winter ranges ship June–October anyway; the buyers who get hurt are the ones with restocks or spring programmes planned into the gap. Use the buying calendar to pull decisions forward instead of pushing production forward.
  • Ship partial. If the order will not finish, take 60% before the break and the balance after. Splitting beats missing entirely — see our notes on staggered split shipments.
  • Pre-book yarn and trims before December. Yarn mills and trim suppliers face the same calendar. A factory that cannot knit for lack of labels is the silliest possible way to lose three weeks.
  • Use the shutdown for development, not delivery. Sampling that needs a technician but not a full line can often restart early — many pattern technicians are back before the general workforce. Plan your sampling to land in the gap and bulk to start clean in March.
  • Ask for the factory's actual return-date history. A factory that says "we're back on day 8" with a straight face is telling you about its best year. The honest answer includes the return-rate percentage — ask what it was last year.

The conversations worth having now

Our suggestion is simple: put the holiday on the agenda of every order discussion from late October onward. Ask which side of the break each order lands on, what the factory's pre-holiday last-ship date is, and what happens contractually if the post-holiday restart slips a week — the answer should be a recovery plan in the contract, not improvisation. Buyers who raise it early get the honest answer; buyers who raise it in January get the optimistic one, and then the late one.

There is also a silver lining nobody mentions: February is the quietest month of the year for attention. With the factory closed, engineering reviews, next-season planning and cost discussions with suppliers all get undivided attention. The buyers who use the break to prepare their SS range briefs consistently start spring-summer programmes two weeks ahead of everyone else.

Planning is the whole game here — the holiday is fixed, and so is the math.

Frequently asked questions

How long are knitwear factories actually closed for Chinese New Year?

The official holiday runs about a week, but plan on a three-to-four-week productivity gap: workers leave early, return gradually, and full-line stability usually returns by early March. Order-by-order, treat the gap as four weeks of reduced output rather than one week of closure, and pad post-holiday lead times by a week or two accordingly.

My order might not finish before the break — should I delay or split it?

Split it. Shipping the finished portion before the holiday protects your launch date, and the balance follows once the factory stabilises. Missing the pre-holiday shipping window entirely pushes everything into the March capacity crunch, when every delayed order in the region competes for machines and finishing lines at once.

Is quality really worse right after the holiday?

Reassembly risk is real: re-formed teams, restarted machines and new workers mean the first production runs after the break carry more variability than a mid-season run of the same style. It is manageable with weighted inline inspection and a tightened first-week check — just do not schedule your most complex style as the first thing back on the machines.
