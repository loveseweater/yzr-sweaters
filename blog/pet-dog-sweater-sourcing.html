Sourcing Dog Sweaters and Pet Knitwear from China

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Pet knitwear is a genuine factory category, not a side product: the garments are small, the machinery is the same, but sizing has no standard, the design details are functional (harness openings, belly straps, leg holes), and the buyer who wins is the one who publishes a clear, honest size chart. Plan for machine-washable yarns, hard-wearing finishes and safety-conscious trims, and the category rewards you with fast reorders all winter.

Pet humanisation is one of the few retail trends that kept growing through every wobble of the last few years, and knitwear rides it naturally — a dog sweater is cheap to make, gifts well, photographs well, and gets reordered every season because dogs keep growing, shedding and chewing. We produce pet programmes alongside our human ones, and buyers are usually surprised by how different the rulebook is. Here is that rulebook.

The market, in one minute

Pet apparel splits into three channels that each want different things. Pet-specialty retail and chains want breadth — a dozen breeds' worth of sizes, strong packaging, barcode-ready product for shelves, the same requirements we describe in our GTIN and barcode guide. Fashion brands extending into pet want a mini-me story — the pet version of a human style already in the range, which is the easiest possible design brief since the block already exists. Online DTC pet brands want photos, videos and a size chart that works, because their returns problem is entirely a sizing problem. All three reorder fast when sell-through is good, which is why the category behaves more like accessories than outerwear in margin terms.

Sizing: the whole game

There is no standardised pet sizing. None. "Medium" means nothing across brands, and every serious pet programme is built on two body measurements instead: chest girth at the widest point, and back length from neck base to tail base. Weight bands help marketing but cause returns on their own — two 8-kilogram dogs can be shaped completely differently.

  • Build your grade on measurements, not breed names. Breed names belong in the marketing copy as guidance ("fits most corgis, Frenchies and beagles"), not in the factory spec. A six-size run from 30cm to 70cm chest girth covers the great majority of the market.
  • Decide the length philosophy early. Sweaters for long-bodied breeds (dachshunds sell disproportionately well) are a different block than generic cuts; some brands run a "long" sub-range as a story in itself.
  • Publish garment measurements, not just dog measurements. The logic in our online size guide guide applies double for pets: show the actual sweater's chest and length with a tolerance, and tell the customer to measure the dog. The brands that do this cut their pet-apparel returns dramatically.

Design details that decide whether it sells

Pet knitwear looks like a shrunken human sweater until you wear-test it on an actual dog, at which point the functional details take over:

  • The harness opening. The single most requested feature — a bound slit or buttoned flap at the shoulder blades so a leash harness sits under the sweater. Buyers now expect it; put it in the spec at sampling, not as a bulk-stage change.
  • Belly closure. Velcro or button straps under the belly beat pull-over comfort for older dogs and for walks in bad weather, and they forgive measurement error — worth offering as an alternate fit block.
  • Leg straps and leg holes. Leg openings need a clean, non-chafing finish — we bind them the way we finish armholes on baby knits. Rear leg straps keep the sweater from riding up on deep-chested dogs.
  • High-visibility and reflective details. A large share of dog sweaters is bought for dark-evening walks; reflective yarn trims or piping are a cheap upgrade with a real story.
  • Keep the trim count low. Every pompom, button and bow is a chew risk. The safety logic from our children's knitwear compliance guide — small parts securely attached, nothing that detaches under reasonable force — maps directly onto pet product, because a dog tests hardware more aggressively than any toddler.

Yarns and finishing: washability beats everything

Dog sweaters get washed constantly and dried carelessly, so the fibre choice is dominated by practicality. Machine-washable wool and washable blends — the categories we cover in our machine-washable wool guide — are the premium tier; acrylic and cotton-acrylic dominate the value tier because they survive hot washes and impatient drying. Whatever the fibre, two performance requirements are non-negotiable: pilling resistance, because claws and harnesses abrade fabric in ways human garments never experience (see our pilling prevention guide), and colourfastness, because a wet red sweater on a white dog is a customer-service incident, not a quality footnote. We always run a wash test on pet samples at 40°C before approving bulk — the same discipline as our general sample wash test, just with more mud in the brief.

One fibre note specific to pets: avoid hairy, shedding yarns — angora-type fibres transfer onto the animal and the sofa. A tight, low-hair yarn is not just a durability choice; it prevents the sweater from depositing fluff into a dog's coat, which groomers complain about loudly and publicly.

Production and commercial notes

The good news: pet garments are small, so per-piece yarn consumption is low and a lot of pieces come off the same machine hours as a human programme — which makes honest low-MOQ production easier to quote than in adult knitwear. The complications are scale-driven: six sizes times four colourways fragments the order quickly, so lock the size-colour matrix before cutting yarn, and consider a ratio plan per size — large sizes routinely outsell expectations in the pet market because the small-dog bias in marketing is not matched by the dog population. Packaging matters more than in human knitwear: pet retail wants header cards or boxed product with the size chart printed on it, so treat packaging as part of the product spec from the first quote, the approach we recommend in our general packaging and shipping guide.

Seasonally, pet knitwear follows the gifting calendar hard: it is a top-three Christmas gifting category, which means the buying calendar deadlines are earlier than buyers expect, and a February re-order season follows for the January sales and the "dog's birthday" trade that DTC brands have successfully invented.

Frequently asked questions

Is pet apparel subject to the same labelling rules as human clothing?

Generally no — pet products sit outside the textile fibre-content and care labelling regimes that govern human apparel in the EU, US and UK. That said, most serious retailers ask for voluntary fibre content and care labelling anyway, and pet products are still covered by general product safety rules (secure trims, no toxic finishes), so we recommend treating RSL chemical compliance — see our restricted substances guide — as fully applicable. A dog chews its sweater; test accordingly.

How many sizes do I actually need?

Four to six. A six-size run from roughly 30cm to 70cm chest girth covers most of the market from chihuahuas to labradors; a four-size run (XS–L) is enough for a first collection if you would rather put the money into colour and photography than breadth. Skip XXL unless you are deliberately targeting giant breeds — the tail of the size curve sells slowly and the volume goes to the middle sizes.

Can you make a mini-me version of a human style we already produce?

Usually yes, and it is the most efficient pet brief there is — the yarn, colour and trim decisions are already made, and the pet version is a re-cut block rather than a new development. The two things that do not shrink well are trims and necklines: buttons and zippers usually get replaced with safer closures for pets, and the neckline gets cut deeper and wider than proportional scaling suggests, because a dog's head-to-neck ratio differs from a human's more than most brands expect.
