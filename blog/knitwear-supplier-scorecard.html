How to Build a Supplier Scorecard for Knitwear

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

A supplier scorecard is a one-page quarterly review with four to six measurable categories — on-time delivery, quality (defect or first-pass yield), responsiveness, accuracy of the first sample, and flexibility on changes — each weighted and scored from the same data you already have in emails, inspection reports and freight documents. It takes an hour per supplier per quarter, and it replaces the vague feeling of "they're pretty good" with something you can compare across factories, defend to your management, and show the supplier.

Most knitwear buyers evaluate suppliers exactly once — at the beginning, through vetting and audits — and then never again. After that, performance lives in anecdotes: the late delivery everyone remembers, the sample revision nobody counted. We are on the receiving end of these evaluations, and the buyers who run a simple scorecard get demonstrably better service from us, because we know what is being measured and who is watching. Here's how to build one that works.

Why one-page beats one-spreadsheet-per-factory

The failure mode of scorecards is over-engineering. A template with 47 line items gets filled in twice and abandoned. What actually survives is something a merchandiser can update in fifteen minutes after each order. We recommend five categories, and no more:

  • On-time delivery (25%). Ship date versus confirmed date, measured at the factory gate, not at your warehouse — that way port delays and freight issues stay in your forwarder's scorecard, not the factory's.
  • Quality (25%). Use results you already pay for: AQL inspection outcomes, defect counts from defect classifications, and any customer returns traced to manufacturing.
  • Sample accuracy (20%). How many rounds from brief to approved sample? A factory that nails the first sample saves you more money than a factory that is 3% cheaper.
  • Communication (15%). Response time on material questions, proactive warning of problems, quality of the weekly status update. Subjective, yes — but grade it 1–5 with one sentence of evidence and it stays honest.
  • Flexibility and problem-solving (15%). How they behaved when you made order changes, or when something went wrong and needed rework. Suppliers show their real character in the exceptions, not the routine.

The numbers that matter in knitwear specifically

Generic apparel scorecards miss what is special about knits. Adjust for our trade:

  • Measure quality per thousand units, not per order. A 500-piece and an 8,000-piece order should be comparable. Defects per thousand garments is the honest denominator.
  • Track colour-pass rate across dye lots. If a factory passes shade approval on the first lab dip 80% of the time versus another's 50%, that difference compounds across every colour in your range. It is the knitwear equivalent of a free throw percentage.
  • Count measurement stability, not just compliance. A garment that passes measurement spec at the edge of tolerance every season is riskier than one that lands centred. Trend the average, not just pass/fail.
  • Separate development speed from production speed. Some factories sample brilliantly and ship late; others the reverse. Collapsing both into "lead time" hides which half of your pipeline each supplier strengthens.

We see this a lot: a buyer drops a factory over one late shipment, without noticing the same factory had a 99% first-pass inspection rate over three seasons. The scorecard's real job is to prevent exactly that kind of single-event decision-making — in both directions.

Running the review without making it a ritual

  • Cadence: quarterly, with an annual roll-up. More often and the data is noisy; less often and nobody remembers context.
  • Score from documents, not memory. Inspection reports, courier logs, your own inbox. If a category has no data source, either create one or drop the category.
  • Show the supplier the card — including the good scores. A supplier who sees they are 4/5 on communication but 2/5 on sample accuracy knows precisely what to fix. This conversation, held twice a year, is worth more than a third-party audit for everyday performance.
  • Decide the thresholds in advance. For example: two consecutive quarters below 60% triggers a formal corrective plan; below 50% triggers dual-sourcing. Written before the emotions, applied after the facts.
  • Let results inform order allocation. The scorecard only changes behaviour if it changes order volume. Your best supplier should see the benefit; your marginal one should see the risk.

One caution: scorecards reward what you measure, and knitwear supply chains punish metric-gaming. If you weight on-time delivery too heavily, you will get on-time shipments of goods that should have been held for quality. Keep quality's weight at least equal to delivery, always.

From scorecard to sourcing strategy

After four or five quarters, the numbers start answering strategic questions: which supplier deserves your growth volume, which belongs in the capacity plan for complex styles versus basics, and where a second source genuinely reduces risk rather than just splitting attention. It also feeds the harder conversations — pricing reviews, negotiations, and eventually decisions about trading companies versus factories — with evidence instead of instinct.

For teams, the scorecard is also the cheapest training tool that exists. A new merchandiser inherits two years of scored history and immediately knows which factory needs chasing on samples and which one needs early yarn bookings. It is institutional memory that survives staff turnover, which — as anyone who has lost a supplier relationship when a colleague resigned will tell you — is worth the hour per quarter on its own.

Frequently asked questions

How many suppliers should I scorecard?

Every supplier you reorder from. The exercise pays for itself on the second order, because the data already exists in your inspection reports and correspondence. If you source from five or more factories, rank them and spend your review hour on the top and bottom of the table — those conversations change outcomes; the middle manages itself.

Should delivery be measured at the factory or at my warehouse?

At the factory gate. Measuring arrival at your warehouse punishes suppliers for port congestion, customs queues and forwarder errors that are outside their control — and blurs accountability exactly where you need it sharp. Keep a separate simple scorecard for your forwarder if freight performance is a recurring issue.

What is a good on-time delivery rate for a knitwear factory?

Above 90% on confirmed dates is a healthy target, with the caveat that "confirmed" must mean the last agreed date, not the original quotation date — you have to own your own change orders honestly. Below 75% across a season, with no external cause, is a structural problem that a corrective plan needs to address rather than a scorecard note.
