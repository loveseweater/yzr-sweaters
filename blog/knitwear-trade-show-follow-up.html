After the Trade Show: How Buyers Turn Knitwear Supplier Contacts Into Orders

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

Send the first follow-up within 48 hours of the meeting, with one concrete action attached — a sample offer, a price, or a call slot — instead of a general thank-you. Then run the lead through three stages in order: sample, quotation, factory verification. Contacts pushed through those steps on a fixed cadence turn into orders far more often than contacts who receive a single "nice to meet you" email.

A trade show produces contacts, not orders. The distance between them is covered in the weeks after the halls empty, and by rhythm rather than luck. Attaching one specific next action to a fixed schedule moves conversations into sampling, quoting and orders; a polite message does not.

Why do most trade show follow-ups fail?

Three failure modes kill most leads. The mass mailing: one generic note sent to forty booths, which reads exactly like what it is. No next step: the email asks for nothing, so replying has no purpose. And the memory problem: by the time the supplier answers, the buyer cannot place them.

What should the first follow-up email contain?

Every follow-up should carry one concrete action the recipient can accept: send a sample, prepare a price, or book a call. "Looking forward to cooperating" is not an action.

  • A subject line with the reference point — company name, booth or hall, and the product discussed.
  • One line that proves you remember the conversation — the colour they asked about, the gauge problem they raised, their market.
  • The photo you took at the booth — the reference image makes the conversation instantly recognisable.
  • The ask, with a date — "I can courier two samples this week; confirm the address."

Keep it to one question. A single question is easy to answer; four get ignored.

What is the 48-hour rule, and what comes after?

Send the first message within 48 hours of the meeting, while the conversation is fresh, then hold a cadence:

  • Day 0–2: thank-you plus one action — a sample offer, a price indication or a call slot.
  • Day 5–7: no reply yet? Send something useful — sample photos, a spec question, a first costed option.
  • Day 14–21: a note carrying new information — a similar style finished, a yarn back in stock, a window opening.
  • Beyond that: drop to a monthly or seasonal touch. Three messages that each carried real information is a sensible limit.

Persistence for its own sake achieves nothing; the aim is to be visible when the buyer's calendar opens a sourcing slot.

How do you use samples to turn interest into action?

A sample moves a conversation out of email and onto the buyer's desk, where it gets shown to colleagues and compared with existing styles. Verbal interest costs nothing to give and nothing to withdraw; a garment in hand does not.

Sampling — producing a garment to specification before bulk — is normally charged, because it uses yarn, machine time and labour. The fee is commonly credited against the bulk order if it proceeds, so confirm that in writing before sampling starts rather than arguing later. State the sample lead time, the date you can ship, and whether the buyer's tech pack (the technical file covering measurements, construction and trims) is complete. When should you send a quotation?

Quote too early and you will revise the price three times; each revision costs credibility. Confirm first what drives knitwear cost: style and construction, yarn and composition, quantity per colourway, size ratio, packaging, and the delivery window with its Incoterm — the International Commercial Terms that set who pays freight, insurance and duties. If the buyer's quantity sits under your MOQ (minimum order quantity), say so with the price attached to your smallest workable run.

Where the buyer cannot supply all of it, price the gaps as explicit assumptions: "based on 300 pieces per colour in a 12GG acrylic-wool blend, FOB Shenzhen". Then quote quickly once the specification is real. Our notes on how to read a sweater quotation show the cost blocks buyers compare.

When should you offer a factory visit or video walkthrough?

Not in the first email. Offer verification once the buyer asks about capacity, lead times or certification — the signs of a real program. A video walkthrough of the knitting floor and finishing area costs the buyer twenty minutes; a physical visit signals you expect the relationship to last. Have the evidence ready first: certification reports from partner factories (BSCI — Business Social Compliance Initiative; SEDEX — Supplier Ethical Data Exchange; Oeko-Tex — textile chemical safety testing), and a plain offer of third-party inspection by SGS or BV. How do you keep a lead warm without becoming noise?

Most contacts from a show are months from a sourcing decision. Keep the line warm with material that is useful on its own: a seasonal reminder tied to their planning calendar, one development note per season, and a genuine question about what is selling for them. One substantive message a month is plenty.

A simple lead grading method

Grade every contact on intent and scale.

  • A — asked for samples or prices, or a known brand: follow up personally within 48 hours, then weekly until a decision or a clear no.
  • B — a real conversation, no request yet: follow up in the first week, then every three to four weeks.
  • C — cards with no conversation: seasonal email list only.

Re-grade after every reply. A C contact who asks about a style is an A immediately.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a buyer wait for a supplier to follow up after a trade show?

Two working days is the practical standard. A buyer who is genuinely interested normally replies within that window, so a supplier with no answer after 48 hours should send one short nudge with new information rather than repeating the first message. Silence after two attempts usually means the contact was not qualified in the first place.

Is a sample fee refundable when a knitwear order is placed?

In knitwear it is common practice to charge for samples, because yarn, machine time and labour are consumed, and just as common to credit that fee against the bulk order once it is placed. The credit is not automatic, so confirm it in writing before sampling starts. Sample freight is usually treated separately and is often absorbed by the buyer on larger sample sets.

How many follow-up emails are acceptable before a lead goes cold?

Three messages that each carry something new — a sample photo, a price, a production window — spread over roughly three weeks is a reasonable sequence. After that, move the contact to a seasonal list rather than continuing a weekly chase. Repeated "just checking in" emails add nothing and make a supplier easier to forget, not harder.
