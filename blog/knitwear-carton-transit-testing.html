Carton Transit Testing for Knitwear: Drop, Stack and Crush Before a Container Does It For You

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Transit testing puts a finished, packed carton through the abuse it will actually meet — drops, stacking, vibration and humidity — before you ship thousands of them. For knitwear it is worth doing once per packaging spec, not once per order. The usual reference points are ISTA 2A or 3A and ASTM D4169. What buyers should specify is the carton grade (ECT or burst strength), the piece count per carton, the maximum stack height, and who supplies the test report.

A sweater is not fragile. A carton full of sweaters, stacked six high in a humid container for four weeks, is a different story. We see a particular kind of claim every year: the garments were fine when they left the factory, and arrive with flattened ribs, pressure marks on the shoulder, or a grey scuff from the corrugated board. Almost none of that is a knitting fault. It is a packaging test that nobody ran.

What actually damages knitwear in transit

Three mechanisms, and they compound each other:

  • Compression. Knitwear is light and bulky, so cartons are big and the instinct is to fill them to the brim to protect the cube. A box that is loaded to its limit and then sits under a stack for weeks slowly loses height. The bottom layer takes the load and the garments inside it take the shape of whatever is pressing on them — often the fold of the garment above, or the edge of a polybag.
  • Humidity. Corrugated board is hygroscopic. A carton that comfortably holds a stack in a dry warehouse holds considerably less after days at high relative humidity, and container interiors can be damp on a long voyage. This is also where the mould and mildew problem starts; our notes on mould in stored knitwear cover the moisture side in detail.
  • Vibration and handling. Road legs, terminal transfers and forklift handling loosen folds, work carton corners, and rub board against fabric. Fine-gauge and light-coloured knits show scuffing most.

None of these are dramatic on their own. Together, over a container of six hundred cartons, they produce a returns rate that looks like a quality problem at the factory and is really a packaging problem.

The four tests worth paying for

You do not need a laboratory programme for every style. Four checks on the final packaging spec cover most of the risk:

  • Drop test. Drop a fully packed carton from a set height onto its corners, edges and faces. This catches weak glue joints, undersized board and a closure that bursts. It is cheap and brutally informative — a carton that fails a corner drop will fail a forklift.
  • Compression / stacking test. A compression tester loads the carton until it deflects or fails, giving a box compression value (BCT). Compare that against the load it will actually see: carton weight times the number stacked on top of it, with a safety factor for humidity. This is the one number that tells you whether six-high stacking is realistic.
  • Vibration. A vibration table simulates the road and rail legs. It is most useful for spotting internal movement — garments shifting inside a half-empty carton, or a polybag rubbing a printed surface.
  • Conditioning. Test the same carton after a spell in a humid environment, not only fresh from the dry store. Skipping this step is the most common reason a test result does not match reality.

Two board specifications come up in supplier conversations. ECT (edge crush test) measures the stacking strength of the corrugated board itself; burst strength (often quoted in pounds per square inch) is the older measure of resistance to puncture. ECT is the more useful one for stacking. Ask which grade the factory is quoting and in what unit — we have seen a 32 ECT carton described as heavy duty by one supplier and standard by another.

Why knitwear cartons fail more often than other garments

Denim is dense; a carton of jeans reaches its weight limit long before it reaches its volume limit. Sweaters are the opposite. You can fill a large carton and still be nowhere near a weight limit, so the temptation is to compress more pieces in, or to use a bigger, lighter-grade box to protect the freight cube. Both are rational decisions that quietly remove the carton's structural margin.

Two more knitwear-specific habits make it worse. Vacuum packing saves cube but sets a hard, flat crease into rib and cable structures that steam pressing at destination does not always remove; we see this a lot on chunky gauge orders. And mixed size ratios — a carton with a few XXL pieces on top of many small ones — create an uneven load that no compression test on a perfectly packed sample will predict. Our guides to packaging and shipping and container choice explain how carton dimensions drive the freight bill, which is exactly why this trade-off exists.

Specifying it without over-engineering

Most buyers do not need to run the tests themselves. What they need is a packaging specification that makes the test meaningful and a supplier that will stand behind it. In the purchase order, state:

  • Carton grade and construction — ECT value, single or double wall, and whether a liner or divider is required.
  • Pieces per carton and the size/colour breakdown — a fixed count prevents the end-of-run habit of topping up a half-empty box to save a carton.
  • Maximum stack height in the container and in the destination warehouse, with a humidity allowance.
  • Test report — which lab, which protocol (ISTA 2A, ISTA 3A or ASTM D4169), which carton and load it was run on, and the date. A report for a different carton size is not evidence for yours.
  • When to re-test — after any change of carton supplier, piece count, polybag or vacuum packing.

One test per packaging spec, repeated when the spec changes, is enough for most programmes. Where a retailer has its own routing guide, follow that instead; our piece on vendor compliance describes how those requirements usually arrive.

Who pays, and what it protects

A third-party transit test is a modest line item — far less than a single chargeback or a re-press at destination — and it is normally a one-off cost on a new packaging spec rather than a per-shipment charge. Decide in the PO whether the factory covers it as part of development or the buyer commissions it. Either is fine; what matters is that the report exists before the first container, not after a claim.

It also protects both sides in an argument. If a shipment arrives crushed, a passing test report plus photos from loading supervision and a sound cargo insurance claim file tells you quickly whether the failure happened in the carton, in the stuffing, or in transit. Without them, everyone guesses. Frequently asked questions

Do I need a transit test for every knitwear shipment?

No. Run it once per packaging specification — the same carton grade, piece count, polybag and fold method — and repeat when any of those change. A report on a different carton size or a differently packed box does not cover your shipment. Retailers with their own routing guide may specify a protocol and frequency, in which case follow theirs.

What is the difference between ECT and burst strength?

ECT measures the edge crush resistance of the corrugated board, which predicts how well a carton survives stacking. Burst strength measures resistance to puncture and is the older specification. For knitwear, where the risk is long-term stacking in a humid container rather than a sharp impact, ECT is the more useful number to agree with your factory.

Why do the bottom cartons arrive flattened but the top ones look fine?

Because stacking load accumulates downward. The bottom carton carries the weight of every carton above it, and corrugated board loses strength as it absorbs moisture. That combination, not a single bad box, is what crushes the lowest layer. A compression test with a humidity-conditioned sample and a written stack limit is the practical fix.
