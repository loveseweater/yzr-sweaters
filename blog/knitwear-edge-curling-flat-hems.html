Why Knit Hems Curl: Specifying Edges That Lie Flat

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Single-jersey fabric has a built-in tendency to roll at a free edge, because the loops on the two faces do not balance each other. In a fully fashioned sweater the rib border usually controls it; in cut-and-sew knits, or wherever a designer wants a clean turned edge, it has to be engineered — with a rib or double-bed border, a linked or turned hem, the right edge tension, and proper relaxation and pressing before cutting. Specify the edge construction in the tech pack. A hem that curls after the first wash is almost never a washing fault, and no amount of ironing fixes it for good.

We see this a lot on lightweight viscose and cotton summer knits: the sample looks perfect on the table, then the first wash-test piece comes back with the hem rolled up like a scroll. The buyer assumes the washing was too rough. It was not. The edge was never constructed to stay flat, and the wash simply released the tension that had been holding it. Here is what is actually going on and how to prevent it at the spec stage.

What curling is, and what it is not

Curling is local to an edge: the fabric rolls toward one face along a cut or free boundary. It is a different problem from three things it gets confused with. Spirality is the whole panel rotating out of square, driven by yarn twist. Shrinkage is a change in dimensions. Poor recovery is a hem that grows and stays stretched. Those need different fixes. Curling shows up at the free edge itself — bottom hem, armhole, neckline of a tank, the open edge of a knit dress — and it is visible on the flat table as well as after washing.

Why single jersey rolls

A plain knitted loop is not symmetrical. On the face you see the sides of the loops, on the back the tops and bottoms, and the yarn has stored bending energy from being formed into that shape. In the middle of a panel the neighbouring loops hold each other in balance. At a free edge there is nothing on one side to balance the force, so the fabric relieves it by rolling. It is the same reason a cut piece of single jersey curls the moment it leaves the machine, long before any customer touches it.

Structures with balanced faces do not do this. A 1x1 or 2x2 rib alternates face and back loops in the same course, so the forces cancel — which is precisely why ribs have been the default sweater border for a century. Interlock and double-bed constructions behave the same way. Our ribbed knit guide covers how those borders are specified and what they do to the silhouette.

The constructions that hold an edge flat

  • Rib border. The reliable answer. Knit the last few centimetres in 1x1 or 2x2 rib on the same machine, and the edge stays put. It adds a visible design line and a little weight, which is why buyers sometimes resist it on a minimal style.
  • Turned or folded hem with linking. The fabric is folded back and secured loop-for-loop, giving a clean, rib-free edge. It needs enough depth to resist rolling and skilled linking to keep it flat; a shallow turn just rolls again. See knitwear seam types for how that join is built.
  • Tubular or double-bed border. Common on fine-gauge summer pieces where a rib would look too heavy. Two fabric layers are knitted as a tube at the edge, so the faces balance without a visible rib.
  • Tighter tension at the edge. A few courses worked at a shorter loop length stiffen the boundary. Useful as a support, rarely sufficient on its own for a truly free edge.

Every one of these changes the look, the weight and the cost. Decide it at design stage, not in a factory email two days before cutting.

Finishing decides whether it stays flat

Knitted panels come off the machine under tension and need to relax before they are cut, measured or pressed. Cut a panel straight from the machine and it will move afterwards — and the edge moves most. Let the fabric rest, wash or steam it, then press without stretching, then measure. Over-stretching a hem during pressing is a classic own goal: it looks flat under the iron and rolls back as soon as the garment cools. Our pressing and finishing guide covers the sequence, and the cutting room explains why relaxation belongs before the cut. Fully fashioned panels, knitted to shape with a rib already attached, have fewer free edges to worry about in the first place.

What to put in the spec

  • Name the edge construction explicitly — rib type and depth, or turned hem depth and linking method. "Clean finish" is not a specification.
  • State the edge tension or stitch reference, and the relaxation time before cutting.
  • Require the check after washing, not only on the table. Our sample wash-test protocol takes an afternoon and catches this before bulk.
  • Set a tolerance for hem flatness in the quality standards document, so an inspector knows what to reject.
  • Be extra careful on fine-gauge viscose, cotton and sleeveless styles — summer knits, knit dresses and tanks are where we see it most, because the fabric is light and the armholes are free edges.

Where it shows up in real orders

The pattern is almost always the same: a designer wants a minimal, rib-free edge on a lightweight style; the factory knits a plain single-jersey panel and secures it with a shallow overlock; the sample passes on the table; the wash-test piece curls. Fixing it at that point means re-knitting the border, not re-pressing the sample. We would rather have the argument about rib versus turned hem at the tech-pack stage — see the design brief — than after the first sample has been approved.

Frequently asked questions

Will the curl wash out over time?

No. Curling is structural, caused by unbalanced loops at a free edge. Washing and pressing may relax it briefly and it will roll back. The fix is a balanced border construction — rib, turned hem or double-bed edge — specified before the sample is knitted.

Is curling the same as spirality?

No. Curling is local to an edge and comes from the loop structure at a free boundary. Spirality is the whole panel rotating out of square, driven mainly by yarn twist and finishing. They are tested and prevented differently, so check which one you are looking at before changing the yarn spec.

Can pressing fix it permanently?

Only temporarily. Heat and steam relax the yarn, but the stored bending energy is still there, so the edge rolls back after cooling or the first wash. Pressing is part of a correct finishing sequence, not a substitute for a properly constructed border.
