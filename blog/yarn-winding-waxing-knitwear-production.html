Winding and Waxing Yarn Before Knitting: The Step Nobody Puts in the Tech Pack

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Winding transfers yarn from the spinner's package onto a cone the knitting machine can actually run, clears thick and thin faults on the way, sets the winding tension and often applies a light paraffin or emulsion lubricant. Get it wrong and you get broken ends, barre, tight and loose panels and a machine that stops every few minutes. Buyers rarely specify it — but they can ask four useful questions about it before bulk starts.

A tech pack will usually say the yarn count, the fibre, the gauge and the colour. It almost never says how that yarn was prepared for the machine. That is fair — winding is the factory's job, not the designer's. But when a buyer asks us why a bulk lot has faint horizontal bands, or why a style that knitted fine in sampling suddenly ran slow in production, the answer is quite often a cone, not a stitch.

What winding actually does

Yarn does not arrive from the spinner in a form a flat knitting machine wants. It may come on small tubes, in hanks, or on dye packages after colour work. Winding is the transfer onto a clean, consistent cone or cheese, and four things happen during that pass:

  • Clearing. An electronic or mechanical clearer detects thick places, thin places and foreign matter and cuts them out. This is the last chance to remove a slub or a nep before it becomes a visible fault in the fabric — our guide to yarn faults lists what a good incoming check should catch.
  • Joining. Every cut is replaced by a knot or splice. Too many joins in one cone means the yarn was poor to begin with; a badly made join is a guaranteed end break at the needle.
  • Build and tension. The machine winds the package at a controlled tension and with a specific traverse pattern, so the yarn pays off smoothly. A cone wound hard at the core and soft at the outside will feed differently at the start and end of the run, and knitting tension changes with it — see our notes on loop length and tension.
  • Lubrication. A wax disc or an emulsion applies a small, controlled amount of lubricant as the yarn passes. This is the part most buyers never hear about and the part that matters most for knitwear.

The output package matters too. Conical packages suit high-speed unwinding; the taper, the angle and the net weight are chosen for the machine and the style. A factory running the same yarn on a 12GG and a 3GG programme may wind it differently for each.

Why wax matters more in knitting than in weaving

In knitting, a single yarn is pulled around guide eyes, tensioners, feeders and then bent around a moving needle hook to form a loop. That is a lot of friction on a strand that may be hairy, low-twist wool or cashmere. A very thin film of paraffin or a water-soluble emulsion reduces that friction enough to keep the loop formation consistent and the ends from breaking. It is not a cosmetic treatment; it is a process aid, and most of it is removed in the washing and finishing that follow.

The dose is the whole game. Too little and the machine stops for broken ends, especially on coarse woollen-spun yarns and in dry winter air. Too much and wax builds up in needle grooves and guide channels, which shows up as dropped stitches, dirty marks and — after dyeing or washing — patchy shade where the deposits resisted liquor penetration. On white and pastel knits, we have seen a heavy wax application read as a faint grey cast that no amount of re-washing fully cleared. The honest answer to how much is right is that it depends on the fibre, the twist, the gauge and the humidity on the floor; a good technician adjusts it by watching the knitting, not by a fixed number in a manual.

Not every yarn needs the same treatment. Woollen-spun and hairy luxury fibres generally do; smooth, plied worsted and continuous-filament synthetics often need little or none. If your style uses a fancy or brushed yarn, the finishing route described in our brushed finishes guide interacts with whatever is left on the surface.

The defects that trace back to this step

When a bulk lot looks wrong, buyers usually suspect the dye lot or the gauge. Four of the common alternatives are winding faults:

  • Barre or horizontal banding across a panel, where the yarn from one cone knitted slightly tighter than the next. Our fabric defects guide covers barre and needle lines in detail; mixed cone tension is one of its quieter causes.
  • End breaks and holes clustered around a specific machine or a specific cone, usually a bad splice or a hard-wound package that will not pay off cleanly.
  • Spirality or twist in the finished garment that appears after washing, because the yarn went into the knit with residual torque from winding. Our piece on spirality explains the mechanism from the fabric side.
  • Shade variation within a single garment where a wax-heavy section took up dye differently. It looks like a colour problem and gets argued about as a colour problem — see colour consistency for how we separate those.

None of these are visible in a photo of a finished sweater hanging on a rail, which is why we ask buyers to look at a washed, full-size knitted panel rather than a swatch. Our sample wash-test protocol is a cheap way to catch them before bulk.

What a buyer can actually specify

You should not be dictating winding tension or wax pickup — that is the mill's craft, and a factory that lets a buyer set it is probably not running a very controlled process. What you can do is ask four questions that tell you whether the step is being managed:

  • Is the yarn cleared, and what is the splice count per cone? A supplier who tracks this can tell you when a yarn lot is behaving badly before it reaches the knitting floor.
  • What lubricant is used, and is it removable in the normal wash? You want a yes on removable, especially for light shades and for any garment that will be garment-dyed. Our garment dyeing and finishing notes explain why residues matter there.
  • Are cone labels retained by dye lot? If a fault appears in bulk, traceability back to a specific cone and lot is the difference between a one-hour fix and a whole shipment held. It is the same discipline as yarn stock planning.
  • Will the same preparation be used for the reorder? A first order wound from one dye package and a reorder wound from another is a classic source of the hand-feel difference buyers complain about six months later.

It is also worth remembering that this step sits inside your yarn cost and your lead time, not on top of them. Winding is a few hours of machine work, but a poorly prepared lot can cost a shift of stoppages on a floor that is already booked solid — the situation our capacity planning article describes from the scheduling side. Frequently asked questions

Does wax stay on the finished sweater?

Most of it does not. Paraffin and water-soluble emulsions are process aids that reduce friction during knitting, and the washing and finishing stages remove the great majority. Problems arise only when the application was too heavy, which can leave deposits that affect dye uptake or hand feel. Ask whether the lubricant is removable in the normal wash, especially for pale shades and garment-dyed styles.

Can I specify the winding tension in my tech pack?

We would advise against it. Winding tension, cone build and wax pickup are chosen by the mill for the fibre, twist, gauge and machine, and they are adjusted by a technician watching the knitting. What a buyer should specify is the outcome: cleared yarn, a removable lubricant, cone labels traceable by dye lot, and the same preparation used on the reorder.

Is winding the reason my bulk fabric looks different from the approved sample?

It is one of the possible reasons, alongside a new dye lot, a different gauge setting or a different finishing route. Cone-to-cone tension variation, a changed splice count and residual torque from winding all show up as banding, holes or spirality after washing. A washed, full-size knitted panel from the actual bulk yarn is the fastest way to tell which one you are dealing with.
