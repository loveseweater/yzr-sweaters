Knitwear Production Lead Times: How Long a Sweater Order Really Takes

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

A standard bulk sweater order — stock yarn, straightforward construction, 500 to 3,000 pieces — typically runs 45 to 60 days from purchase order to goods ready, plus shipping. Custom-dyed yarn adds two to three weeks. Peak season (June to October) stretches everything by 10 to 20 days because knitting capacity, not enthusiasm, is the constraint. If a quote promises 30 days for a custom-yarn order in September, treat it the way you'd treat a price that seems too good: ask which part is being optimistically left out.

Buyers usually plan around the lead time on the quote, then discover the number was optimistic, or true only if everything before production went perfectly. We'd rather walk you through where the weeks actually go. Once you can see the stages, you can see which ones you control, which ones your factory controls, and which ones belong to the spinner in another province. That distinction decides whether your container ships before your launch date.

Where the days actually go

For a typical mid-size order in regular season, here is the honest breakdown we work to:

  • Yarn sourcing and QC: 7–20 days. Stock yarn from our own or a partner warehouse: about a week including incoming checks for yarn faults. Custom-dyed yarn: 15–20 days, because the spinner runs a dye lot with its own schedule. This is the single most variable stage, and the one most quotes quietly assume will be fast.
  • Knitting: 10–20 days. Panels are knitted across machine capacity. A style with fine gauge knits slower per piece than a chunky 3-gauge one, despite what intuition says — more loops per centimetre. Jacquard and multi-colour work add machine time.
  • Linking and seaming: 7–15 days. The most labour-intensive stage and the one that most often becomes the bottleneck, because good linkers are skilled and scarce. Linking quality is also where the garment's durability is decided, so this is the wrong place to rush.
  • Washing, pressing, finishing: 5–10 days. Including the relaxation time that makes final measurements meaningful — see our notes on pressing and finishing.
  • QC and packing: 3–7 days. Inline checks run during production, but final inspection to your AQL standard, needle detection, labelling and carton packing happen here. If you are booking third-party inspection — which we recommend — their availability adds days too.

Add those up and the honest total for a smooth order is 32 to 72 production days, before a single day of freight. The quotes you see at the lower end are real, but they describe the best case: stock yarn, approved samples, no capacity queue and no changes.

Yarn is usually the bottleneck — plan it first

The most common way a delivery slips is not the factory being slow; it's the yarn not existing yet. If your colour isn't a stock shade, someone has to spin and dye it, and minimums apply — our notes on yarn minimums and colour choice cover the trade-offs. Wool prices also move, which is worth remembering when you schedule a re-order; yarn price volatility sometimes makes factories hesitate to pre-buy speculative stock, adding days while the commercial side is settled.

Two habits keep yarn from eating your calendar:

  • Approve colours via lab dips while yarn is being ordered, not after it arrives. Lab dip approval and dye lot production can overlap.
  • Ask the factory to reserve cones at PO stage. The practice of planning yarn stock turns a 20-day stage into a 7-day one for the styles where it matters most.

Peak season compresses everything

Knitwear production is seasonal in a way cotton jersey never is. Roughly June through October, every factory in Dongguan is knitting for autumn/winter deliveries, and the same machines that quoted you 50 days in March realistically need 60–70 in August. Capacity, linking labour and even pressing tables get congested at once.

The standard responses are capacity planning and booking machine capacity in advance — both work, but only if you use them before the season, not during it. Once you're inside peak season, the only remaining lever is priority, and priority is priced. The other fixed disruption is Chinese New Year, which takes a bite out of both January and February; our guide to planning around Chinese New Year shutdowns sets out which dates actually matter.

Why rush orders cost more — the honest arithmetic

When a factory "rushes" your order, it does not knit faster. It displaces someone else's order, adds overtime shifts, and often re-sequences finishing so your goods go to the pressing table ahead of work that was there first. You are buying queue position, and you pay for it in:

  • A rush surcharge — typically 5–15% depending on how much of the schedule has to be rebuilt.
  • Overtime rates on linking, where the labour cost concentrates.
  • Air freight when the date can no longer be met by sea — the comparison in our notes on air versus sea freight shows how quickly that dwarfs everything else.

We see this a lot with buyers who lose two weeks in sample approvals and then ask production to recover them. It can be done — sometimes — but a rush surcharge plus air freight usually costs more than the margin on the goods. The cheaper rush order is the one you never need: approvals on schedule, comments consolidated, and order changes made before yarn is committed rather than after.

A realistic timeline you can plan against

Here is how a well-run order actually sequences, counted from the purchase order:

  • Day 0: PO and deposit received; yarn reserved or ordered; tech pack locked.
  • Day 10–14: Pre-production sample made from bulk yarn, submitted, approved. Production does not responsibly start before this — the exceptions are the factories that skip PP samples, and you can read about how that ends in any QC claim.
  • Day 14–45: Knitting, linking, washing and pressing, with inline QC running.
  • Day 45–55: Final inspection, needle detection, labels and trims verified, packing to your packaging spec.
  • Day 55+: Freight — add 3–7 days by air, 25–40 days by sea to Europe or North America, and customs clearance at destination.

Work backwards from your on-sale date, not your shipment date, and the calendar usually tells you when the PO needs to be placed. Our notes on building a buying calendar turn that into a routine.

Frequently asked questions

What is a typical lead time for a bulk sweater order?

For 500 to 3,000 pieces in stock yarn with approved samples, plan on 45 to 60 days from purchase order to goods ready, plus freight. Custom-dyed yarn adds two to three weeks, and peak season from June to October stretches the same order by 10 to 20 days. Anything dramatically faster should prompt the question of which stage is being assumed away.

Why does my factory quote 40 days in some months and 60 in others?

Because knitting capacity is seasonal. From roughly June to October the whole industry is producing autumn/winter deliveries, so machines, linking labour and finishing all queue. The same factory quoting 45 days in spring may honestly need 60–70 in August. Booking capacity in advance is the standard fix.

Can a rush order recover a missed sample approval?

Sometimes, but you pay twice: a rush surcharge on production, and usually air freight on top. Since a rush means your order displaces others and runs on overtime, the total often exceeds the margin saved. The cheaper alternative is protecting the approval calendar so the rush never becomes necessary.

Should production start before the pre-production sample is approved?

No. Bulk yarn should be committed and lab dips approved, but knitting the bulk before the PP sample is signed off means gambling the order on an unverified spec. Factories that offer to "start now, approve later" are quoting you a schedule built on skipping the step that catches mistakes while they are still cheap.
