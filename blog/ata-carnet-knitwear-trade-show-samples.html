ATA Carnets for Knitwear Samples: Taking One Sample Set to Three Trade Shows

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

An ATA Carnet is a temporary-import document that lets the same set of professional samples cross borders without paying duty or import VAT each time, for up to a year, across the countries that accept it. If one knitwear sample set travels to three shows in a season, a carnet usually beats declaring it as a commercial shipment at every border. The catch is that the carnet is a list: every item on it has to come back, and the samples cannot be sold, given away or left behind. Match the list to the suitcase exactly, keep every stamp, and close the carnet on return.

We pack sample sets for buyers heading to Première Vision, Magic and the regional shows, and the question that comes back most often is not about the garments — it is about the border. A courier shipment of samples is fine when they stay in one country. When the same set travels to three shows and comes home again, the paperwork changes. We see this a lot with first-time exhibitors; here is the version that actually works.

What a carnet actually is

ATA stands for Admission Temporaire / Temporary Admission. It is an international customs document, issued in China by CCPIT and backed by a chain of national guaranteeing organisations, that stands in for the normal import declaration and the duty or VAT deposit. It is valid for up to twelve months, can cover multiple trips and multiple member countries, and consists of counterfoils stamped at each exit and entry — see the shipping document set for where it sits alongside the commercial invoice and packing list. It is for professional samples and exhibition goods that will be re-exported, not for goods you intend to sell. If a buyer wants to keep a style, that is a separate import, with duty paid — our VAT, GST and de minimis guide explains the ordinary route.

When it pays for itself — and when it does not

  • Worth it: one sample set crossing several borders; high-value samples where repeated duty deposits would be painful; a set that returns to China between trips.
  • Not worth it: a single show in one country; samples you plan to leave with buyers; low-value sets where a courier declaration or a simple temporary import is cheaper — see sample shipping by courier.
  • Budget for the mechanics: the carnet fee, a guarantee or deposit with the issuing chamber, and processing time. Allow a week or two before the flight rather than sorting it at the airport. Some destinations add their own paperwork, so check the itinerary when you apply.

The sample list is the whole job

Most carnet problems start with a lazy item list. Customs officers compare the document to the physical set, so build it like an invoice:

  • One line per style: style number, description, fibre composition, quantity, unit value, total value and weight.
  • Realistic commercial values, not zero. Customs will assess their own if the declared value looks like an attempt to avoid the guarantee.
  • The physical set must match the list exactly at every border. Adding a swatch or leaving a garment behind breaks it.
  • Swatch cards, colour cards, trim cards, hangtags and labels go on the list too — they are goods, not packaging.
  • Photograph the packed set and keep a signed inventory with the carnet. It settles arguments faster than memory does.

What goes wrong at the border

  • A sample is given away or sold. The carnet cannot be discharged for that item, and duty, VAT and possibly a penalty land on the holder.
  • A missing stamp. Every export needs a matching re-import counterfoil. Miss one and the guarantee stays open; the chamber may claim against it.
  • Inconsistent values between the carnet, the commercial invoice and any courier paperwork for the same set.
  • The set travels as checked baggage. A carnet has to be presented to customs in person at each crossing; a courier shipment needs a different arrangement entirely.
  • Late return. Carnets expire. Re-import before the validity lapses and close the document with the issuing chamber — the follow-up work in trade show follow-up is a good moment to do it, not three months later.

How we pack a carnet sample set

One garment per style, neutral hangtags, a duplicate swatch card for the booth, a numbered inventory sheet with photos, and a spare copy of every document both in the suitcase and in the cloud. We also mark which styles are one-offs so nobody hands them out by mistake — a sample given away at a show is a real cost and one of the most common ways a carnet gets into trouble; sample types and the sample review checklist cover how we label them. If a buyer does want to keep a style, the clean answer is to ship a fresh one afterwards by courier rather than delete a line from the carnet at the airport. Frequently asked questions

Can I leave a sample with a buyer at the show?

Not under the carnet. Samples covered by a carnet must be re-exported. If a buyer wants to keep one, either remove it from the carnet and handle a normal import with duty paid, or ship a fresh sample separately after the show.

How long does a carnet last?

Up to twelve months from issue, and it can cover several trips within that period, subject to each country's rules. Re-import the goods before it expires and keep every counterfoil stamp so the chamber can discharge the guarantee.

Do I need one for a single trade show?

Often not. For one show in one country, a courier shipment with a proper temporary-import or sample declaration is usually simpler and cheaper. A carnet earns its keep when the same set crosses several borders, or when the declared value is high enough that repeated duty deposits would hurt.
