Colour Consistency Across a Knitwear Order: Shade Bands and Lots

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Quick answer

Colour is not a fixed value in knitted production — it varies slightly between dye lots, and a large order may be dyed across several lots. A shade band is the small tolerance range around an approved standard that those lots must fall inside. Buyers who understand this ask for the shade band up front, require lot segregation in packing, and plan reorders against the original lot; buyers who do not usually discover the issue when two shipments sit side by side at a store.

A colour approved on a lab dip is a target, not a guarantee. Between that dip and the garments arriving there are dye lots, machine variations, yarn source changes and finishing effects, each of which nudges the shade. The difference between a range that looks consistent on the shelf and one that does not is usually not the dyer's skill — it is whether the buyer set a shade band and planned around lot variation.

Why does colour vary at all?

Because dyeing is a chemical process with variables, and because the substrate is not perfectly uniform. Three sources account for most of the variation a buyer will see.

Dye lot variation. Each batch dyed in a vessel is a lot. Water quality, temperature control, dye concentration and vessel loading all shift slightly between runs, so two lots of the same recipe can differ perceptibly. This is normal and universal, not a sign of a bad dyer.

Fibre variation. Wool, cashmere and cotton from different origins or seasons take dye slightly differently, and a fibre source change mid-programme shifts the shade even with an identical recipe. Our notes on choosing a yarn supplier explain why consistency of supply matters beyond price.

Finishing effects. Washing, brushing and milling all change how colour reads on the finished surface, because they change the surface itself. A brushed knit and a flat knit in the same yarn and dye can look like different shades. Our notes on garment dyeing and finishing cover what those stages do.

What is a shade band, and how is it used?

A shade band is the permitted range of colour variation around an approved standard. The buyer approves a reference — a lab dip, a yarn sample or a physical standard — and the dyer is required to keep production lots inside a defined tolerance around it.

The band is normally expressed visually rather than numerically. A shade band card carries several small swatches spanning the acceptable range from slightly lighter to slightly darker, so a lot can be judged against it in daylight rather than against a single perfect target that nothing will match exactly.

Two practical points follow. First, the band should be agreed with the dyer before production, because the achievable band depends on the fibre and the colour — a pale shade and a saturated black behave very differently. Second, the band is only useful if it is visible at the point of judgement, which means having the reference and the production lot side by side under the same light. Our notes on lab dips and colour approval explain how the initial approval is done and why light source matters.

How should lots be handled within one order?

A single order large enough to exceed one dye lot will be produced across several. How those lots are used decides whether the customer ever notices.

  • Keep a lot together within a garment. The panels of one sweater should come from one lot, or from lots that match closely. Mixing lots within a single garment produces a visible tonal difference between body and sleeves.
  • Segregate lots in packing. Cartons should not mix lots, so a retailer receiving a delivery can keep a batch together on the shelf. Our notes on packaging and shipping cover how carton marking supports this.
  • Label the lot on the carton and the packing list. Without a lot number on the outside, segregation is theoretical — nobody can act on it during receiving.
  • Send the darker and lighter lots to different destinations where a buyer has that option. A single store receiving both lots side by side is where the variation becomes visible to a customer.

The aim is not to eliminate variation, which is not achievable, but to ensure variation is never visible within one garment or one shop fixture.

What about reorders?

Reorders are where colour consistency most often breaks down, and it is a planning problem more than a dyeing one. A repeat order placed months later will be dyed in a new lot, and there is no guarantee it matches the original closely enough to sit next to it.

Three ways to manage it. Keep the original yarn lot for reorders — buying ahead and holding stock is the most reliable route, and the reason our notes on yarn stock planning treat yarn stock as a strategic decision. Use a stock yarn rather than a bespoke shade where the colour is not a signature. Accept and plan for a step — retail the reorder as a separate drop, or re-merchandise it away from the original.

The worst outcome is a reorder that arrives slightly off and is mixed into existing stock, which is how a consistent range develops a visible patchwork.

What should a buyer write into the order?

  • The approved standard, referenced by number and date. A verbal "the cream we agreed" is not a standard. Our notes on working to Pantone explain why a physical standard usually beats a code alone.
  • The shade band, agreed in advance. State that production lots must fall inside the band and that judgement is made against the band card in daylight.
  • Lot segregation and lot marking. One lot per garment, no mixing within a carton, lot number on the carton and the packing list.
  • The approval route for each lot. Whether every lot is submitted for approval before shipping, or sampled — and who judges. Our notes on AQL inspection explain how colour checks fit the inspection routine.
  • A documented position on reorders. Whether yarn will be held, and what happens if a reorder cannot match.
  • What counts as a rejectable colour difference. A shade outside the band is a defect; a shade inside it is not. Without that line, every delivery becomes a negotiation. Our notes on quality standards documents explain where it belongs.

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal for two dye lots of the same colour to differ?

Yes, and it is universal rather than a sign of poor workmanship. Dyeing is a chemical process with small variations in water, temperature, dye concentration and vessel loading between runs, so two lots of the same recipe can differ perceptibly. The shade band exists precisely because exact matching between lots is not achievable.

How do I stop a reorder looking different from the original order?

The most reliable route is to buy and hold the yarn from the original lot, so the reorder is knitted from the same dye lot. Where that is not possible, using a stock yarn rather than a bespoke shade reduces the risk, and planning the reorder as a separate drop avoids it being mixed into existing stock.

What is a reasonable shade band for knitwear?

It depends on the fibre and the colour — pale and highly saturated shades are harder to hold than mid-tones — which is why the band should be agreed with the dyer before production rather than set by the buyer in advance. The useful discipline is to define it in writing and judge production against a band card in daylight.
