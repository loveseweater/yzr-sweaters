The Knitwear Bill of Materials: The Document That Prevents Substitution

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Quick answer

A bill of materials (BOM) is the line-by-line list of everything that goes into one garment: yarn, trims, labels, packaging, each with its specification, supplier and quantity per piece. The tech pack tells the factory how the sweater should look and measure; the BOM tells it exactly what to buy. Where a buyer experiences silent substitution — a cheaper yarn, a thinner button, a lighter polybag — the cause is almost always that no BOM existed to hold the specification in place.

Most buyers put real effort into the tech pack and then treat the components as an afterthought, mentioning the buttons in an email and the care label in a later one. That works until a price conversation happens or a supplier changes, at which point nobody can say precisely what was agreed. The BOM is the document that closes that gap, and it takes an hour to build.

What is a BOM, and how does it differ from a tech pack?

The two documents answer different questions and are often conflated. A tech pack describes the garment: silhouette, measurements, gauge, stitch, colour, construction method, finishing. It is a specification of the outcome. A BOM itemises the inputs — every physical thing that has to be purchased and consumed to produce that outcome, with enough specification to buy it again identically.

The practical difference shows up in who uses each. The technician works from the tech pack. The person ordering yarn, buttons, labels and cartons works from the BOM. When a buyer supplies only a tech pack and expects component control, the purchasing side is left to fill the gaps by inference, and inference is where variation enters.

A BOM is not a replacement for the tech pack, and our notes on building a knitwear tech pack cover that document in its own right. The two sit side by side: the tech pack defines the garment, the BOM defines what it is made from.

What belongs on a knitwear BOM?

Knitted garments have a comparatively short component list, which is exactly why omissions are easy to miss. A complete BOM for a basic sweater usually carries these lines:

  • Body yarn. Fibre composition, count, ply, colour reference, and the spinner or mill. This is the line that matters most, because yarn is the largest component and the one most likely to be quietly changed. Our notes on ply and twist explain why the count alone is not a sufficient specification.
  • Contrast or trim yarns, where the style uses a second colour or a rib in a different yarn. Each colourway is a separate line with its own reference.
  • Buttons, zips or other closures, with size, material, finish and colour matched to the garment. Our notes on cardigan closures cover how the choice interacts with the knit.
  • Main label, care label and size label, each specified with content, size, fold type and placement. These are compliance items, not decoration, and our notes on care and composition labelling explain what each market requires.
  • Hangtags, including the attachment method — a plastic barb carries its own waste and compliance implications.
  • Inner packaging and outer carton, with material and dimensions. Our notes on packaging compliance cover why these lines now carry regulatory weight.
  • Sewing thread, fusible tape and any interlining, which matter more on cut-and-sew constructions than on fully fashioned ones.

Each line should carry four things: what it is, the specification that makes it reproducible, the quantity per garment, and the supplier. Quantity is the field buyers most often leave blank, and it is the one that turns a BOM into a costing document as well as a purchasing one.

Why does a BOM prevent substitution?

Because it removes the room for interpretation that substitution lives in. A quotation that says "buttons, to be confirmed" can be filled with a fifty-cent button or a five-cent button, and the factory is not acting in bad faith either way — it is buying to the level of detail it was given.

Three specific failure patterns recur, and all three are BOM problems rather than quality problems.

Yarn swapped for a cheaper count. A 2/28 replaced with a 1/14 keeps the specification sheet looking similar while changing the hand feel and the durability. Our notes on yarn substitution explain why this should be a written, approved decision rather than a purchasing choice.

Trims downgraded. Buttons get thinner, zips get a lighter gauge, the polybag loses a micron. Individually trivial; collectively they explain a garment that feels cheaper than the sample without any single visible defect.

Labels changed to suit the factory's stock. A care label with the wrong fibre percentage or an untranslated care instruction is a compliance exposure, and it happens when the label was never specified beyond "standard".

The BOM's function is to make each of these a visible change rather than an invisible one. If a component differs from the BOM, the difference can be identified, discussed and either rejected or approved on the record — which is the whole point.

How does a BOM relate to costing?

More directly than most buyers realise. A quotation is built from four inputs: yarn weight per garment, trim costs per garment, make-up labour and packaging. Three of the four are BOM lines. This is why a BOM with quantities on it becomes a costing tool — when a price comes in high, the buyer who has a BOM can see which line is driving it rather than negotiating against a total.

It also explains why a request to reduce cost without touching the BOM is really a request to change a component. Our notes on what drives sweater price set out how the lines interact.

How should a buyer build and manage one?

  • Build it once, then reuse it. The first BOM takes an hour. Every subsequent style in the same programme reuses most of the lines, so the marginal effort falls sharply.
  • Specify the reference, not just the description. "Antique brass 15mm shank button" is a description; a supplier, article number and colour code can be reordered. Where a factory-sourced component is used, ask for the article number and keep it.
  • Tie each line to a quantity per garment. This is what makes the BOM usable for costing, for trim ordering and for checking that a shipment consumed the components it should have.
  • Attach the approved trims to the approved garment sample. A physical trim card — a labelled card with each button, label and thread taped to it — is the fastest reference in a dispute, and it is standard practice in outerwear for exactly that reason.
  • Version it and date it. A BOM without a version number cannot resolve a disagreement about which specification was current when the order was placed.
  • State that component changes need written approval. This is the clause that gives the BOM teeth. Our notes on supply contracts cover where it belongs.

Frequently asked questions

Is a bill of materials the same as a tech pack?

No. The tech pack specifies the garment — how it looks, measures and is constructed. The BOM specifies what it is made from, component by component, with quantities and suppliers. Both are needed: the tech pack guides the technician, the BOM guides purchasing, and component variation is controlled by the second.

Do I need a BOM for a simple sweater?

Yes, and a simple sweater's BOM is short — perhaps a dozen lines. The value is not complexity but precision: it records which yarn, which buttons and which labels were approved, so a later change is visible. That is exactly the situation where an unspecified component gets quietly swapped.

What is the single most important line on a knitwear BOM?

The body yarn line, because yarn is the largest cost and the component whose substitution is hardest to detect. It should name the fibre composition, count with ply, colour reference and the spinner — enough detail that the same yarn can be bought again, and enough that a different one is obviously different.
