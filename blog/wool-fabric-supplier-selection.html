Choosing a Wool and Yarn Supplier: What Matters Beyond Price

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

Do not choose a wool or yarn supplier on unit price alone. Spinning route, fibre fineness and staple length, batch-to-batch consistency, colour fastness, pilling grade, shrinkage behaviour and traceability documents decide whether the same sweater can be produced to the same standard again, so vet the mill on those seven points before placing any bulk yarn order.

Price is the easiest thing to compare and the least useful thing to decide on. Two mills can quote the same yarn description and deliver different products, because price hides the spinning route, the fibre selection and the quality control behind the label.

Why does the yarn supplier set the ceiling on sweater quality?

Yarn is normally the largest single material cost in a sweater, and it is where most faults begin. A weak yarn breaks on the machine; a hairy yarn pills in wear; an unevenly dyed yarn shows as barre. Those faults repeat in every garment knitted from that lot, so a poor yarn choice costs against a whole production run, not the price difference per kilogram.

What must you confirm beyond price?

A generic composition is a starting point, not a specification: "70% wool, 30% nylon" describes a wide range of yarns. Ask for these in writing.

Spinning route: worsted or woollen

The spinning route decides hand feel, strength and surface character. Worsted (combed) spinning combs out short fibres and lays the rest parallel, giving a smooth, compact, stronger yarn with little hairiness — the base for fine-gauge, clean-surfaced sweaters. Woollen (carded) spinning keeps a wider mix of fibre lengths and traps air, giving a bulkier, softer, fuzzier yarn for heavier styles. The two are different products, so a mill that will not state the route cannot be compared on price.

Fibre fineness and staple length

Fibre fineness is measured in microns, one micron being one thousandth of a millimetre; finer wool feels softer and costs more. Staple length — the average length of the individual fibres — drives pilling, because short fibres work out of the yarn and ball up on the surface, as our notes on micron, hand feel and prickling explain. Ask for a range, not a single average.

Batch consistency

Shade and yarn count must repeat from lot to lot, not just in the first delivery. Ask how the mill records dye lots, whether it keeps retention samples of every batch sent to you, and whether one lot can be reserved for your programme. A signed standard sample plus a batch number on every delivery lets a deviation be traced to a lot.

Colour fastness

Colour fastness is the dyed yarn's resistance to losing colour, tested by property rather than as one figure: wash, rubbing (crocking) and light fastness are separate results. All three matter, because a red wool that passes wash fastness can still bleed onto a pale lining or fade in a shop window.

Pilling grade

Pilling is the formation of small fibre balls on the knit surface, graded on a scale where a higher number means less pilling. Methods such as the ICI pilling box or Martindale (ISO 12945 series) produce a grade a mill can quote and a contract can hold to. Set a contractual minimum, not a promise.

Shrinkage behaviour and yarn stability

How far the yarn has been relaxed or shrink-resisted before knitting decides how much the finished garment moves in a home wash. Loose, unrelaxed yarn produces an unstable sweater that finishing cannot fully correct. Ask whether the yarn is pre-relaxed.

Traceability and compliance documents

Expect documents rather than assurances: fibre origin, a certificate chain for certified-fibre claims (Responsible Wool Standard or Global Recycled Standard, for example), and third-party test reports. A mill that cannot produce paperwork at sampling rarely produces it under deadline.

What supply questions should you ask?

  • MOQ (minimum order quantity) per colour and count — in kilograms or knitted garments?
  • Stock position — does the mill hold this yarn, or is every order spun to order?
  • Lead time and peak-season capacity, stated as a delivery window, not a single date.
  • Lot reservation for repeat programmes, so the second order matches the first.
  • Shortage policy — is there a pre-agreed substitute, and how are you notified?

Yarn price exposure is a separate negotiation; our notes on wool yarn price volatility cover how buyers manage it, and our knitwear yarn guide compares the fibres themselves.

How do you verify a yarn before committing?

The reliable test is to knit it. Ask for a trial panel or sample sweater in the actual yarn, not a stock swatch, and wash it as your market does. Compare the washed sample against the standard for shade, pilling, shrinkage and hand feel, then keep a sealed standard signed by both parties.

What are the common traps?

  • Buying on price without asking the spinning route.
  • No batch-consistency agreement or retention samples.
  • Leaving colour fastness untested until garments are packed.
  • Describing pilling in words instead of agreeing a test method and grade.
  • No written standard sample — the most common cause of shade disputes.

Who should buy the yarn — you or the sweater factory?

Most buyers purchase garments, not yarn, and the knitting factory buys yarn against your approved specification, because it knows what knits cleanly on its machines. You should still request the yarn source, count, composition and test reports, and approve the standard sample yourself. On nominated-yarn programmes, keep the mill's specification sheet and your tech pack stating the same thing in writing before bulk.

Frequently asked questions

Is a cheaper wool yarn ever the right choice?

Sometimes. Where the sweater is heavily brushed, printed or blended with a fibre that dominates the hand feel, a lower-cost yarn changes little at retail. The mistake is saving on yarn in a plain, fine-gauge style where the yarn is the product.

What should a yarn specification sheet include?

Composition and blend ratio, spinning route, micron range, staple length, yarn count, colour reference with batch number, finish or treatment, and the test results you require. A sheet that lists only composition cannot be enforced.

How do I handle a shade difference between two yarn batches?

Compare both deliveries against the signed standard sample under the same light, then ask the mill for the batch numbers and retention samples. Knitting the two lots into one garment — for example sleeves and body — is what makes a small batch difference visible, so keep one lot per colourway where you can.
