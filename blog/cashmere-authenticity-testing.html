Is It Really Cashmere? Adulteration, Testing and Buyer Protection

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Cashmere mislabelling is common because the price gap between cashmere and the fibres that imitate it is large and hand-feel can't close the gap — superfine wool, viscose and yak down all pass the touch test. The reliable method is laboratory fibre analysis: microscopy of scale structure to identify the fibre, plus quantitative analysis to give the true blend percentage. Insist on a written micron and percentage spec, an independent test report on bulk yarn or garment samples, retained samples from every lot, and payment terms tied to test results.

A buyer we work with once received two "100% cashmere" programs from two different suppliers at nearly the same price. One tested 96% cashmere. The other tested 62% cashmere, 30% wool, 8% nylon — and it felt every bit as soft in the showroom. That's the whole problem in one story: cashmere fraud survives because it works on the hand. Here's what actually gets faked, what tests reveal, and how to structure an order so the truth comes out before your money is gone.

Why mislabelling is so common

Cashmere commands a multiple of wool's price per kilo, and the physical difference between a 15.5-micron cashmere fibre and a very fine 17-micron wool fibre is invisible without magnification. The fraud incentive is structural:

  • Micron overlap. Cashmere is defined (under most standards) as goat undercoat fibre under roughly 15–16 micron with a specific scale structure; superfine merino can sit just above that line and feel nearly identical.
  • Label-inheritance. A spinner buys yarn on trust, a knitter buys the yarn on trust, a factory labels the garment based on the paperwork — and each link can be honestly wrong while the final label is fiction.
  • Testing is optional in practice. Most retail brands never test; when enforcement or a consumer body does, mislabelling rates found in market surveys have repeatedly been double-digit percentages of "cashmere" garments.

And it isn't always fraud in the criminal sense — much of it is sloppy blend bookkeeping: a "30% cashmere" blend that's really 18%, a "100% cashmere" that's a wool-cashmere blend from a spinner's stock lot. For you as the buyer, the distinction hardly matters: your label, your market's labelling law, and your customer's trust all see the same problem.

The usual adulteration patterns

  • Superfine wool. The classic. Close in micron, far cheaper, and the fraud is hardest to detect — only scale-structure microscopy reliably separates the two.
  • Viscose or "cashmere-feel" rayon. Sold by the feel in low-price channels; trivially detectable (cellulose, not protein — see the burn-test limits below).
  • Yak and camel down. Genuinely fine fibres in their own right, but cheaper than cashmere and visually near-identical under casual inspection; a known route for "cashmere" in the mid market.
  • Percentage drift. The most common in real production: blends that quietly drop — 30% becomes 20%, often at a blending stage nobody re-checks. Your fibre composition tolerance clause is what catches this.
  • Grade substitution. Not an identity fraud but a quality one: grade C or short-staple cashmere delivered against a grade A spec. The identity test passes; the wear performance doesn't — see our cashmere grades guide for what a written grade spec should contain.

What testing can and can't do

Worth being honest about the buyer-side tools:

  • Hand-feel. Nearly useless for authenticity. Experienced people can rank softness, but a 17-micron wool with soft finishing can feel "more cashmere" than honest 15.5-micron cashmere. Never accept hand-feel as evidence.
  • Burn test. A lighter tells you protein (hair smell, crushable ash) versus cellulose (paper smell) versus synthetic (melts). That's it. It catches viscose-in-cashmere; it says nothing about wool-versus-cashmere, which is the fraud you're most likely to face.
  • Microscopy. The industry workhorse. Cashmere and wool are distinguished by scale height and pattern along the fibre — visible only at magnification, which is why identification requires a trained analyst. Standards like ISO 17751 and AATCC 20A govern the method; reputable labs (SGS, Intertek, Bureau Veritas, or a third-party inspection firm's textile lab) run it routinely.
  • Quantitative blend analysis. The same lab session extended: count and measure enough fibres to give the actual percentage composition, plus micron distribution. This is what turns "is there cashmere in it?" into "there is 87.4% cashmere, mean 15.3 micron" — the number your label and your spec can stand on.
  • DNA testing. Newer species-verification methods exist and are gaining ground; treat them as a complement rather than a replacement for microscopy today.

Cost and turnaround are modest: a fibre-identification and composition report on a garment or yarn sample typically runs in the low hundreds of US dollars with about a week of lab time — trivial insurance on any program big enough to matter, and the pricing logic is the same as for other lab testing: the cost scales with claims you need to defend, not with order size.

How to structure the order so verification is real

  • Spec in writing: fibre percentage with tolerance, and for pure cashmere a micron and (for quality) fibre-length spec. "100% cashmere" alone is not a specification.
  • Test the bulk, not the pitch. Require an independent lab report on a sample drawn from the production lot — bulk yarn cones or finished garments, not the salesman's swatch. Ideally you nominate the lab or draw the sample yourself at pre-shipment inspection.
  • Retain samples from every dye lot — sealed, signed, kept on both sides. If a market-surveillance query arrives eight months later, a retained lot sample answers it.
  • Tie money to results. Balance payment released after a passing composition report is the clause that makes all of the above self-enforcing; pair it with the retention rights in your supply contract.
  • Choose your blend strategy deliberately. An honest, well-made cashmere blend with a truthful label is a stronger business than an accidental fake — which is the framing we use with buyers weighing "100% cashmere" claims against price.

Frequently asked questions

Can you tell fake cashmere by touch or a burn test?

No. Hand-feel cannot separate fine wool from cashmere — soft finishing can make wool feel softer than honest cashmere. A burn test only distinguishes protein fibres from cellulose or synthetics, so it catches viscose fakes but is silent on the most common adulteration, wool substituted for cashmere. Fibre identification requires microscopic analysis of scale structure by a trained lab analyst.

What test actually proves cashmere content?

Microscopy-based fibre identification to standards such as ISO 17751 or AATCC 20A, extended to quantitative analysis for the true blend percentage and micron distribution. Run by an independent lab (SGS, Intertek, Bureau Veritas and peers) on samples drawn from the production lot, this produces a report that stands up to customer, customs and market-surveillance scrutiny. DNA-based species verification is emerging as a complement.

How do I stop my supplier quietly cutting the cashmere percentage?

Three clauses together: a written fibre spec with tolerance, an independent composition report on the production lot (with the sample drawn at pre-shipment inspection rather than supplied by the factory), and balance payment released only on a passing report. Add sealed retained samples per dye lot on both sides, and a supplier who agrees to all four is very unlikely to gamble on the blend.
