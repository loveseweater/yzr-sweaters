Bamboo, Viscose and Modal in Knitwear: A Buyer's Sourcing Guide

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Bamboo, viscose and modal are all regenerated cellulose — plant pulp dissolved and spun into fibre, not "natural" fibres in the cotton sense. They knit into a smooth, fluid, cool-feeling fabric with lovely drape but weak recovery and a tendency to pill and bag at cuffs. They are almost always blended with cotton, wool or synthetics, and "bamboo" is just viscose made from bamboo pulp. Spec them for hand-feel and drape, blend them for structure, and treat any "eco" claim with the scrutiny our certifications guide demands.

Buyers reach for bamboo, viscose and modal when they want a sweater that feels cool, drapes like liquid and costs less than silk. The trouble is the marketing has run well ahead of the material science, and we spend a surprising amount of time unpicking claims before a buyer commits to a programme. These three fibres are close cousins — all regenerated cellulose — and understanding that one fact resolves most of the confusion about price, performance and sustainability. Here is the practical version we give customers.

They are cousins, not three different things

Bamboo, viscose (often called rayon) and modal are manufactured fibres made by taking plant cellulose — wood pulp, or bamboo pulp — dissolving it and spinning it back into filament or staple fibre. The starting material differs (bamboo versus beech or eucalyptus versus generic wood), and the process differs slightly (modal is a higher-tenacity variant of viscose; lyocell is the closed-loop cousin we cover in lyocell and hemp knitwear), but they are all regenerated cellulose. This matters because "bamboo" gets marketed as a miracle natural fibre when it is, chemically, viscose made from a different plant. The environmental story depends entirely on the solvent system used to dissolve the pulp — closed-loop processes recover the solvent, open-loop ones do not — not on whether the label says bamboo or viscose. We source both, and we are blunt with buyers about which process their yarn uses.

Why the hand-feel is so good — and the recovery so poor

The same molecular structure that gives these fibres their signature cool, smooth, fluid drape also makes them structurally weak. A pure viscose or modal knit has almost no elasticity, so it stretches and stays stretched: cuffs and hems bag, body length grows, and a tight rib relaxes into a sloppy one. That is why finished garments are nearly always blended — 30–50% with cotton for stability, or with wool for warmth and bounce, or with a little elastane for recovery. The art is in the blend ratio: too little synthetic and the garment grows on the hanger; too much and you lose the very drape the buyer wanted. We dial this in the same way we handle acrylic blends — by knitting a size set and measuring growth against the measurement chart before bulk.

Pilling, bagging and care behaviour

Two failure modes dominate. First, pilling: the long smooth filaments of viscose and modal form pills at friction points just like any soft fibre, and because the fabric is smooth the pills show against a clean surface. A tighter knit and a touch of nylon reduce it, but you cannot eliminate it on a fluid blend — see our pilling prevention guide for the trade-offs. Second, bagging: cuffs and collars that will not snap back, the single most common complaint on viscose-rich knits. The fix is structural — blend, gauge, rib construction — not a finishing trick. On care, these fibres are hydrophilic and weaken when wet, so they need gentle washing and flat drying; our fabric care guide is where we set the label. A damp, warm viscose sweater stretched on a hanger is a returned garment waiting to happen.

The sustainability claim trap

This is where brands get burned. "Bamboo" sounds green; "viscose" sounds chemical — but they can be the same fibre made in the same mill. Regulators in the EU and beyond now police vague green claims, and a "100% natural bamboo" line on a regenerated-cellulose sweater is exactly the kind of statement that draws EU sustainability scrutiny and retailer questions. If sustainability is the selling point, specify the process, not the plant: look for closed-loop solvents and credible chain-of-custody certification such as FSC or PEFC for the pulp, and resist the urge to call it natural. For genuinely lower-impact options, our recycled and organic fibres guide covers alternatives buyers actually certify.

How we spec these programmes

When a buyer wants the bamboo/viscose/modal hand-feel, we start from the end use. A fluid summer top wants a high viscose or modal content with a cotton stabiliser; a slightly structured cardigan wants the blend pulled toward wool or acrylic for recovery. We knit a development sample, measure growth and pilling, and only then fix the composition in the bill of materials. Anyone promising a pure-cellular-fibre sweater that holds its shape is selling you a garment that will not survive one season of wear — and we would rather tell you that at the sampling stage than after a container lands.

Frequently asked questions

Is bamboo knitwear really more eco-friendly than viscose?

Not inherently. Bamboo, viscose and modal are all regenerated cellulose — plant pulp dissolved and spun into fibre. Bamboo is simply viscose made from bamboo pulp. The environmental difference comes from the solvent process (closed-loop recovers solvent; open-loop does not) and the pulp's chain of custody, not the plant source. Spec the process, not the label, and back any green claim with certification.

Why does my viscose or modal sweater bag at the cuffs?

These fibres have almost no natural elasticity, so a pure or high-content knit stretches and stays stretched. Cuffs, collars and hems relax and the body grows in length. The fix is structural: blend with cotton, wool or a little elastane, choose a tighter gauge and a stronger rib. No finishing trick recovers shape once the fibre has crept.

Can bamboo, viscose or modal be used pure in knitwear?

Technically yes, but we rarely recommend it for garments that must hold shape. Pure regenerated-cellulose knits deliver beautiful drape and a cool hand but poor recovery and higher pilling, and they weaken when wet. They work as fluid, unstructured pieces; for anything with cuffs, ribs or structure, blend them — typically 30–50% with cotton, wool or synthetics.
