RFID Tagging for Knitwear: Meeting Retail Requirements at the Factory

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

When a retailer mandates RFID, the garment must leave the factory with an encoded UHF RFID tag whose EPC links to the same GTIN your barcode carries, positioned per the retailer's placement guide and passing a read-rate test on the actual garment. For knitwear suppliers the work is in the details: tags sewn into care labels or hangtags, encoding data supplied before bulk, and verification that the tag reads reliably through wool and acrylic. Budget a few US cents per garment for the tag and a sourcing process that starts before production, not at packing.

The RFID conversation usually reaches a knitwear factory as a one-line email from a buyer: "our retail partner now requires RFID, can you do it?" The honest answer is yes — we apply and encode source tags — but the question deserves a better answer than a yes, because RFID compliance programmes fail in boring, preventable ways: wrong placement, duplicate EPCs across two factories, tags that read on the bench but not through a stack of chunky knits. Here is what actually happens when knitwear goes RFID.

Why retailers want RFID on knitwear in the first place

A barcode must be seen to be scanned; an RFID tag is read by radio, in bulk, through packaging. A store receiving cartons of sweaters can read every unit in the carton without opening it, count a rail of hanging garments in seconds, and find a specific size in the stockroom with a handheld. The retail gains are inventory accuracy — programmes routinely report moving from 65–75% stock accuracy to the high nineties — and that is why major department stores and big-box retailers in North America and Europe have progressively made RFID a condition of supply, rolling it from their private labels out to vendor brands. If you supply or aspire to supply those retailers, RFID is not optional; the question is only whose job the tagging is. Retailers call it source tagging when it happens at the factory, and it is cheaper for everyone when it does.

What the requirement actually consists of

Every retailer programme reduces to the same handful of technical elements:

  • An EPC-compliant UHF tag. The industry has converged on RAIN RFID — UHF tags following GS1's EPC standards. The tag carries a unique Electronic Product Code; the standard retailer-grade inlay on a care label or hangtag is the workhorse format.
  • Encoding that links to your GTIN. The EPC encodes your product identifier — the same GTIN that appears on your barcode and GTIN structure — plus a unique serial number per garment. This is where duplicate-encoding errors happen: two suppliers accidentally issuing the same serials, or a re-run reusing numbers. Encoding files must be generated carefully and communicated.
  • Placement per the retailer's guide. Retail programmes publish a placement specification — typically the tag sewn into the care label at a defined seam, or applied as a hangtag in a defined position. On knitwear the placement also has to survive the wearer: a rigid inlay at the neck seam of a fine-gauge merino is a scratch complaint waiting to happen, so tag format and position interact with label and trim selection.
  • Read-rate verification. The retailer tests that tags read at a defined rate — commonly 95–100% — on the actual garment, in cartons, at their receiving dock. Knit fabric is mildly absorbent to RF, and thick, densely knitted wool attenuates the signal more than a cotton tee. Testing before bulk is the only reliable way to know.
  • Data communication. The retailer needs the EPC-to-GTIN mapping, typically via their portal or through your ASN. This is an administrative step, and it is the one that quietly fails: goods arrive perfectly tagged and unreadable in the retailer's systems because the advance data never loaded.

The knitwear-specific problems

Three issues come up on knitted goods more than on woven apparel. First, read performance through dense knit structures: a 7-gauge chunky lambswool sweater stacks a lot of fibre and moisture between the reader and the tag, and a tag that reads at 100% on a hanger can struggle at the bottom of a packed carton of heavy knits. Tag selection — a larger inlay antenna compensates — and a carton-level read test settle it. Second, metallic yarns: lurex and metallic-effect knitwear detunes the antenna; we flag this to buyers early because it changes the tag choice. Third, washability: if the tag is permanent, it must survive the care regime of the garment — industrially laundered uniforms need a different tag construction than a hand-wash cashmere. Buyers specifying durable labels should specify durable RFID inlays in the same breath.

Costs, and who pays

The economics are simple and worth stating plainly. A standard UHF inlay on a care label or hangtag costs a few US cents at volume; encoding, application and verification add a modest labour line. On a USD 9–12 FOB sweater, the whole programme is well under 1% of FOB — real, but not a margin event. The usual commercial arrangement is a line item in the quote, either priced into the garment or charged per tag. What genuinely costs money is improvisation: applying tags after goods arrive at a third-party facility costs multiples of source tagging, and a failed compliance audit — goods at a retailer's dock that will not read — can mean chargebacks or refused cartons. Encode and apply at the factory; verify before shipping; upload the data before the vessel sails.

How we run RFID orders

Our process is unglamorous and reliable. The buyer's retail programme gives us the specification — tag type, placement, encoding rules. We confirm tag stock with an approved supplier, tie the EPC serials to the production plan so numbers are never reused, and sew or hang the tags during normal finishing rather than as a separate re-handling step. Verification is a sample read test per size and colour on the packed carton, plus a full encoding audit against the GTIN list. The result goes into the packing data alongside the shipping documents, and the EPC file goes to the retailer portal the day the order ships. For programmes where the buyer's own brand is the end retailer, the same capability runs inventory accuracy for e-commerce operations — warehouse cycle counts on tagged knitwear are faster by an order of magnitude.

Frequently asked questions

How much does RFID tagging add per garment?

Typically a few US cents for the tag itself plus a small application and verification cost — in total usually under 1% of FOB on a mid-range sweater. The expensive version is not doing it at the factory: third-party tagging after import, or chargebacks from a retailer whose dock cannot read your cartons, cost multiples of source tagging.

Does RFID replace the barcode on knitwear?

No — the two coexist, and they are linked: the RFID tag's EPC encodes the same GTIN the barcode carries, plus a unique serial per unit. The barcode remains the fallback scan at checkout and for smaller retailers, while RFID enables bulk reads for inventory. Keep the GTIN structure clean and both systems stay consistent.

Will RFID tags read through thick wool knits?

Usually yes, with the right tag and placement. Dense, heavy knits and cartons stacked with chunky sweaters attenuate the UHF signal more than lighter fabrics, so the tag inlay is selected with the garment in mind and verified with a carton-level read test before bulk. Metallic and lurex yarns are the genuine exception — they detune antennas — and need special handling, which is why we raise the yarn composition early in any RFID programme.
