Eastern Europe Knitwear Sourcing: A Buyer's Guide to Poland, Czechia, Romania & the Baltics

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Eastern Europe — Poland, Czechia, Romania, the Baltics and, increasingly, Ukraine — is a fast-growing but price-sensitive knitwear market inside the EU single market. Buyers range from value retail chains and online brands to wholesalers feeding neighbouring EU states. They want warm, commercial, mid-price knits and they hold Chinese factories to EU compliance standards: the same sustainability, labelling and due-diligence rules as Germany or France. Expect sharper price negotiation than Western Europe, longer payment terms, and a real appetite for private label.

Most knitwear sourcing guides for Europe stop at Germany, the UK and the Nordics, which is a mistake. The eastern half of the EU — Poland especially, then Czechia, Romania, Slovakia, Hungary and the Baltic states — has become one of the more active knitwear procurement regions on the continent, driven by a rising middle class, strong local retail groups, and a generation of online brands that source globally. We supply several of them, and the pattern is distinct enough from Western Europe to warrant its own playbook. Here is what buyers there actually want.

Who is buying, and what for

The Eastern European knitwear buyer is not one profile. Value and mid-market retail chains — the LPPs and CCCs of Poland, the retail groups in Romania and Czechia — buy volume commercial knitwear: chunky winter cardigans, basic pullovers, kids' knits, often private label, often at aggressive price points. Online-first brands across the region behave like their Western peers: smaller runs, faster react, more design-led, but with tighter margins because local purchasing power is lower. Wholesalers in Warsaw, Prague or Bucharest buy to distribute into neighbouring EU states, so their range has to clear every EU market's rules at once. The through-line is value consciousness: even the design-led buyers negotiate harder than a German or Nordic counterpart would, which is a point we make in our broader sourcing countries comparison.

What they source, and at what price

Climate drives the mix: cold winters mean a heavy bias toward warm, substantial knits — 5–12 gauge lambswool and wool-blend cardigans, chunky acrylic and acrylic-wool mixes for the value tier, and a steady base of cotton and viscose pieces for the shoulder seasons. The price ceiling is lower than in Western Europe, so the conversation is constantly about what moves the price — gauge, fibre, trim, pack-out — and about hitting a retail price point rather than a quality ceiling. We see strong demand for private label because local retailers want branded-in-their-name ranges without building a sourcing desk in Asia. For new entrants, our starting-a-brand guide maps the usual first steps.

EU compliance still applies — fully

This is the part buyers from outside the EU sometimes miss: Poland, Czechia and Romania are EU members, so sweaters imported from China clear EU customs and must meet the same rules as goods entering Germany or France. That means EU sustainability and due-diligence legislation, labelling rules, fibre-content accuracy (the EU equivalent), and the digital-product-passport trajectory we track in our sustainability coverage. There is no "eastern loophole." Buyers there are often highly compliance-aware precisely because they serve the whole EU, and they will ask for the same documentation a Western retailer demands. Our certifications overview is the reference we point them to.

Lead time, logistics and the China question

Eastern Europe is close to Western European consumers but far from the yarn and knitting capacity of Asia, so the "near-shoring" instinct collides with reality: there is almost no scale knitwear manufacturing left in Poland or Romania that competes with Chinese cost and flexibility. The practical result is that most Eastern European brands still import the bulk of their knitwear from China, accepting the sea-freight lead time because the price gap is real — our landed-cost model shows why even with EU duty the maths still favours Asia for volume. The differentiator is not avoiding China but planning the calendar: these buyers want reliable repeat deliveries across a season, not one big container, so replenishment planning and lead-time management matter more than for a single-drop wholesaler.

How it compares to neighbouring markets

Set against the rest of Europe, Eastern Europe sits between the price discipline of Germany and the value focus of southern Europe, with less of the premium-design appetite you find in the Nordics and none of the post-Brexit customs friction of the UK. Ukraine is the wildcard: a skilled workforce and lower costs, but logistics and payment-risk questions that serious buyers handle through EU-based intermediaries. For a Chinese factory, the region rewards the same things everywhere — consistent quality, documentation discipline, and a willingness to speak private label — just at a more negotiated price.

Frequently asked questions

Do Eastern European buyers import knitwear from China despite being in the EU?

Yes, heavily. There is little scale knitwear manufacturing left in Poland, Czechia or Romania that matches Chinese cost and flexibility, so most volume still comes from Asia. Buyers accept the sea-freight lead time because the price gap is real even after EU duty. The skill is planning the season for reliable repeat deliveries rather than one container.

What compliance applies to sweaters imported into Poland or Romania?

The same EU rules as anywhere in the union: EU customs entry, labelling and fibre-content accuracy, and the expanding sustainability and due-diligence legislation. There is no eastern exception. Buyers serving the whole EU will ask for the same documentation a Western retailer demands, so build compliance in from the purchase order.

What kinds of knitwear sell best in Eastern Europe?

Warm, commercial, mid-price knits dominate: chunky wool-blend cardigans, basic pullovers, kids' knits, plus cotton and viscose pieces for shoulder seasons. The value tier leans on acrylic and acrylic-wool mixes. Private label is strongly favoured because local retailers want their-own-name ranges without an Asian sourcing desk.
