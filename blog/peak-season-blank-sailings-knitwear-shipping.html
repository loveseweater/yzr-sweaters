Rolled Containers and Blank Sailings: Booking Knitwear Freight in Peak Season

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

A rolled container is cargo booked on one vessel but left behind and moved to the next sailing — typically seven days later, often more in peak season. Carriers overbook, cancel sailings (blank sailings) when demand drops, and cut off documentation strictly when vessels are full. For knitwear the damage is a missed selling window, so booking space belongs in the production schedule, not in the week the cartons are sealed.

There is a particular phone call we get in September and again before Chinese New Year: the goods are finished, packed and waiting, but the forwarder says the container has been rolled. The buyer did everything right on production and still misses the launch date. We see this a lot with autumn/winter programmes, because the sweater shipping window collides with the busiest weeks of the year for China-to-Europe and transpacific freight. Our lead-time guide covers making the garments; this one covers getting them onto the ship.

Why a container gets rolled

  • Overbooking. Carriers accept more bookings than the vessel can carry, expecting some no-shows. When everyone turns up, the lowest-yield or latest-confirmed cargo is pushed to the next sailing.
  • Blank sailings. The carrier cancels a scheduled voyage or skips a port to manage capacity or catch up a disrupted rotation. Your booking still exists, but there is no ship that week. Two blank sailings in a row can add a fortnight.
  • Weight and space limits. A vessel may hit its stability or weight limit before every container is loaded. Bulky, light knitwear is rarely the heaviest cargo, which makes it an easy one to leave behind.
  • Late documentation. Shipping instructions, the verified gross mass (VGM) and customs declaration all have cut-offs. Miss one by an hour in a full week and the container does not load — our shipping documents guide lists what has to be in and when.
  • Changes at the last minute. A revised carton count, a changed consignee or a late packaging decision forces the booking to be reissued. In peak season a reissued booking loses its place in the queue.

Notice that only one of those five is about the factory. Most rolling happens in the space between a finished order and a confirmed booking, which is exactly where nobody owns the schedule when the buyer and the forwarder each assume the other is handling it.

When the risky weeks are

Three windows matter for sweater programmes. First, roughly August to October, when autumn/winter inventory and holiday retail stock move at the same time and carriers push through general rate increases and peak season surcharges. Second, the three to five weeks before Chinese New Year, when factories rush to ship before the holiday shutdown and space disappears fast — see our CNY production planning guide. Third, any week after a port disruption, when a backlog of rolled cargo rolls again.

A buying calendar that ends at "ex-factory date" is missing at least one step. Work backwards from the in-store date: transit, clearance, delivery to the warehouse, then a buffer of one full sailing. Our capacity booking notes explain why reserving production capacity and reserving vessel space are two separate bookings that need to line up.

For a full container, confirm the loading plan and cut-off dates with the factory as well — container loading has its own timing, and a truck that arrives after the terminal cut-off is simply a rolled container with a different explanation. If the season is genuinely late, expediting production is pointless when the constraint is the vessel, not the knitting machines.

What to do when you are rolled

First, get the reason in writing and the new sailing and arrival dates. Second, check whether the rolled container is still inside the terminal free-time window; if it sits at the port for a week waiting for the next vessel, demurrage and detention charges may start before the goods have even left China. Third, tell the receiving warehouse immediately — an unexpected arrival a week later is what turns a delay into a missed markdown. Fourth, decide whether a partial air shipment of the launch-critical sizes is cheaper than losing the first two weeks of the season. That calculation is usually a few hundred kilograms, not the whole order.

Frequently asked questions

What does it mean when a container is rolled?

It means the cargo was booked on a vessel but not loaded, and has been moved to a later sailing. Common causes are carrier overbooking, a cancelled (blank) sailing, vessel weight limits, or missing a documentation cut-off. On most weekly services the next sailing is about seven days away, so the real cost is a week of selling season.

How early should I book ocean space for a knitwear order?

In ordinary weeks, one to two weeks before the goods are ready. In the August–October peak and the pre-Chinese New Year rush, allow two to three weeks and insist on a written booking confirmation with a booking number. Freeze the carton count, weight and consignee before the shipping instruction cut-off, because a reissued booking loses priority.

Is paying a premium for guaranteed space worth it?

Sometimes. A premium or a contract allocation makes sense when a launch date is fixed, when the goods are seasonal, or when a missed week costs more than the surcharge — which is typical for winter knitwear. For replenishment stock with flexible timing, a cheaper spot booking and a one-sailing buffer in the plan is usually the better trade.
