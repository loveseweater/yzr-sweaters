Baggy Elbows and Saggy Hems: Stretch Recovery in Knitwear

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

A knitted garment keeps its shape only as well as its yarn and its loop structure allow. Bagging elbows, stretched necklines and hems that grow two sizes after a season come from the same root cause: loops that were extended past the point where the yarn can pull them back. The buyer-side controls are fibre choice (wool recovers, cotton does not), knitting tension, rib constructions at edges, and — for fitted styles — a small elastane or nylon content. If shape retention matters to your range, put a growth-and-recovery test in the tech pack rather than discovering the problem in reviews.

We see this a lot with fitted women's styles: the sample looks sharp, the first wear looks sharp, and by week six the elbows are pouched and the hem sits two inches lower than the size chart says. Nothing "broke" — the garment simply never had the recovery the design assumed. This is one of the most predictable, and most preventable, quality complaints in knitwear.

Growth versus recovery

Knitted fabric is a looped structure, so it stretches far more than woven cloth — that is the point of it. Two different things matter:

  • Growth is how far the fabric extends under a given load — from gravity, from wear, from a heavy garment hanging on a hanger.
  • Recovery is how much of that extension comes back when the load is removed.

A garment fails on shape when growth is high and recovery is poor. Wool fibres have a natural crimp that acts like a spring, so a wool sweater extends and pulls back. Cotton fibre is essentially inelastic — a cotton knit extends and mostly stays extended, which is why 100% cotton knits are notorious for growing at the hem and cuffs. Acrylic sits in between: decent elastic behaviour when new, but the fibres relax permanently under sustained load and heat, so growth accumulates.

Where the shape loss comes from

  • Yarn and fibre. This is the biggest lever. Wool and wool blends recover; cotton, viscose and most linen blends do not. For fitted styles in plant fibres, a 2–5% elastane core-spun into the yarn, or a nylon reinforcement content, is the standard industry answer.
  • Knitting tension. A loose, drapey knit on a coarse gauge has long loops that straighten easily and stay straight. A firmer loop length gives the yarn less distance to travel and better recovery. Buyers often approve a soft, loose sample not realising they have approved the growth behaviour with it.
  • Edge constructions. Necklines, cuffs and hems are where recovery matters most because they are stretched every time the garment is worn. A 2x2 rib recovers far better than single jersey; a rib with elastane better still. This is a pattern-level decision that costs almost nothing — see how much construction detail sits inside a simple neckline choice.
  • Garment weight. A heavy, chunky style is literally hanging off its own shoulders all day. The heavier the garment, the more the shoulder seams and upper body grow under gravity — one reason oversized heavy knits need either firm tension or a fibre with genuine recovery.
  • Finishing. Washing and pressing set the garment at its finished dimensions. If relaxation is incomplete, the first home wash finishes the job on your customer's time — that overlaps with shrinkage control, and the two should be specified together.

What to put in the specification

Shape retention is testable, and serious buyers treat it like colourfastness — a number, not a hope:

  • Growth and recovery test. Labs stretch the fabric to a defined extension, hold it, release it, and measure the permanent set — ASTM D2594 is the standard most US-bound programmes reference. Ask for the result after your stated wash count, because recovery usually degrades with washing.
  • Measurements after wash, not just before. Your size chart should be validated on washed garments. A sweater that measures correctly off the line but grows 4cm in body length after three washes is a returns generator.
  • A wear trial on the sales sample. Have someone actually wear the sample for a week before bulk — elbows, seat and neckline tell you more in seven days than a lab can in an afternoon. We do this as standard on fitted styles and it routinely changes the yarn or tension decision.
  • Fibre content that matches the silhouette. If the design is body-hugging, spec the elastane or the wool content at the start. Retrofitting recovery into a cotton fitted style after the first bad reviews is the expensive version of the same decision.

Frequently asked questions

Does adding elastane to a sweater make it feel synthetic?

Not at the levels used for shape retention. Two to five percent elastane core-spun inside the yarn is invisible to the hand — the outer fibre (wool, cotton, acrylic) is what the wearer touches. What the wearer notices is that the cuffs still grip and the hem still sits where it did on day one, which is exactly the point.

Why do my cotton knits grow more than my wool knits?

Fibre physics. Wool has a natural crimp that behaves like a spring, so the yarn pulls loops back after stretching. Cotton fibre is smooth and inelastic, so once a cotton knit's loops straighten under load there is nothing to retract them. Cotton styles that need to hold a silhouette should be knitted firmer, edged in rib, or blended with a recovery fibre.

Can a growth-and-recovery test be part of pre-shipment inspection?

It is a lab test rather than an on-site inspection item, but it fits naturally into the same order: we submit bulk-fabric or garment samples to the third-party lab early enough that results arrive before your pre-shipment inspection, so a failure can still be acted on. The key is to have the standard and the pass limit written into the quality specification, not agreed after the fact.
