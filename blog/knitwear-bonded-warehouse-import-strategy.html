Bonded Warehouses for Knitwear Imports: Deferring Duty Without Freezing Cash

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

A bonded warehouse lets your knitwear sit in your destination market under customs supervision with duty and import VAT unpaid until stock is actually withdrawn for sale. For seasonal importers that means paying duty in November when the sweaters ship to stores, not in August when the container lands. It works best for high-duty goods held three to nine months; for stock that sells within weeks, the storage fees usually eat the benefit.

Most buyers we work with discover bonded warehousing at exactly the wrong moment — two weeks before their container arrives, when their accountant points out that duty is due on the whole shipment at entry. We see this a lot with buyers who take autumn deliveries in July to catch cheap sea freight, then watch a five-figure duty bill land in the same week as the freight invoice. Bonded storage exists to break that link between arrival and payment, and it is underused in knitwear because nobody explains the mechanics. So here they are.

What a bonded warehouse actually is

A bonded warehouse is a customs-authorised facility where imported goods can be stored without paying duty, import VAT or GST at the border. The goods sit under customs control — you cannot open retail cartons, rework garments or hand out samples freely, and stock movements in and out are recorded against your bond with the warehouse operator. Duty is triggered only when goods leave the warehouse for domestic consumption, and it is assessed on the value at entry, not at withdrawal. In most regimes you can also re-export the goods duty-free, which matters more than buyers expect — we have had European clients redirect part of a container to a Middle East buyer straight from the bonded facility, paying zero EU duty on the redirected portion.

Every major market runs some version of this: the US customs bonded warehouse program under CBP, the UK and EU customs warehousing procedures, and equivalents across Canada, Australia and the Gulf. Your freight forwarder or customs broker will usually have a partner warehouse, because the paperwork — the bond, the entry type, the stock ledger — is routine for them and alien to everyone else.

The cash-flow case, with numbers

Take a realistic order: 3,000 lambswool sweaters, FOB value around USD 28,500, landing in Europe where duty and import charges on the customs value run to roughly USD 4,200 — the same stack we break down in our landed cost worked example. On a normal entry you pay that USD 4,200 the week the container clears, five months before the first sweater sells. In a bonded facility you pay it in slices as cartons are drawn out for delivery to stores or your fulfilment partner.

The arithmetic buyers care about: if the order lands in June and sells through to December, six months of duty and VAT deferral on USD 4,200 is not life-changing on its own — but the import VAT element, which in many European markets is the far larger number, is also deferred. On a VAT-inclusive entry that can mean deferring USD 8,000–10,000 of cash for half a year. For a small brand financing inventory on an overdraft, that is a real difference to how much they can buy.

When bonded storage pays for knitwear — and when it does not

We only recommend it in specific situations, because the storage itself is not free:

  • Seasonal stock arriving early to catch cheap freight. If you booked sea freight in the summer trough to save on the container, bonded storage preserves that saving instead of trading it back to the bank. Check the freight angle in the knitwear buying calendar.
  • Uncertain final destination. Distributors selling into several countries can hold goods at a central bonded hub and pay duty only in the market that actually takes each carton. This is the strongest use case for multi-market wholesalers.
  • Pending classification or origin questions. If your HS classification or duty rate is genuinely under review, bonded storage stops you paying a rate you may later reclaim.
  • High-duty fibres. Wool sweaters into high-tariff markets carry duty that can exceed total freight; deferral matters more when the deferred number is big.

When it fails: fast-turning replenishment stock that sells within four to six weeks of arrival. Weekly storage on cartons plus the withdrawal paperwork per release usually exceeds the financing saved. If your product moves that fast, a duty-paid entry and a good fulfilment and storage arrangement is simply cheaper.

The costs and constraints nobody mentions upfront

Bonded storage bills in three places. First, the storage rate itself — commonly a monthly charge per pallet or per CBM, at a premium over ordinary third-party warehousing because of the customs supervision. Second, handling: every inbound and outbound movement is a billable event, and part-carton releases are charged like full ones. Third, the entry work — your broker files the original warehouse entry and then a separate customs line each time stock is withdrawn. Some brokers charge a minimum per withdrawal, which punishes the drip-feed release pattern that makes bonded storage attractive in the first place. Ask for the withdrawal fee schedule before you commit.

The constraints matter too. You generally cannot process goods inside the bond — no re-pressing, no re-labelling beyond what the regime permits, no swapping hangtags when your buyer changes their mind. Knitwear that needs re-pressing or refurbishment has to leave the bond first. And if your goods stay past the storage time limit — five years in some regimes, less in others — customs can require disposal. Six months of seasonal holding is nowhere near the limit, but buyers should know the clock exists.

How to brief your factory and your broker

From our side of the table, bonded destinations change very little in production but a few things in documents. The commercial invoice and packing list must reconcile perfectly with the physical cartons, because the warehouse ledger is audited against them — mixed cartons or repacked assortments that survive a normal LCL delivery will create misery in a bonded facility. If you are releasing stock colour by colour, tell us at the pre-production meeting and we will pack solid by colour and size ratio, not mixed, so each withdrawal is clean. And if part of the order might be re-exported, we keep the origin documentation airtight from day one — traceability paperwork that satisfies one customs authority usually satisfies the next one too.

Frequently asked questions

Is import VAT also deferred in a bonded warehouse?

In most regimes yes — duty and import VAT are both triggered at withdrawal rather than entry, which is often the bigger cash-flow benefit since VAT is usually the larger number. Some markets run variations, such as duty-only deferral in certain warehouse classes, so confirm the exact treatment with your broker in the destination country before committing.

Can I inspect or QC goods while they are in bond?

You can usually inspect goods under supervision, and many facilities support audit-style checks, but you generally cannot open, rework, press or re-label retail units while in bond. If your QC process expects full access to every carton, plan the inspection before the goods enter the warehouse — for example a pre-shipment inspection at the factory, which we routinely arrange.

How long can knitwear stay in a bonded warehouse?

Regimes differ — five years is common in Europe and North America, but some procedures and markets are shorter, and the commercial storage rate rises over time in any case. For seasonal knitwear the practical horizon is under a year; if stock would sit longer than that, the problem is your buying plan, not the warehouse.
