Socks, Gloves and Mittens: Sourcing the Small Knit Accessories

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Small knit accessories are a different production problem from sweaters: much finer gauges, elastic yarns in every course, strict left/right or pair matching, and a unit price where packing and pairing cost more than the yarn. MOQs are set per colour and per size, packaging and barcode work is usually done at the factory, and the defects that generate returns are mismatched pairs, shade differences between two halves, and lost elasticity at the cuff.

Buyers often add gloves or socks to a sweater order as an afterthought — a small line on the same purchase order, expected to follow the same rules. We see this a lot, and it is where the surprises start. A sweater is one garment; a pair of gloves is two small garments that must match each other exactly, in shade, in size and in the direction of the thumb. Our guide to scarves and beanies covers the larger accessories; this one is about the small ones that come in pairs.

How the production differs

  • Gauge and machinery. Socks are normally made on fine-gauge circular sock machines, gloves and mittens on small flat-bed or specialised glove machines — often 7 to 13 gauge for gloves and far finer for hosiery. A sweater factory running 12-gauge flat machines does not automatically have either. Our gauge guide explains why the number changes hand feel and weight so much.
  • Elastic in every course. Cuffs, tops and sometimes the whole body rely on elastane or covered rubber yarn to stay up and recover. That makes stretch and recovery the number one performance test, not an afterthought.
  • Pairing. Two halves of a pair have to come from the same dye lot and the same knitting run. Production is planned in pairs, not pieces, and a single lost half turns a good unit into waste.
  • Low unit value, high handling. Yarn is a small share of cost; linking toes, closing fingertips, pairing, pressing, hang-card attachment and bagging are the expensive part. This is why the economics look nothing like a sweater margin calculation.

Decoration is worth deciding early: an embroidered logo or a knitted-in motif behaves differently on a small, stretched surface. Our embroidery guide and pattern structures cover the two routes. For novelty programmes, check the seasonal design constraints before committing to a complicated motif on a glove back.

MOQs, colours and the price conversation

Accessories are quoted per pair or per dozen, and minimums are driven by yarn dyeing and machine setup, not by garment weight. Expect a per-colour minimum — commonly 300 to 1,000 pairs depending on the yarn and whether the colour is stock or custom-dyed — and a separate minimum per size if the style has a real size range. Five colours across four sizes is twenty production cells, not one order, which is why a small accessory line can carry a higher total commitment than a sweater style. Our MOQ explainer covers the same logic at a larger scale, and yarn minimums explain the mill side of it.

Ask for the quote broken into yarn, knitting, linking/closing, finishing, pairing and packing. When a buyer negotiates only the unit price, the usual outcome is a lighter elastic content and a cuff that fails after three washes. Sample approval should include a wash-and-recovery test — see our sample review checklist.

The defects that come back

  • Mismatched pairs. Two different shades, two different sizes, or two left hands. Sorting at pairing is the only real control; a final AQL check on a random carton will not catch a systematic mismatch.
  • Lost cuff recovery. Cheap elastic, too little of it, or excessive heat in pressing. Check after washing, not only on the fresh sample.
  • Snags and ladders. Fine-gauge hosiery catches on rough carton interiors and jewellery easily — we cover the mechanism in snags, ladders and pulled threads.
  • Finger and toe seams. Bulky or twisted closing seams cause comfort complaints, and a thumb set too high makes a glove unwearable even when every measurement is within tolerance.
  • Packaging errors. A pair clipped to the wrong hang card, a barcode that will not scan, or a size sticker that disagrees with the product. Retail programmes need the same discipline as GTIN and barcode work on garments.

Children's versions add a compliance layer: small parts, cords, drawstrings and pull-test requirements all apply, and the rules differ by market. Read our children's knitwear safety guide alongside the general kids sourcing guide before placing a kids' accessory order.

Frequently asked questions

Can a sweater factory also make socks and gloves?

Sometimes through partner mills, but rarely on the same machines. Socks need fine-gauge circular hosiery machines and gloves need small flat-bed or glove machines, while sweaters are made on coarser flat knitting machines. Ask which factory will actually run the order and request samples from that line, not from the sweater sample room.

What MOQ should I expect for custom knit gloves or socks?

Plan per colour and per size, typically 300 to 1,000 pairs per colour depending on whether the yarn is a stock shade or custom-dyed. Five colours across four sizes is twenty production cells. Ask the supplier to show the yarn minimum behind the number — that is usually the real constraint.

How should pairs be packed and labelled for retail?

Specify the pair attachment method (clip, band or card), the hang card artwork, the polybag, the barcode position and the inner and outer carton quantities. Require a pairing and shade check before packing, and a scan test of the finished barcode. Mismatched pairs and unscannable labels are the two most common retail complaints on small accessories.
