Spirality in Knitwear: Why Sweaters Twist

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Spirality — also called torque or garment twist — is when a knitted panel rotates out of square after washing, so side seams swing forward and the hem rises on one side. It comes from the loop structure itself, driven mainly by yarn twist and the direction the yarn was spun, and it is exaggerated by finishing. It cannot be corrected by cutting; it has to be prevented in the yarn and the knit. The test is a washed square of fabric, measured for how far a marked line departs from square.

A sweater with spirality looks fine on the table and wrong on the body: the side seams drift toward the front, the hem sits unevenly, and the whole garment seems to be rotating slowly around the wearer. Buyers usually meet it as a complaint two washes into the season, because spirality often appears or worsens after the first domestic wash rather than at inspection.

What exactly is spirality?

Knitted fabric is built from loops, and each loop leans. In a single-jersey structure the loops all lean the same way, so the fabric has a natural tendency to rotate. When a panel is cut and joined, that rotation shows up as the panel going out of square.

The measurable consequence is that a horizontal line marked on the fabric no longer runs parallel to the course direction after relaxation. In a garment, this becomes visible as skewed side seams, a hem that dips on one side, and sleeves that hang with a slight rotation.

It is distinct from shrinkage, though the two interact. Shrinkage changes the dimensions; spirality changes the angles. A garment can shrink within tolerance and still fail on spirality, which is why a shrinkage test alone does not catch it. Our notes on controlling shrinkage cover the dimensional side.

What causes it?

Four factors, in rough order of influence.

  • Yarn twist. The single biggest lever. A tightly twisted yarn stores more rotational energy, which releases as the fabric relaxes. Our notes on ply and twist explain how twist is specified and why a balanced ply matters.
  • Twist direction and balance. A plied yarn twisted in the opposite direction to its singles is balanced and behaves symmetrically. An unbalanced yarn rotates. This is a spinner-level decision the buyer rarely sees but always feels.
  • Knit structure. Plain single jersey spirals most; rib structures spiral less because the alternating knit and purl loops counteract each other; interlock and double-knit structures are more stable still. Our notes on flat and circular knitting cover how structure and machine type interact.
  • Finishing. Washing, tumbling and steaming relax the fabric and release the stored rotation. A gentle finish can leave a garment looking square that will spiral after the customer's first wash — which is exactly why the test is done on a washed sample, not a finished one.

Which garments are most at risk?

Highest risk: fine-gauge single-jersey pieces in a tightly twisted yarn, especially long panels. A long knitted dress in plain jersey is the classic failure, because the rotation accumulates over the length. Our notes on knitted dresses cover the construction choices that reduce it.

Moderate risk: lightweight summer knits in cotton or viscose, where the yarn is often finely spun and tightly twisted, and where the garment is washed frequently. Our notes on spring/summer sourcing cover the yarn selection.

Lower risk: chunky gauges, ribbed constructions, and anything with a strong texture. The heavier yarn carries more stability, and rib structures counteract the rotation. This is one reason a cable knit is more forgiving than a plain fine-gauge knit of the same fibre.

Across all of them, the risk rises with the length of the panel and falls with the structural stability of the knit.

How is spirality tested?

The standard method is straightforward and does not need a specialised lab for a rough check.

Prepare a square panel — typically around 50cm by 50cm — from the production fabric, and mark it with a square reference, either by marking lines or by using a square template with a marked course line.

Wash and dry it according to the care instructions the garment will carry, then relax it flat.

Measure the displacement of the marked line from the perpendicular, expressed either as a distance in centimetres or as a percentage of the panel width. Most specifications state a maximum percentage, and the figure varies by product type and by the standard used.

The important discipline is that the test is done after the wash, because spirality is a relaxation phenomenon. A test on unfinished fabric understates it. Our notes on lab testing explain where this test sits alongside shrinkage, pilling and colour fastness.

How is it prevented?

  • Specify a balanced plied yarn. The single most effective control. Ask the spinner explicitly whether the yarn is balanced for the structure it will be knitted in.
  • Choose the structure deliberately. Where a design allows, a rib or a half-cardigan structure resists spirality far better than plain jersey. For long panels, consider a structure change or a seam placement that breaks the run.
  • Reduce twist for fine-gauge plain knits. A slightly softer twist in a fine-gauge jersey costs some crispness in the stitch and buys stability.
  • Control the finishing. A relaxed, well-controlled wash and dry sets the fabric nearer its final state before the garment ships, so less movement is left for the customer's first wash.
  • Test the actual yarn and structure, not a substitute. Spirality is a property of the combination, and a result from a similar fabric is not transferable.
  • Write a maximum into the spec, with the test method and the wash conditions named. Our notes on quality standards documents cover where it belongs.

What should a buyer do if it appears?

First, establish whether it is spirality or a cutting fault. A panel cut off-grain also produces skewed seams, but the cause is the cutting room rather than the yarn. Our notes on fabric defects cover how to tell the difference between a structural fault and a making-up fault.

Second, test a washed sample from the actual bulk. If a washed bulk panel spirals beyond the agreed limit, the lot is affected, and no amount of pressing will fix it permanently.

Third, establish the remedy. Re-knitting with a balanced yarn or a different structure is the real fix; accepting the lot at a discount is the commercial one, and it needs to be a decision rather than a drift. Our notes on handling complaints cover how to document that.

Frequently asked questions

Can spirality be fixed after the garment is made?

No, not permanently. Pressing and steaming can make a garment look square temporarily, but the stored rotation in the yarn remains and returns after washing. The genuine fix is at the yarn and structure level — a balanced ply, a more stable knit structure, or a softer twist — which is why spirality has to be designed out rather than corrected downstream.

Why does spirality appear after washing rather than at inspection?

Because it is a relaxation phenomenon. The fabric holds its rotation until the fibres and loops are relaxed by water and heat, which is what a wash does. Inspection on finished, pressed goods can look entirely square while the same panel spirals after the customer's first domestic wash, which is why the test is specified on a washed sample.

Is spirality worse in cheap knitwear?

Not necessarily — it correlates with yarn twist and structure rather than with price. A tightly twisted fine-gauge jersey can spiral at any price point. What price does affect is the likelihood that the yarn was specified with balance in mind, since a spinner given a cost target may supply a higher-twist yarn that knits cheaply and spirals later.
