Selling Knitwear to Canada: Bilingual Labelling and a Winter Market

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

Canada is not simply a smaller United States. It has its own labelling regime requiring English and French, its own import rules, and a longer, colder winter that makes genuine warmth a purchasing criterion rather than a marketing angle. Sizing leans slightly larger than US and the market is concentrated in a few urban centres, with provincial differences that matter for logistics.

Brands often treat Canada as a satellite of their US programme: same styles, same sizing, same labels, shipped north. That works up to a point, and then it fails — usually on a label that lacks French, or on a garment that a Montreal customer finds too thin for the season they actually experience. Canada is a relatively small market with a distinct set of requirements, and the suppliers who understand those run programmes there for years.

What is different about Canadian labelling?

Canada has federal textile labelling requirements, and the practical consequence for knitwear is straightforward: information must generally appear in both English and French. That applies to fibre content, country of origin and care instructions, and it means a label or hangtag designed for the US market is usually not usable in Canada as it stands.

Points worth confirming before ordering labels:

  • Bilingual content for required information, with French given equal prominence rather than as a footnote.
  • Fibre content by percentage, expressed by the generic fibre names recognised in Canada rather than whichever term the yarn supplier uses. Our overview of care labelling rules covers how composition and care information is structured in general.
  • Care instructions in both languages, in a clear sequence.
  • Identity of the responsible party — the Canadian importer or the supplier, depending on the arrangement. This is often the detail that gets overlooked when a US label is adapted.

The cost of getting this wrong is not just a fine: a shipment that cannot legally be sold has to be re-labelled, and re-labelling finished knitwear is slow and awkward. Confirm the label layout with the buyer before labels are printed, and again before bulk production.

How does the winter shape product decisions?

Canada's cold season is longer and colder than most of the US market, and that changes what sells:

  • Genuine warmth sells. Chunky gauges, high necks and heavyweight wool blends are core rather than niche. The warmth conversation in our notes on how knitwear keeps you warm is relevant to the buying decision here, not just to the marketing.
  • Layering pieces matter. Fine-gauge knits worn under coats extend the wearable range and sell alongside the heavier styles.
  • Accessories are a real category. Scarves, beanies, mittens and gloves are functional purchases with steady demand — see our notes on knit accessories for how they sit in a range.
  • Transitional demand is compressed. Spring and autumn windows are shorter than in milder markets, so transitional ranges need to be timed tightly — relevant to our notes on transitional knitwear.

How does sizing and fit differ from the US?

Canadian sizing generally runs slightly larger than US sizing, and consumers often expect a roomier fit than the equivalent US size would give — partly a layering habit, and partly because sizing conventions have drifted. Carrying a US size chart straight into Canada tends to result in more returns at the larger end.

The practical approach is to confirm the size chart with the Canadian buyer specifically, then take the same disciplined route as any market: agree target measurements, decide tolerances, and test fit on a body before grading — see our guide to sizing and grading. It is also worth remembering that Canada's population is concentrated in a handful of cities, so a small number of fit decisions affect the whole market.

What import and logistics points should be noted?

Canada is a separate customs jurisdiction from the US, which catches out brands that assume a shared North American programme. Goods need their own import documentation, and duty treatment and origin rules are set by Canadian rules rather than American ones. Our overview of HS codes and duties explains how classification works and why it should be confirmed rather than assumed.

On logistics, the concentration of demand in Toronto, Vancouver and Montreal means distribution is manageable, but it also means winter weather can disrupt inland transport in a way that a US delivery plan may not anticipate. Building a little slack into winter delivery windows is cheaper than expediting in January. Our notes on Incoterms cover where responsibility sits for freight and clearance in these arrangements.

How should a supplier approach a Canadian account?

  • Ask about the label layout first — before sampling, since French content affects artwork, label size and sometimes the garment's internal finishing.
  • Confirm the size chart against the buyer's own rather than reusing a US chart.
  • Take warmth seriously in the specification — a weight that reads as substantial in a US catalogue can read as thin for a Canadian winter, and buyers notice.
  • Expect a smaller but focused order. Canadian volumes are modest by US standards, but programmes tend to be stable and repeat in a way that suits reliable suppliers — the same dynamic as in our notes on planning capacity around smaller accounts.
  • Be accurate about compliance documentation, including composition and origin, since the buyer carries the responsibility for what is on the shelf. Partner factories certified to BSCI, SEDEX or Oeko-Tex and open to audits give buyers the evidence they need; at YZR Sweaters we also support full inspection and third-party inspection through firms such as SGS and BV.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use US labels for knitwear sold in Canada?

Usually not as they stand. Canadian requirements generally call for required information — fibre content, origin and care — to appear in both English and French, which a US-only label will not cover. Adapt the label design for Canada and confirm the layout with the buyer before printing, because correcting labels after production is slow and expensive.

Does Canadian sizing really differ from US sizing?

In practice, yes — Canadian sizing tends to run slightly larger, and customers often expect a roomier fit, partly because of layering habits. Reusing a US size chart usually produces more returns at the larger end. Confirm the buyer's own measurements rather than assuming the two markets share a chart.

Is Canada treated as part of the US for customs purposes?

No. Canada is a separate customs jurisdiction with its own import requirements, duty treatment and origin rules. A shipment bound for Canada needs its own documentation and classification, and it is worth confirming the duty position for your specific product and origin rather than assuming it mirrors the US.
