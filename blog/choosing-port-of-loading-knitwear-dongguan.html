Choosing a Port of Loading for Knitwear from Dongguan: Yantian vs Shekou vs Nansha

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-11

Quick answer

Dongguan sits between the Shenzhen and Guangzhou port groups. Yantian (Shenzhen) is the default for deep-sea US and Europe sailings with the most direct services; Shekou and Chiwan are closer to much of the western delta and handy for LCL and feeder cargo; Nansha (Guangzhou) can win on trucking from the west. Name the exact terminal in the Incoterm — "FOB Shenzhen" leaves two very different gateways open, and the inland trucking, cut-off times and sailing options are yours to argue about later.

We see this a lot with buyers who write "FOB Shenzhen" on a purchase order and assume the port is a detail the factory will sort out. It is not a detail. Shenzhen has more than one container terminal, they are a real trucking distance apart from a Dongguan factory, and they do not offer the same direct sailings or the same cut-off times. Here is how we talk buyers through the choice, from the factory side of the gate.

Why the port belongs in the Incoterm

An Incoterm should name a place precisely: "FOB Yantian, Incoterms 2020", not "FOB China" and preferably not even "FOB Shenzhen". The reason is that the seller's cost and responsibility end at that named point, and every kilometre of inland trucking before it is priced into the goods. Shenzhen and Ningbo are real money apart on trucking, as our Incoterms guide notes, but Yantian and Shekou are also different enough to matter on a full container.

Dongguan sits between Guangzhou and Shenzhen, so most factories in the delta can reach two port groups. Which one is right depends on where the factory actually is, which lane you are shipping, and who nominates the forwarder. Our manufacturing hubs guide gives the geography; this is the shipping decision that follows from it.

Yantian: the deep-sea default

Yantian is Shenzhen's deep-water container terminal on the eastern side and the one most buyers end up using for transpacific and Europe cargo. It has the deepest draft, the largest mainline vessels and the widest choice of direct services, which is what you want when a winter programme has a fixed arrival window. The trade-offs are a longer truck from some western Dongguan towns and serious congestion in the August to October peak, when rolled containers and blank sailings are most common.

For a full 40HQ of knitwear with a fixed US or EU delivery date, Yantian is usually the first quote we ask for. The direct sailing matters more than a small trucking saving: fewer transshipment points means fewer places for a container to miss a connection.

Shekou and Chiwan: closer for much of Dongguan

Shekou and Chiwan sit on the western side of the Pearl River estuary. For factories in the western and central delta they can be a shorter, cheaper truck, and they are well set up for LCL consolidation and intra-Asia and feeder traffic. If your order is small enough to share a container, or your destination is served via a hub, this is often the practical answer.

The catch is that some long-haul lanes rely on a feeder leg to a hub port rather than a direct mainline call. That is not a problem in itself, but it is a transshipment decision with its own schedule risk, and it should be visible on the quote. Ask the forwarder to state plainly whether the routing is direct or via a hub, and which port the mother vessel actually sails from.

Nansha: when Guangzhou wins on trucking

Nansha is Guangzhou's deep-water terminal, on the western side of the delta. From factories in the west and south of Dongguan it can beat both Shenzhen terminals on inland trucking, and it has grown its deep-sea services substantially. Where it still loses on some lanes is frequency: fewer direct sailings to certain destinations can mean waiting for the next vessel or accepting a transshipment.

We treat Nansha as a quote to compare, not an automatic choice. On a full container the trucking difference is real but modest next to the ocean rate; on a tight schedule, an extra three days waiting for a sailing costs more than the trucking saves. Ask for both and compare on total door-to-door time, not on the trucking line alone.

Ningbo and Shanghai: when the buyer's consolidator decides

Sometimes the port is not a factory choice at all. A buyer-nominated forwarder may run its consolidation hub in Ningbo or Shanghai, or a retailer's vendor routing guide may name the gateway. In that case the goods truck north — or move by rail for certain Europe lanes — and the buyer carries the inland leg. That is a legitimate setup, but it needs to be priced honestly into the landed cost rather than assumed away as "the factory handles shipping".

One practical warning: a longer inland leg also means an earlier gate-in and documentation cut-off relative to your production finish date. Buyers who book a Ningbo sailing without adjusting the production lead time routinely miss the vessel by a day.

Cut-offs, documents and the last mile

Every port works to several cut-offs, not one: the shipping instruction or documentation cut-off, the VGM (verified gross mass) cut-off, the customs declaration deadline and the physical gate-in cut-off. Miss any one and the container rolls to the next sailing regardless of how empty the vessel is. Our container loading supervision note covers the VGM and physical side, and the shipping document set covers the paperwork that has to be in before the documentation cut-off.

The factory's job is to have the cartons, the packing list and the marks ready early enough to meet all of them. The buyer's job is to name the port precisely and to let the forwarder confirm the cut-offs for the chosen sailing. When those two things happen, a port choice is uneventful — which is exactly what it should be.

A practical way to choose

  • Full container, US or Europe, fixed date: start with Yantian for the direct services.
  • LCL or intra-Asia, western delta factory: ask for Shekou or Chiwan and confirm the feeder routing.
  • Western delta factory, flexible timing: compare Nansha on total transit, not trucking alone.
  • Buyer-nominated forwarder: accept their hub, but price the inland leg and pull the production finish date forward.
  • Always: name the terminal in the Incoterm and get the cut-off dates in writing before production starts.

Frequently asked questions

Is FOB Yantian always the cheapest option from Dongguan?

Not always. Yantian usually wins on direct sailings to the US and Europe, but a factory in the western delta may have a shorter truck to Shekou/Chiwan or Nansha, and an LCL shipment may consolidate more cheaply there. Compare the all-in door-to-door cost and transit time for your specific lane, not the ocean rate alone. On a full container a small trucking saving is often worth less than a direct sailing.

Can I name Ningbo or Shanghai even if the factory is in Dongguan?

Yes, and it is common when a buyer-nominated forwarder runs a consolidation hub there or a retailer routing guide names the gateway. The cost is a longer inland leg, which belongs in the landed cost, and earlier cut-offs relative to your production finish date. Confirm who books and pays the inland trucking, and pull the production completion date forward so the container does not miss the vessel.

Does the port of loading actually affect my lead time?

Yes, in two ways: the frequency and directness of sailings on your lane, and the cut-off dates that determine how early goods must be ready. A port with a weekly direct service gives a different buffer from one needing a feeder connection. Ask the forwarder for the documentation, VGM and gate-in cut-offs for the specific sailing, then work backwards into the production schedule.
