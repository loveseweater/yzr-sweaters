Yarn Minimums and Colour Choice: Why Small Orders Have Fewer Options

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

Yarn carries its own minimum order, separate from the garment minimum, because dyeing is done in batches — a dyehouse will not run a machine for a few kilos. On a small knitwear order this is usually the real constraint: the garment quantity may be achievable, but the colour has to come from a yarn that already exists in stock or the minimum dyeing quantity has to be bought.

A buyer asks for 300 sweaters in a specific burgundy and is told the colour needs a minimum of far more yarn than the order will use. The confusion is understandable, because the buyer is thinking about garments and the factory is thinking about dyeing. Once the yarn minimum is understood as a separate threshold, the options become predictable and manageable rather than an obstacle that appears mid-negotiation.

Why does yarn have its own minimum?

Because dyeing happens in batches, and a dyehouse operates on an industrial scale. Yarn arrives at the dyer undyed or in a base shade, is loaded into a dye vessel, and is processed as a lot. The vessel has a practical minimum load — running it partially full wastes water, energy, chemicals and machine time — and every lot carries setup work: recipe preparation, lab verification, and quality checks afterwards.

The result is a floor below which dyeing a colour does not make economic sense for anyone in the chain. That floor is set by the yarn count and the dyehouse, and it is expressed in weight of yarn rather than in garments, which is precisely why it feels unrelated to the order quantity the buyer is discussing.

A related factor: the yarn itself may have a spinning minimum from the mill, separate from the dyeing minimum. A specialist count or a fine-fibre yarn that is not held in stock can carry a higher threshold than a common one. Our notes on choosing a yarn supplier cover what else to ask about beyond price.

How does the yarn minimum translate into garment quantities?

Through consumption. The minimum applies to weight of yarn, and each garment uses a certain amount of it, so the number of garments the minimum supports depends on the weight of the sweater. A heavy chunky knit consumes several times the yarn of a fine-gauge top, which means the same yarn minimum supports far fewer heavy garments.

This produces a counter-intuitive outcome worth understanding: a chunky sweater in a bespoke colour can hit the yarn minimum with a relatively small garment order, while a fine-gauge top in the same bespoke colour may need a much larger run to justify the dyeing. Our guide to yarn consumption explains how the per-garment figure is estimated.

What are the buyer's realistic options?

Four paths, in rough order of how often they are the right answer.

  • Choose a colour that exists as stock yarn. The most effective answer for small orders, and the reason a supplier's colour range matters. Stock yarn means no dyeing minimum at all, only the constraint that the colour must already be one the mill holds — and stock colours change seasonally, so the options available now may not be the same next season.
  • Accept the minimum and carry the yarn. Buy the minimum dye quantity even though the order uses less, keeping the balance for a reorder. This only makes sense if a repeat is genuinely likely; otherwise the buyer owns yarn they may never use.
  • Consolidate colourways. Reducing four colours to two often brings each colour's yarn volume above the minimum, at the cost of a narrower range. Our notes on range planning cover how that trade-off fits the wider assortment.
  • Change yarn or fibre. A different count, a stock blend, or a mill with a lower threshold can remove the constraint — at the cost of changing the hand feel the design was built around. Our notes on yarn substitution explain why this is a formal decision rather than a swap.

How do you plan around it before it becomes a problem?

  • Ask about yarn availability at the colour-selection stage, not after the design is finalised. A supplier can usually say immediately whether a colour is stock or needs dyeing, and what the threshold is.
  • Design the palette around stock colours for small runs. Keep bespoke colours for styles where the volume justifies them, and use stock colours for the pieces that are testing the market. This is the single most useful habit for a new brand — see our notes on low MOQ manufacturing for how it fits the wider picture.
  • Plan the colourway count with the yarn minimum in mind. Each colour is its own yarn purchase, so four colours on a small order means four separate minimums rather than one divided by four.
  • Ask about the mill's stock list before choosing, and ask again next season, since stock ranges change.
  • Keep the yarn lot record. If a colour was dyed for you, note the lot — a reorder in the same colour from a later lot may not match. Our notes on yarn stock planning cover how lot continuity affects repeat orders.

What should you expect a supplier to tell you?

A supplier who works with small brands will raise the yarn question before it becomes an issue: which colours are stock, which need dyeing, what the minimum is in kilos and roughly what that means in garments, and what the lead time difference is. Presenting a colour range without noting which options are feasible at the order quantity is either careless or evasive, and it tends to surface later as a price increase or a delay.

Frequently asked questions

Is the yarn minimum the same as the garment MOQ?

No, they are separate thresholds. The garment minimum is about what the factory can economically knit; the yarn minimum is about what the dyehouse can economically dye. A small order can clear the garment minimum easily and still fail the yarn minimum, which is why the colour is often the real constraint on a small run rather than the quantity itself.

Can I buy less yarn than the minimum and pay a surcharge?

Sometimes, but it is not simply a matter of paying more — many dyehouses will not run a vessel below a certain load at any price, because the practical limit is the equipment rather than the economics. Where a surcharge is possible the supplier will say so; where it is not, no amount of negotiation changes it.

Do stock yarn colours change over time?

Yes. Mill stock ranges are refreshed seasonally, so a colour available this season may be discontinued or replaced next. That is a reason to note which colours your range depends on and to confirm availability before each season rather than assuming the stock list is stable — and a reason to plan reorders with the same lot in mind.
