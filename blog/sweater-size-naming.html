Sweater Size Naming: Alpha Sizing vs Numbered Sizes

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Alpha sizing means XS to XXL; numbered sizing means 8, 10, 12. The choice is not cosmetic — it decides how many sizes you produce, how the grading is built, what the retailer can reorder and how the customer self-selects. Knitwear often suits alpha sizing because the fabric stretches and the fit tolerances are wider, but a market with a strong numbered-size convention will resist it. The buyer has to decide this before the size set is sampled, because changing the naming means re-grading.

Size naming looks like a decision for the packaging designer and is really a decision about production. Whether a sweater comes in six alpha sizes or five numbered ones changes the number of graded patterns, the size set the factory samples, the ratio the buyer orders and the way returns are reported. Buyers who treat it as a label detail usually discover the consequence two seasons later, when they want to add a size and find the whole grade needs rebuilding.

What is the difference between alpha and numbered sizing?

Alpha sizing names sizes by letter: XS, S, M, L, XL, and increasingly XXS and XXL. Numbered sizing names them by a figure: in womenswear typically 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, or in the UK convention 8, 10, 12, 14, 16. Menswear more often uses chest measurements or a letter range.

The important difference is precision. A numbered system implies a body measurement standard behind each number, with a defined increment. An alpha system is a range: "M" covers a span of bodies, and the garment is designed to fit that span acceptably rather than one precise measurement.

That is why alpha sizing suits knitwear particularly well — a knitted garment with natural stretch accommodates a range of bodies more gracefully than a woven shirt. Our notes on sizing and grading cover how the underlying chart is built.

Which markets expect which system?

  • US womenswear has a strong numbered convention in department and specialty retail, though DTC brands increasingly use alpha. Both appear.
  • UK womenswear is firmly numbered, and a UK 12 is not a US 8 on paper. Our notes on the UK market cover the sizing consequences.
  • EU markets use numbered sizing with their own conventions, often based on body measurements in centimetres, differing again from UK and US.
  • Germany and the Nordics expect precise numbered sizing and are less forgiving of a vague alpha range — our notes on the German market cover why.
  • Japan uses numbered sizing with its own body standard, and the fit expectation is narrower — our notes on the Japanese market explain.
  • Menswear across most markets accepts alpha sizing more readily, often with a chest measurement alongside.

What does the choice change in production?

The number of graded patterns. A six-size alpha range needs six graded patterns; a five-size numbered range needs five. Each pattern is a separate sample and a separate set of measurements, which is why adding a size to an existing range is more than a labelling change.

The size set sample. Factories sample a size set — usually the base size plus the extremes — to prove the grade. The set is defined by your naming, so a change of system means a new set. Our notes on sample types explain where the size set fits in the sampling sequence.

The order ratio. Alpha sizing tends to sell in a different distribution from numbered sizing, because customers self-select differently. Our notes on size ratio planning cover how to build the ratio from sales data rather than assumption.

The retail conversation. A stockist with a numbered-size fixture will struggle to merchandise an alpha range consistently, and vice versa. Our notes on supplying independent boutiques cover how that plays out at the smaller-retailer end.

What is dual sizing, and should you use it?

Dual labelling prints both systems on the label — "M / UK 12 / US 8" — so the customer can find their size whichever convention they know. It is common in export-focused ranges and in brands selling across markets from one stock pool.

The advantage is obvious: one SKU serves several markets, and a customer accustomed to numbering is not alienated by an alpha label. The risk is that dual labels invite precision the garment may not have. If the "M" is designed as a range and the "UK 12" implies a specific body, a customer who is a UK 14 but comfortably fits the M will read the label as telling them it does not fit.

The workable approach is to dual-label where the underlying chart genuinely supports both, and to state the fit intent plainly alongside — the subject of our notes on online size guides. Dual labelling is not a substitute for a clear fit statement.

How should a buyer decide?

  • Start from the channel. A department store with numbered fixtures, a DTC site, and a boutique with mixed rails each imply a different answer. The naming should fit how the garment will be sold.
  • Decide before the size set is sampled. Changing the naming after sampling means re-grading, resampling and a new fit approval. Our notes on fit sessions and pattern blocks explain why a block change cascades.
  • Check whether a size at the end of the range is genuinely viable. Adding XXL to an alpha range is not just a label — the grade has to hold and the armhole has to work at that size, as our notes on plus-size sourcing cover.
  • Confirm what the factory will sample. A size set of three is standard; a full run of six may not be, and knowing which sizes get a physical sample affects how confident you can be at the ends of the range.
  • Keep the naming stable across seasons. A range that switches from numbered to alpha breaks reorder history and confuses returning customers, and the system belongs in the size spec rather than only on the label.

What are the common mistakes?

Relabelling a chart from another system. Taking a US numbered chart and printing alpha letters on it produces an "M" that fits like a US 8, which is not what an alpha-M customer expects. Our notes on sizing and grading explain why the chart has to be built rather than converted.

Adding sizes without re-grading. Extending a range by scaling the existing pattern proportionally produces a larger garment with the same armhole and neck, which is the failure our notes on sleeve construction describe.

Assuming alpha sizing is more inclusive. It is not — it is a different way of covering the same bodies. An alpha range stopping at XL is no more inclusive than a numbered range stopping at the equivalent size.

Letting the label contradict the chart. A sewn-in "L" that measures like the chart's M is a returns engine. Our notes on care and labelling cover why label content has to come from the approved chart.

Frequently asked questions

Is alpha sizing better for knitwear than numbered sizing?

It suits knitwear well because the fabric stretches, so an alpha size can genuinely cover the range of bodies it claims. But better depends on the channel: a market or retailer with a strong numbered convention will resist alpha labelling regardless of how well the garment accommodates it. The channel usually decides.

Can I use one size system and sell in several markets?

You can, most easily with dual labelling that prints both systems on the label. The risk is implying precision the garment does not have — a "UK 12" beside an alpha M suggests a specific body measurement, and a customer who is a UK 14 but fits the M may read the label as telling them it will not fit. Dual labelling needs a clear fit statement alongside it.

What happens if I change the size naming later?

It is a production change, not a label change. A different naming system means a different number of graded patterns, a new size set to sample and a new fit approval, because the measurements behind each size change. Changing it after sampling means re-grading and resampling, which is why the decision belongs before the first size set is made.
