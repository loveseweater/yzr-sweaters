The Pre-Production Meeting: Preventing Knitwear Problems Before They Happen

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Views: 0

Quick answer

A pre-production meeting brings the buyer and the factory together — usually by video for offshore orders — to walk through the approved sample, the specification and the production plan before the machines start. It catches the gaps that sampling leaves open: yarn availability, measurement tolerance questions, trim delivery, packing instructions and the inspection plan. An hour spent here routinely saves a batch.

Most bulk production failures are not mysteries. They are the predictable consequence of something that was never explicitly agreed: a measurement that was assumed rather than confirmed, a trim that had not arrived, a colourway count the buyer thought was four and the factory thought was three. Sampling resolves what the garment should look like; it does not resolve whether the production of it has been planned properly. That is what the pre-production meeting is for.

What is a pre-production meeting?

A pre-production meeting — commonly shortened to PP meeting — is a structured review held after the sample is approved and before bulk production begins. Its purpose is to confirm that both parties understand the same order in the same way, and to surface anything that would otherwise be discovered mid-production, when correcting it is expensive.

For offshore sourcing the meeting is normally a video call, often with the factory's merchandiser, production manager and quality staff present. Buyers occasionally treat it as optional. In practice, the orders that run smoothest are almost always the ones where this conversation happened.

What should be covered?

The agenda is short and specific. Each item is on the list because it has caused a problem for someone before.

  • The approved sample. Confirm which physical sample is the reference — the sealed one, with both sides holding an identical copy — and walk through any known deviations from specification that were accepted at approval. This is where the counter sample discussion belongs if the buyer ordered one.
  • Measurements and tolerances. Restate the point-of-measure list, confirm tolerances and the measurement method (relaxed, on a flat surface), and highlight the two or three points that matter most for fit. Ambiguity here is the single most common source of bulk rejection — see our guide to shrinkage control for why measurement method has to be agreed, not assumed.
  • Yarn and colour. Confirm the yarn is ordered and in the factory, whether it is the same dye lot as the sample, and that the colour has been approved. If any yarn is still to arrive, agree the production sequence around it. Our notes on yarn stock planning explain why lot continuity matters more than buyers expect.
  • Trims and labels. Confirm what has arrived, what is coming, and the date each is needed on the line. Label content should be checked against the market's requirements — our guide to care labelling rules covers what must appear.
  • Production plan and sequence. Which sizes and colourways run first, expected daily output, and the completion date. If the buyer needs a partial shipment or a particular colourway prioritised, this is when to say so.
  • Quality plan. What will be checked, at which stages, against what standard, and whether a third-party inspection is booked. See our guide to AQL sampling for how inspection levels and acceptance numbers are set.
  • Packing and shipping. Folding method, polybag, size ratio per carton, carton markings, and the shipping deadline. Packing instructions given after production often mean repacking.
  • Escalation contact. Who the factory calls if something goes wrong, and how quickly. A named contact on both sides shortens every subsequent problem.

Why does this meeting prevent so many problems?

Because it exposes assumptions. A specification answers what to make; a production plan answers how it will actually be made, in which order, with which materials arriving when. Those are different documents, and the gaps between them are where orders fail.

The failure patterns this meeting prevents are consistent:

  • Yarn arrives late, production waits. Discovered now, the delivery date can be adjusted or the sequence changed. Discovered in week three, the buyer loses a launch window.
  • Trims missing at the packing stage. Hangtags and labels are the classic delay, because they are ordered late and are always assumed to be trivial.
  • A tolerance nobody agreed. The bulk measures differently to the sample, and the argument starts after the goods exist.
  • Packing done the factory's way. Then repacked, usually at the buyer's cost, because the buyer's requirement was never stated.
  • Inspection scheduled too late. Third-party inspectors book up, and a missed inspection date can delay shipment by a week.

How should a buyer prepare and run it?

  • Circulate the agenda and documents beforehand. The meeting is a confirmation exercise, not a reading exercise — sending the tech pack and specification in advance lets the factory arrive with answers rather than questions.
  • Ask the factory to present their plan, rather than reading yours at them. What they say reveals what they understood.
  • Walk through the sample physically on camera if video is used: hold it up, point at the details that matter, and mark anything that must not change.
  • Get the outcome in writing within a day. A short recap email listing decisions, dates and open items is more useful than a recording, because it becomes the document both sides work from.
  • Note the open items with owners and dates. A meeting where three things remain unresolved but assigned is successful; one where everything is "agreed" but nothing is scheduled is not. Our notes on sourcing communication cover how to keep that record clean.

How does this connect to the rest of the order?

The pre-production meeting sits between sampling and bulk, and it is the point where the development work becomes a production commitment. It is also the natural moment to confirm anything that has drifted since sampling: a change of yarn supplier, a revised colourway count, a shifted delivery date. Buyers who use it as a genuine review rather than a formality find that the rest of the order needs far less supervision.

Frequently asked questions

Is a pre-production meeting necessary for a repeat order?

It is shorter, but still worth holding. Repeat orders carry their own risks: a new yarn lot, a changed spec, a different trim supplier, or a shift in the delivery window. A ten-minute confirmation that nothing has changed is cheap compared with discovering a change mid-production.

Who should attend from the buyer's side?

Whoever owns the specification and whoever owns the delivery date — often the same person at a small brand. If the buyer's marketing or design team has firm opinions about details that must not change, they should either attend or have their requirements written into the document circulated beforehand.

What if the factory resists holding one?

Treat it as information about how the order will be run. A supplier who cannot spare an hour before production is unlikely to communicate well when a problem appears during it. On larger orders, making a documented pre-production review a condition of the purchase order is a reasonable position for a buyer to take.
