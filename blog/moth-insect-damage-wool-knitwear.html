Moth and Insect Damage in Wool Knitwear: What Buyers Can Actually Control

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

Insect damage in wool comes from the larvae of clothes moths and carpet beetles, which digest keratin — the protein wool is made of. It almost never starts on the knitting machine; it starts somewhere dark, warm and undisturbed, which usually means a warehouse, a stock room or a wardrobe. The controls are storage conditions, an insect-resist treatment applied during dyeing where the market allows it, and clear agreement on when risk passes from supplier to buyer.

A hole in a sweater is one of the few faults that customers recognise instantly and trust you less for afterwards, because it reads as neglect rather than as a manufacturing problem. The awkward part is that most of these holes appear long after the goods left the factory, which is why the argument about who is responsible tends to be worse than the damage itself.

What the damage actually looks like

There are two culprits and they leave slightly different evidence.

  • Clothes moth larvae leave irregular holes with silk webbing or a small tunnel-like case, often surrounded by surface grazing where the larva has cropped the fibre rather than gone clean through.
  • Carpet beetle larvae leave clean-edged holes and shed bristly skins as they grow. They tend to work along folds and seams.

Both are frequently misdiagnosed as abrasion or snagging. The practical test is the surrounding area: insect damage usually appears with grazing, webbing or frass — fine granular waste — nearby, and it often clusters where the garment was folded rather than where it would rub. Our notes on fabric defects cover how to separate a structural fault from damage that occurred later.

An important detail for anyone writing a specification: the adults do no damage. It is the larvae that eat. Anything that only kills adults is not a solution.

Where it starts — usually not where you think

Keratin is not attractive to insects because it is wool; it is attractive because almost nothing else will eat it, which is why larvae thrive wherever protein is left undisturbed. A garment carrying traces of body oils, food spill or skin flakes in a dark cupboard is a complete habitat.

In a commercial chain, three places account for most cases we see:

  • Long-term storage. Cartons that sit for months in a warm warehouse, especially pallets stored against a wall in a corner. This is the same risk pattern as mould and mildew — both follow heat, humidity and stagnation.
  • Retail stock rooms. Garments held on hangers or in returns areas with limited turnover. Returns are the worst case, because a worn garment carries exactly the residue larvae need.
  • Transit. A container crossing a warm climate can hold heat and humidity for weeks. Our notes on fulfilment and storage cover the warehouse side of the same problem.

The factory's part of this is genuinely limited, which is why it is worth being honest about it rather than promising something nobody can deliver.

What a supplier can genuinely do

  • Clean finishing. Garments should leave finishing free of loose fibre, soil and residue. It does not make wool inedible, but it removes the food source that larvae depend on to establish themselves.
  • Individual bagging. A sealed polybag around each garment is ordinary rather than exotic, and it is effective — it stops an insect arriving from outside and establishing before the customer ever opens the bag.
  • Insect-resist treatment. Where the destination market and the buyer's chemical requirements allow it, wool can be given an insect-resist finish applied during dyeing. This is a chemical decision: ask your mill which agent it uses and request the treatment record, because many brand RSL lists restrict finishes and an undocumented one is a compliance problem even when it works. Our notes on restricted substances testing and the EU chemical rules explain how that check is documented.
  • Dry, sealed cartons with desiccant, particularly for sea freight through warm climates.

Note that cedar, lavender and similar products are repellents rather than treatments. They discourage but do not reliably protect a stored garment, and we would not want a buyer relying on them as a specification.

What the buyer controls

Realistically, most prevention sits with whoever holds the stock. That is not a supplier trying to avoid responsibility — it is simply where leverage is.

  • Stock rotation. Undisturbed storage is the ingredient everything else depends on. Garments that move get inspected by accident; garments that sit for two seasons do not.
  • Cool, dry, ventilated storage. Warm and humid accelerates the whole life cycle.
  • Monitoring. Pheromone traps tell you whether adult moths are present, which is how you find out early rather than by opening cartons in autumn. They are a monitoring tool, not a cure, and a trap being full is information rather than reassurance.
  • Handling returns. Anything that has been worn should be cleaned before it goes back into saleable stock. Our notes on returns handling cover the commercial side.
  • Care instructions that customers follow. Storage advice on the care label and product page genuinely reduces returns that arrive looking like faults.

Specifying and testing an insect-resist finish

Where a treatment is required, three things belong in writing: the agent used, its compliance with the buyer's restricted substances list, and the test evidence.

There are standard laboratory methods for assessing insect resistance of wool textiles, run by exposing treated fabric to a controlled larvae population and measuring feeding damage and mortality. We would rather a buyer ask us which method was used and request the report than accept a verbal claim that the yarn is "mothproofed". Our notes on lab testing explain how to read those reports alongside fastness and pilling results.

Blends complicate it. A wool-rich blend often includes a synthetic that the treatment does not cover in the same way, so the protection available on a 100% wool yarn is not automatically available on a 70/30. Worth confirming per quality rather than assuming across a range.

Who carries the loss

This is where most of the cost actually sits. Damage found on arrival is a supplier discussion. Damage found after months in a warehouse is very hard to attribute, and no amount of arguing recovers the season.

The workable approach is to agree the handover point in the same breath as the packing specification: individually bagged, sealed cartons, inspection window for reporting defects, and photographic evidence required for any claim. Our notes on handling complaints cover how to document a claim so that it is settled rather than disputed.

Frequently asked questions

Is a wool blend safer than pure wool?

Partly. Larvae digest keratin, so a high synthetic content gives them less food and the damage progresses more slowly, but blends containing wool will still support them. A wool-acrylic blend at 30% wool is at risk; a garment sitting undisturbed in a warm room is at risk regardless of blend. Blend reduces appetite, not interest.

Does freezing or heating kill moth larvae?

Both can, if done properly. A sustained deep freeze is the consumer-friendly option — what matters is that the whole load actually reaches temperature and stays there long enough, which is why a single garment in a full chest freezer behaves differently from one in a dedicated unit. Industrial heat treatment exists for infested stock but is not something to improvise on garment stock. Neither replaces clean storage.

Are pheromone traps enough to protect stock?

No. They tell you moths are present so you can act before you find holes, which makes them valuable — but they catch adults and do nothing about the larvae doing the damage. Use them as an early warning alongside rotation, cleaning and climate control rather than as protection on their own.

Will a supplier replace insect-damaged garments found after delivery?

Seldom, unless the damage demonstrably existed at handover. Most such claims fail on evidence rather than goodwill, because the window in which the supplier could be responsible closed months earlier. That is why the practical answer is prevention plus a clear inspection window at delivery, agreed before the order rather than after the discovery.
