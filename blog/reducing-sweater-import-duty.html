Reducing Duty on Sweater Imports: Classification, Origin and Legal Savings

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-09

Quick answer

Duty on sweater imports is decided by three things: the HS code the goods are classified under, the country of origin, and any trade agreement or relief scheme that applies. Legitimate savings come from getting all three right and documenting them properly — not from describing goods inaccurately. Misclassification is a compliance risk with penalties attached, and customs authorities audit retrospectively.

Duty is a line on the landed cost sheet that buyers often accept as fixed, and it is worth more attention than it usually gets. On a sweater programme the difference between a correct classification and a careless one can be several percentage points of the invoice value, which is real money across a season. The discipline is that the savings have to be legitimate and documented, because the alternative is not a clever workaround, it is a penalty.

How is a sweater classified for customs?

Knitted garments sit in Chapter 61 of the Harmonized System. Within it, the subheading depends on the garment type and the fibre composition: the system separates pullovers, cardigans and similar articles from other knitted garments, then splits again by fibre — wool or fine animal hair, cotton, man-made fibres and others. Our notes on HS codes and duties walk through the chapter structure.

Two classification points decide the rate more often than buyers expect.

  • Fibre content decides the subheading. A sweater that is 55 percent cotton and 45 percent polyester classifies by the predominant fibre, not by the fibre with the highest value or the one in the name. Getting the percentages right on the invoice and the composition label is what makes the code defensible — and a mismatch between the label, the test report and the declaration turns a routine entry into an audit.
  • Garment construction decides whether it is a sweater at all. A fine-gauge knitted top that opens all the way down may classify as a cardigan, a shirt or something else depending on its features, and the difference is a different duty rate. For a genuinely ambiguous style, a binding ruling is worth the effort.

Fix the classification at the development stage, not at the port. A buyer who knows the code before the style is signed off can adjust fibre content or construction while it still costs nothing.

How does origin affect duty — and why does it not move easily?

Origin is a separate question from where the goods were shipped from, and it is the point most often misunderstood. Origin follows where the goods were wholly obtained or underwent substantial transformation, judged against the destination country's rules. For a sweater knitted in China from Chinese yarn, origin is simply China.

What origin is not is a shipping choice. A Chinese-origin sweater does not become ASEAN-origin by transiting through a port in Vietnam, and it does not gain preferential treatment under an EU or UK agreement by being warehoused in Europe. Moving goods to change their declared origin without genuine processing is origin fraud, not planning, and customs authorities investigate it directly.

Legitimate origin decisions do exist. If a sweater is genuinely knitted and linked in a country covered by a trade agreement with the destination, it may qualify for preferential duty, provided the rule of origin is met and the paperwork to prove it exists. Assessing that is a job for the buyer's customs adviser together with the factory.

Which reliefs and agreements actually reduce duty?

Several legitimate mechanisms exist, and which ones apply depends on your market and your supply chain.

  • Free trade agreements and preferential origin. Where a preferential agreement covers the heading, qualifying goods from a partner country can enter at a reduced or zero rate. Qualification requires meeting the specific rule of origin — usually a change in tariff classification or a minimum regional value content — and providing a valid origin declaration. Without the documentation the preference cannot be claimed, and an incorrectly claimed preference can be recovered with penalties years later.
  • Duty drawback. If imported goods are subsequently exported, some jurisdictions allow the duty paid on import to be refunded. Most relevant to exporters who import garments and re-export them, rather than to a brand selling domestically.
  • Inward processing relief. Similar in logic to drawback: duty is relieved on goods imported for processing and re-export.
  • Preferential tariff quotas. Some products have a reduced rate available within a limited volume. These are allocated and not always available, but a buyer with a large programme should check whether a quota exists for their heading.
  • De minimis and low-value thresholds. Relevant to small direct-to-consumer shipments rather than container imports, and increasingly restricted. Commercial consignments should plan on standard treatment.

Where a buyer has genuine choice in the supply chain, the decision is made at the sourcing stage: which country the goods are made in, and under what manufacturing route. A different origin means a different factory, not a different document.

What documentation makes a duty position defensible?

The savings are only as good as the file behind them. A defensible position usually contains:

  • Correct commercial invoice and packing list, with fibre composition stated on the invoice and matching the label and the declaration. Our notes on packaging and shipping cover how the commercial documents are prepared at the factory.
  • Fibre content test reports where composition drives the classification, from a recognised lab and current for the batch.
  • Origin documentation, as the scheme requires — origin declaration, certificate of origin, or supplier's declaration.
  • Classification reasoning, ideally a binding ruling for any style whose classification is arguable. A ruling taken before the first shipment removes the argument for every subsequent one.
  • Production records showing where and how the goods were made, which is what an origin verification actually examines — the same evidence base our notes on supply chain traceability describe.
  • Consistency across the file. The most common reason a preference fails is not a wrong answer but an inconsistent one — a label, an invoice, a test report and a declaration that do not all say the same thing.

What should buyers avoid?

Describing goods inaccurately to obtain a lower rate. This is misdeclaration, it carries penalties, and customs authorities audit retrospectively — often years later, when the buyer's records and the factory's relationship have both moved on.

Assuming a shipment's origin from its port of loading. A container loaded in one country says nothing about where the goods were made, and the origin declaration is what governs.

Treating a preference as automatic. A free trade agreement creates an opportunity, not an entitlement. The rule of origin has to be met and evidenced, and if the yarn or the knitting does not satisfy it, the preference does not apply no matter how the goods are invoiced.

Ignoring the interaction with trade measures. Additional duties and trade measures apply to certain origins and products on top of the base rate, and they change. Our notes on UFLPA compliance cover the documentation obligations that sit alongside the duty question.

Frequently asked questions

Does the country I ship from affect the duty I pay?

No. Duty is determined by the origin of the goods, meaning where they were produced, not by the port of loading or the country they were shipped through. A sweater made in China is Chinese-origin for customs purposes even if it is consolidated and shipped from a different country.

Can I lower duty by changing the fibre content of a sweater?

Fibre composition does drive the subheading, so it affects the rate — but the composition on the declaration has to match the garment, the label and the test report. Changing the actual composition is a legitimate design decision; describing it differently from what it is would be misdeclaration.

Is it worth getting a binding customs ruling?

For any style where the classification is genuinely arguable, yes. A ruling settled before the first shipment applies to subsequent shipments of the same goods, removes the risk of a retrospective reclassification, and costs far less than the duty and penalties a dispute would involve.
