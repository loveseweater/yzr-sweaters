Knitwear Colour Cards: Building a Season Palette

By YZR Sweaters · 2026-10

Quick answer

A knitwear colour card is a physical set of yarn swatches in the shades you intend to use, grouped so a buyer can see how they relate. It exists because a Pantone reference describes a colour but not how that colour behaves in a particular yarn. Building one before the season — from yarn the mill actually holds, grouped into core and seasonal, with carry-over stated — shortens approval and removes most of the surprise at dyeing.

Buyers usually work from a colour reference rather than a physical yarn card, which is reasonable until the first lab dip comes back and the mustard looks like ochre. A colour card built from the actual yarn is the document that prevents that, and it takes an afternoon per season once the habit exists.

What is a colour card, and how does it differ from a Pantone reference?

A Pantone reference names a colour target. A colour card shows that colour as it appears in a specific yarn, at a specific count, knitted or wound.

The difference matters because yarn changes colour. A shade that reads warm on a paper chip can read cooler or duller in a wool with a natural base tone, and differently again in a cotton. Our notes on hand feel and micron explain why fibre character varies; the same variation applies to how the fibre takes and reflects colour.

So the colour card is not a replacement for a Pantone reference — you still need the target — but a record of what that target actually looks like in the yarn you will use. Our notes on working to Pantone cover why the reference alone is not enough.

What belongs on a colour card?

  • A yarn swatch per shade, wound or knitted, big enough to judge. A knitted swatch is better because it shows the colour as the customer will see it.
  • The shade reference and the yarn it is in. The same Pantone becomes a different colour in a different yarn, so the card has to say which yarn the swatch represents.
  • Whether the shade is stock or dyed to order. This single field determines the minimum, the lead time and the reorder risk, and our notes on yarn minimums explain why it matters so much.
  • The season it belongs to, and whether it is core or seasonal. Core shades carry over and earn depth; seasonal shades are tested and earn breadth.
  • The previous season's shade it replaces, if it does. This is what prevents a range from drifting so far each season that customers cannot match a new purchase to an old one.
  • A date and a version. A colour card without a version cannot settle the question of which shades were current when an order was placed.

How should shades be grouped?

Grouping is the part that turns a set of swatches into a usable document. Three groupings do the work.

By role: core neutrals, supporting shades, and seasonal accents. A core neutral is one you expect to sell across seasons and reorder; a seasonal accent is one you test. Our notes on range planning cover how those roles map onto the assortment.

By family: the reds, the greens, the neutrals, so a buyer can see at a glance whether the palette has enough continuity or has fragmented into unrelated colours.

By position in the ladder: light to dark within a family, which is how a customer actually shops. Our notes on size and range structure touch on how the assortment as a whole is organised.

A palette that groups cleanly usually merchandises cleanly. One that does not is usually a palette assembled shade by shade rather than designed as a set.

Why does the stock-versus-dyed distinction matter so much?

Because it dictates almost every commercial property of that colour. A stock shade has no dyeing minimum, shorter lead time, and a reorder that will match. A dyed-to-order shade has a minimum measured in yarn weight, a dyeing lead time, and a reorder that may step in shade.

Putting that field on the colour card forces the decision at palette-building time rather than at order time, which is exactly when it is cheap. Our notes on booking capacity explain why the colour decision is a cut-off date in its own right, and colour consistency covers what happens on a reorder when the shade is not held.

How does a colour card shorten approval?

Three ways, all of them time saved at the point where time is scarcest.

It removes the first rejection. A lab dip rejected because the shade looks different in yarn is a wasted round, and rounds cost weeks. A card built from the yarn means the target was already judged in the right material.

It lets the mill prepare. A supplier who knows the season's palette in advance can check stock, flag a shade that needs dyeing, and warn about a minimum before the order arrives.

It makes approvals repeatable. With a swatch and a reference photographed together, judging a bulk lot becomes a comparison against a known object rather than a memory. Our notes on lab dips and colour approval explain the judgement process and why light source matters.

How should a buyer build and maintain one?

  • Start from the mill's held range. Build the core of the palette from shades the yarn supplier actually stocks, and reserve dyed-to-order shades for the pieces that justify the minimum.
  • Request swatches in the yarn you will use, not a generic card. A swatch in a different count or blend is a different colour.
  • Photograph each swatch in daylight with its reference beside it, so the card has a digital twin that can be emailed.
  • Carry over deliberately. Decide which shades persist, which retire and which are replaced, rather than starting the palette from zero each season.
  • Keep the previous cards. They are the reference for reorders and the record of what a customer already owns.
  • Review at the end of the season against what actually sold, and adjust the core/seasonal split accordingly. Our notes on dead stock explain the cost of getting that split wrong.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a physical colour card if I have Pantone references?

Yes, because a Pantone reference names a target while a swatch shows how that target appears in a specific yarn. The same shade reads differently in a wool with a natural base tone than in a cotton, and differently again across counts. The colour card records the target as it actually looks in the material you will produce.

How many shades should a knitwear palette have?

Enough to cover the range's roles without fragmenting: typically a small group of core neutrals that carry over, a handful of supporting shades, and a few seasonal accents to test. The practical constraint is that each shade is a yarn purchase with its own minimum if it is not stocked, so a wide palette on a small order usually means most shades cannot be dyed to order.

What is the most useful field to record on a colour card?

Whether the shade is held in stock or dyed to order. That one field determines the minimum quantity, the lead time and whether a reorder will match — which are the three things that go wrong when a colour is chosen without knowing its status.
